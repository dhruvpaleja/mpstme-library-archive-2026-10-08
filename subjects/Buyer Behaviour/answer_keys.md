Explaining the three points of McClelland’s acquired needs theory with
application- 6 marks for just the theory & 4 for application
Achievement
The need for achievement means you’re motivated by completing tasks you set out
for yourself or that someone else sets out for you. Achievement-motivated people
often seek out situations and projects that highlight their skills and are neither
too simple nor too complex. Tasks that don’t present any challenge at all won’t
garner the recognition they desire while tasks that are outside of their comfort
zone pose too much of a risk.
Affiliation
The need for affiliation means you’re motivated by your connections with others.
Affiliation motivates interpersonal relationships and emotional connections.
Often, they prefer working in groups rather than working independently in order
to build those relationships. Affiliation driven individuals tend to avoid situations
in which they may face rejection or uncomfortable boundaries within their
relationships. The case mentions their friends and peers learning online too.
Power
The need for power means you’re motivated by authority and control. People
motivated by power seek positions and relationships in which they can
demonstrate their leadership and be the primary decision-maker. Many people
who are power-motivated enjoy competition and debate. Unlike those motivated
by achievement and affiliation, those motivated by power do not avoid high-risk
situations. Instead, they seek them out to show their superiority.

TechWave is aware that a significant portion of its potential customers may not
be deeply engaged or motivated to evaluate the smartphone's technical

and visually appealing device. TechWave's approach aligns with the
characteristics of the peripheral route:
Focus 1. on Surface-Level Appeal: TechWave focuses on creating a visually
appealing and reliable smartphone. The marketing and product design prioritize
aesthetics and user-friendly features. These aspects serve as peripheral cues to
attract and persuade potential customers.
Minimal 2. Cognitive Processing: Casual consumers, who do not want to delve
into technical intricacies, are likely to rely on peripheral cues and surface-level
attributes to make their decisions. TechWave's emphasis on the smartphone's
design, user interface, and overall visual appeal caters to this lower level of
cognitive processing.
Peripheral 3. Elements: The use of an attractive spokesperson, a catchy slogan,
limited-time offers, and customer testimonials are all peripheral cues designed to
capture the interest and trust of consumers.
These elements are often more effective in peripheral route persuasion.
Emotional 4. and Aesthetic Appeal: The focus on creating an emotionally
appealing experience and an attractive design reinforces the use of the peripheral
route. These elements aim to evoke positive emotions and aesthetics as persuasive
factors.
While the smartphone may still have strong technical specifications, the
marketing strategy and the emphasis on aesthetics, visual appeal, and user-
friendliness align with the peripheral route to persuasion. TechWave understands
that, for these casual consumers, the decision to purchase the "Voyager X" is
influenced more by surface-level attributes and emotional appeal than in-depth
technical details.

Proposal Solicitation
During the next stage of the process, qualified suppliers are invited to submit
proposals. Depending on the nature of the purchase, some suppliers send only a
catalog or a sales representative.
Supplier Selection
At this stage, the buyer screens the proposals and makes a choice. A significant part
of this selection involves evaluating the vendors under consideration. The selection
process involves thorough review of the proposals submitted, as well as consideration
of vendor capabilities, reputation, customer references, warranties, and so on.
Order-Routine Specification
The buyer now writes the final order with the chosen supplier, listing the technical
specifications, the quantity needed, the warranty, and so on. At this stage, the supplier
typically works closely with the buyer to manage inventories and deliver on agreement

In this final stage, the buyer reviews the supplier’s performance and provides

1. Absolute Threshold
Explanation: This is the minimum level of stimulus intensity needed for it to be
detected by the senses. In marketing, brands need to ensure their stimuli (e.g., scent,
sound, visuals) are strong enough to be noticeable by consumers.
Example: The faint scent of fresh coffee at Café Coffee Day outlets is just strong
enough for customers to detect, enhancing their sensory experience and drawing
them in.
2. Awareness Threshold
Explanation: This is the point at which a consumer becomes consciously aware of a
stimulus. For marketing, it’s crucial that consumers not only detect but also
consciously notice the brand message.
Example: On social media, a distinctive campaign by brands like Zomato (e.g., their
humorous posts and notifications) grabs attention, making consumers aware of their
promotions in a highly competitive space.
3. Differential Threshold (Just Noticeable Difference - JND)
Explanation: This is the smallest detectable difference between two stimuli.
Marketers often use the JND when making minor changes to products or packaging,
ensuring these changes are either noticeable or subtle enough based on the intended
effect.
Example: When Amul reduces the weight of its butter slightly due to cost pressures,
it’s done subtly to stay within the JND so consumers don’t perceive a drastic change.
4. Subliminal Perception

awareness but may still influence behavior. Though controversial and regulated,
some brands attempt subtle messaging to influence attitudes or feelings without
overtly drawing attention.
