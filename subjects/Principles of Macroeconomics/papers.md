Sweden is often cited as a global leader in the transition to a cashless society. The use of cash
has fallen to record lows, with many businesses, from retail shops to churches, refusing to
accept physical currency. This shift was not primarily driven by a top-down government
mandate but was a gradual, consumer-led revolution, fueled by convenience and security. The
widespread adoption of mobile payment apps like Swish, which allows instant, real-time
transfers using a phone number, has made daily transactions seamless. Furthermore, a series of
high-profile bank robberies and a general societal trust in technology have made digital
payments the preferred and often only option.
However, this rapid decline of cash has raised significant social and economic challenges.
Concerns have emerged about financial exclusion for vulnerable populations, particularly the
elderly, immigrants, and those with disabilities, who may not have access to or proficiency
with digital technology. There are also issues of cyber security, with a rise in digital fraud and
scams targeting individuals. The central bank of Sweden, the Riksbank, has been forced to
consider the societal implications of this trend and is exploring the creation of a Central Bank
Digital Currency (CBDC), the "e-krona," to ensure that the public continues to have access to a
state-backed form of money in a cashless future.
Question: Based on the case study, what factors led to the widespread adoption of digital
money in Sweden, and what is one major societal challenge that has emerged from this trend?
Evaluate the argument that a Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) like the "e-krona" is a
necessary response to Sweden's move towards a cashless society.

India has undergone a massive transformation in its payments landscape, largely driven by the
Unified Payments Interface (UPI). Introduced in 2016, UPI is an instant, real-time payment
system that allows users to transfer money between bank accounts using a simple mobile app.
The system's success is rooted in its open architecture, which allows a wide range of private
companies and banks to build their own user-friendly apps on top of the public infrastructure.
This has led to a remarkable surge in small-ticket digital transactions, from buying a cup of tea
to paying a street vendor, which were previously dominated by cash. The system has been
instrumental in extending financial inclusion to millions of underbanked individuals across the
country. This shift has had a profound impact on the Indian economy. The velocity of money
has increased significantly as transactions become instantaneous and frictionless. For
policymakers, it provides a wealth of real-time data on consumption patterns, which can be
invaluable for monetary policy decisions and fiscal planning. However, this reliance on a
single, public digital infrastructure also creates new vulnerabilities, such as the risk of system-
wide technical failures or cyber-attacks. The push for a cashless society, while promoting
transparency and reducing transaction costs, also raises concerns about privacy and financial
exclusion for those without access to smartphones or the internet.
Questions: Based on the case study, what technological and policy factors contributed to the
