A. Keynesian Criticisms of the Classical Theory of Employment and Income Determination   12
(8 marks)
John Maynard Keynes challenged the core tenets of classical economics, which held that a
market economy would naturally tend towards full employment. His major criticisms include:
1. Rejection of Say's Law: The classical theory was based on Say's Law, which stated that
"supply creates its own demand." It implied that all income earned would be spent, either
on consumption or investment, preventing a general glut of goods and services. Keynes
argued that a portion of income could be saved and not invested, leading to a deficiency in
aggregate demand and a state of underemployment equilibrium.
2. Possibility of Involuntary Unemployment: Classical theory assumed that any
unemployment was "voluntary," caused by workers' unwillingness to accept a lower real
wage. Keynes argued that an economy could reach equilibrium at a level of income that is
below the full employment level, resulting in involuntary unemployment. He pointed out
that wages are "sticky" and do not fall easily in a recession, preventing the market from
clearing.
3. Interest Rate and Its Role: The classical view held that the interest rate was the flexible
price that equated savings and investment. Keynes countered that while investment is
indeed influenced by the interest rate, it is more heavily dependent on the "marginal
efficiency of capital" (business expectations of future profitability). He also argued that
savings are primarily a function of income, not the interest rate. This disconnect means
that interest rates may not be able to restore a balance between savings and investment.
4. Money is Not Neutral: Classical theory viewed money as a neutral medium of exchange
that did not affect real variables like output and employment. Keynes's theory of "liquidity
preference" posited that money also serves as a store of value. People may choose to hold
money in a liquid form rather than investing it, which can reduce aggregate demand and
lead to a fall in income and employment.

Savings-Investment Approach to Determination of Equilibrium Income (4 marks)
The Savings-Investment approach is an alternative to the Aggregate Demand (AD) and
Aggregate Supply (AS) approach for determining equilibrium income. It states that
equilibrium income is achieved when planned savings (S) equal planned investment (I).
S > I: When planned savings exceed planned investment, it means that households are
withdrawing more from the income stream (in the form of savings) than firms are injecting
(in the form of investment). This leads to an unplanned build-up of inventories. To clear
these excess stocks, firms will cut production, leading to a fall in income and employment
until savings and investment become equal.
S < I: When planned savings are less than planned investment, it means that firms are
injecting more into the income stream than households are withdrawing. This leads to an
unplanned decrease in inventories. To replenish their stocks, firms will increase
production, which leads to a rise in income and employment until the equilibrium
condition (S = I) is met.

A.   The Concept of Marginal Efficiency of Capital (MEC) – 3 marks
Page 6 of 10
--- page 7 ---
The MEC is the expected rate of return on a new investment project. It is defined as the
discount rate that equates the present value of the expected future income stream from a
capital asset to its supply price (or cost). It is a measure of the profitability of an investment.
For a given capital good, a firm calculates the MEC by estimating the revenue it will generate
over its lifetime and then finding the rate that makes the present value of this income stream
equal to its cost. The MEC is crucial in Keynesian theory because it is based on subjective
and often volatile business expectations.
Numerical Example- 3 marks
Consider a machine that costs ₹10,000. It is expected to generate a revenue of ₹6,000 in the
first year and ₹6,000 in the second year, after which it will have zero value. The MEC (r) is
the rate that satisfies the equation:
₹10,000 = ₹6,000/(1+r) + ₹6,000/(1+r)^2
Solving for 'r' gives a value of approximately 13.1%. This means the expected rate of
return is 13.1%. If the market interest rate is less than 13.1%, the firm will find the
investment profitable and will undertake the project.
Impact of Change in Business Expectations- 3 marks
A change in business expectations directly affects the marginal efficiency of capital.
Increased Optimism: When businesses become optimistic about the future, they
expect higher returns from their investments. This raises the MEC for all potential
projects, causing the entire investment demand curve to shift to the right. At a given
interest rate, this leads to an increase in aggregate investment.
Impact on Economy: The increase in investment is a component of aggregate demand.
This rise in demand, through the multiplier effect, leads to a significant increase in
equilibrium national income (output) and a corresponding increase in employment.
Increased Pessimism: The reverse occurs. Heightened pessimism reduces expected
returns, causing the MEC to fall and the investment demand curve to shift left. This
leads to a decrease in investment, aggregate demand, output, and employment,
highlighting Keynes's view that unstable expectations can lead to economic instability
and business cycles.

Detailed explanation- 6 marks
In a three-sector economy, the circular flow of income includes households,
businesses, and the government. The government plays a crucial role in regulating economic
activity through taxation and public spending, which directly impacts the flow of income
between sectors.
1. Government Taxation and Leakage
The government collects taxes from households and businesses in the
form of income tax, corporate tax, and indirect taxes. This taxation acts as a leakage from the
circular flow since it reduces the disposable income of households and the profits of
businesses, leading to lower consumption and investment.
2. Government Spending and Injection
The government reinjects income into the economy by spending on public goods and services
such as infrastructure, education, healthcare,
and welfare programs. This spending acts as an injection into the circular flow by increasing
demand for goods and services, generating employment, and supporting business activities.
3. Balancing the Circular Flow
Page 1 of 12
--- page 2 ---
If government spending exceeds taxation (budget deficit), it increases total demand and
stimulates economic growth. If taxation exceeds government spending (budget surplus), it
slows down the economy, reducing demand and employment levels.
Conclusion
Government taxation and spending play a dual role in the circular flow of income. While
taxation reduces income available to households and businesses, public spending injects
money back into the economy, ensuring economic stability and growth. The extent of
government intervention determines the overall equilibrium in the three-sector circular flow.

Features of Business Cycles
1. Periodic Occurrence: Business cycles happen regularly but not with exact timing. They
consist of phases like expansion, peak, contraction, depression, and trough, with durations
ranging from two to twelve years.
2. Synchronicity: Business cycles affect all industries and sectors simultaneously. For
example, a recession spreads through various industries, and similarly, prosperity impacts the
entire economy through interconnected sectors.
3. Fluctuations in Multiple Variables: Business cycles affect production, employment,
investment, consumption, interest rates, and price levels all at once.
4. Impact on Durable Goods Consumption: Investment and the consumption of durable goods
(e.g., cars, houses) are most affected by business cycles due to fluctuating profit expectations
and the deferrable nature of such purchases.
5. Stability in Non-Durable Goods Consumption: Consumption of non-durable goods (e.g.,
food, clothing) remains relatively stable throughout different phases of the cycle.
6. Impact on Inventories: During a depression, inventories accumulate beyond the desired
level, leading to production cuts. In recovery, low inventories prompt businesses to increase
production and investment in capital goods.
7. Fluctuating Profits: Profits fluctuate more than other incomes, with businesses facing high
uncertainty. During depression, profits may even turn negative, causing bankruptcies.
8. International Impact: Business cycles spread internationally through trade. For example, a
recession in the USA can reduce demand for imports, causing economic downturns in other
countries as well. The Great Depression of the 1930s is an example of this global impact.

A. Elaborate on the Keynesian theory of investment. – 8 marks   12
The Keynesian theory of investment is based on the idea that investment is driven by a
comparison between the expected rate of return on new capital and the cost of borrowing.
The Marginal Efficiency of Capital (MEC): The expected rate of return is the MEC. It is
calculated as the rate that equates the discounted present value of the expected future
returns from a capital asset to its current cost. The MEC is based on future expectations,
which are highly uncertain and subject to changes in business confidence, or "animal
spirits."
Page 5 of 8
--- page 6 ---
The Rate of Interest: The cost of investment is the market rate of interest. It is the cost of
borrowing funds to finance the investment.
Investment Decision Rule: A rational firm will undertake an investment project if the
MEC is greater than the rate of interest (MEC > r).
The Investment Demand Function: 4 marks
The investment demand function shows the relationship between the level of aggregate
investment and the interest rate, holding expectations (MEC) constant. It can be expressed as
I = f(r, MEC).
Downward-Sloping Curve: As the interest rate falls, more investment projects become
profitable, leading to an increase in the total amount of investment. This inverse
relationship gives the investment demand curve a downward slope.
Shifts in the Curve: Changes in business expectations (the MEC) cause a shift in the
entire investment demand curve. An increase in optimism about future profits will raise the
MEC for all projects, shifting the curve to the right and leading to more investment at
every interest rate. Conversely, an increase in pessimism will shift the curve to the left.
