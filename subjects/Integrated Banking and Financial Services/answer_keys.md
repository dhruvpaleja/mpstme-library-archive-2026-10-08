5.   12
Financial Sector Reforms after 1991
After the 1991 economic crisis, India introduced major financial sector reforms under the
LPG policy based on Narasimham Committee recommendations. The objective was to make
the financial system more efficient, competitive, and globally integrated.
Banking Sector Reforms
Under the guidance of the Reserve Bank of India, reforms included reduction in CRR and
SLR, introduction of prudential norms (IRAC), capital adequacy norms (Basel), and entry of
private sector banks.
Result: Improved efficiency, competition, and financial discipline, though NPAs remained a
challenge.
Capital Market Reforms
The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) was strengthened in 1992. Electronic
trading, dematerialization, and better disclosure norms were introduced.
Result: Greater transparency, investor protection, and modernization of stock markets.
Insurance Sector Reforms
Page 5 of 6
--- page 6 ---
Insurance sector was opened to private players and regulated by Insurance Regulatory and
Development Authority of India (IRDAI).
Result: Increased competition and better insurance penetration.
Overall Evaluation
Financial sector reforms after 1991 made the system market-oriented, technologically
advanced, and globally competitive. However, issues like NPAs and governance concerns
require continuous reforms.

SEBI Guidelines for Post-Issue Management

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) has prescribed detailed guidelines to
ensure smooth post-issue management of public issues and to protect investors’ interests. The
key provisions are:
Allotment of Securities
The allotment process must be fair, transparent, and in accordance with the basis of
allotment approved by the designated stock exchange.
In case of oversubscription, proportionate allotment rules must be followed.
Refund of Application Money
Refunds must be made promptly to unsuccessful applicants.
Delay in refund attracts interest liability as per SEBI regulations.
Credit of Shares
Shares must be credited to investors’ Demat accounts within the prescribed time.
Physical share certificates (if any) must be dispatched within the stipulated period.
Listing of Securities
The company must complete listing formalities and obtain permission from stock
exchanges such as the Bombay Stock Exchange and National Stock Exchange before
trading commences.
Listing should be completed within the time limit specified by SEBI.
Post-Issue Monitoring Reports
The lead merchant banker must submit post-issue monitoring reports to SEBI to ensure
compliance with regulations.
Advertisement and Disclosure
The issuer company must publish post-issue advertisements containing details of
subscription, allotment, and listing.

SCORES Platform
SEBI operates an online complaint redressal system called SCORES (SEBI Complaints

Investors can file complaints online against listed companies, intermediaries, or market
participants.
Complaints are forwarded to the concerned entity for resolution within a specified time.
Time-Bound Resolution
Companies and intermediaries must respond within the prescribed time frame.
SEBI monitors the resolution process and ensures accountability.
Arbitration Mechanism
If disputes remain unresolved, investors can approach stock exchange arbitration
mechanisms for settlement.
Inspection and Enforcement Action

4.   12
Meaning of Factoring
Factoring is a short-term financing arrangement in which a business sells its accounts
receivables (book debts) to a financial institution called a factor at a discount to obtain
immediate cash.
Process of Implementing Factoring as a Financing Option
If I were implementing factoring for a company, the process would involve the following
steps:
Selection of Factor
The company selects a factoring company or bank offering suitable terms (advance rate,
commission, services).
Agreement Signing
A factoring agreement is signed specifying:
Advance percentage (usually 70–90%)
Factoring charges/commission
Recourse or non-recourse terms
Sale of Goods on Credit
The company sells goods to customers and raises invoices.
Assignment of Receivables
The receivables (invoices) are assigned to the factor. Customers are informed to make
payment directly to the factor.
Advance Payment
The factor immediately pays a percentage of invoice value (e.g., 80%) to the company.
Collection from Debtors
The factor collects payment from customers on due date.
Final Settlement
After deducting factoring charges and interest, the balance amount is remitted to the company.
Example
Suppose ABC Ltd. sells goods worth ₹10,00,000 on 60 days credit.
The factor agrees to advance 80%.
ABC Ltd. receives ₹8,00,000 immediately.
After 60 days, the factor collects ₹10,00,000 from customers.
If factoring charges are ₹50,000, the factor remits ₹1,50,000 (₹2,00,000 balance – ₹50,000
charges).
Thus, ABC Ltd. improves liquidity without waiting 60 days.
Key Benefits of Factoring
Page 4 of 6
--- page 5 ---
Improved Cash Flow – Immediate funds against receivables.
Better Working Capital Management – Reduces collection period.
Outsourced Collection Function – Saves administrative cost and effort.
Credit Risk Protection (Non-recourse factoring) – Factor bears risk of bad debts.
No Additional Collateral Required – Financing based on receivables.
Drawbacks of Factoring
High Cost – Factoring commission and interest can be expensive.
Impact on Customer Relations – Customers may feel pressured by third-party collection.
Dependence on Factor – Over-reliance may weaken internal credit control.
Not Suitable for All Businesses – Works best for firms with large credit sales.
5.   12
Financial Stability
Financial Stability refers to a condition in which the financial system — including banks,
financial institutions, markets, and payment systems — functions smoothly and efficiently
without disruptions. A stable financial system can withstand economic shocks and continue to
support economic growth.
In India, financial stability is primarily monitored by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI).
Risks Associated with Financial Stability
Credit Risk
Risk of borrowers defaulting on loans, leading to rising Non-Performing Assets (NPAs).
Market Risk
Losses due to fluctuations in interest rates, exchange rates, or stock prices.
Liquidity Risk
Inability of financial institutions to meet short-term obligations.
Operational Risk
Loss due to system failures, frauds, cyberattacks, or mismanagement.
Systemic Risk
Failure of one major financial institution affecting the entire financial system.
Global/External Risk
International financial crises, capital outflows, or global recession affecting domestic markets.
Early Warning Signs
Rapid increase in NPAs
Excessive credit growth
Asset price bubbles (real estate or stock market)
High inflation or currency volatility
Falling capital adequacy ratios
Sudden liquidity crunch in banks
