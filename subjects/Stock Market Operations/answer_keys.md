A. FPO (Follow-on Public Offering) refers to the process in which a publicly listed company   8
issues additional shares to the public after its Initial Public Offering (IPO). An FPO allows the
company to raise more capital from the market by offering new shares or selling existing shares
owned by current shareholders. (2 mark)
When Do Companies Choose to Go for an FPO?
Companies may opt for an FPO for several reasons: (1Mark for each point = 6 Marks)
1. Raising Additional Capital:
Similar to an IPO, an FPO helps a company raise capital for further expansion, debt reduction, or
funding specific projects. This is often done when the company needs more funds than what was
initially raised through the IPO.
2. Reducing Debt:
Companies with high levels of debt may issue an FPO to pay off loans or reduce their debt
burden. This improves the financial health of the company and reduces interest expenses.
3. Improving Liquidity:
By issuing more shares, the company can increase the number of shares available for trading in
the market, thereby improving liquidity. Higher liquidity makes it easier for investors to buy and
sell shares.

--- page 2 ---
4. Meeting Regulatory Requirements:
In some cases, regulatory authorities may require a company to increase its public shareholding.
For instance, a company listed on stock exchanges may need to maintain a minimum percentage
of shares in public hands. An FPO can help meet this requirement.
5. Diluting Promoter Holding:
If a company’s promoters (founders or early investors) hold a large percentage of shares, they
may issue an FPO to reduce their ownership stake and diversify the shareholder base.
6. Fund Specific Acquisitions:
A company planning an acquisition or expansion may use an FPO to raise the funds needed for
that transaction. This ensures that the company can execute its growth strategy without stretching
its finances.

B. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) plays a crucial role in regulating and   8
overseeing the functioning of the primary market in India. Its primary objective is to protect the
interests of investors, ensure transparency, and maintain fairness in the issuance of securities.
SEBI has implemented several rules and guidelines that companies and market participants must
follow when raising capital through the primary market. (8 marks for explanation)
Here are the key roles of SEBI in the primary market:
1. Regulating the Issue of Securities
SEBI establishes guidelines for companies issuing securities in the primary market, ensuring
fair practices. This includes regulating Initial Public Offerings (IPOs), Follow-on Public
Offerings (FPOs), Rights Issues, and Debt Instruments.
It ensures that companies follow the disclosure norms, providing accurate and comprehensive
information about their financials, business risks, and intended use of the funds.
2. Approval of Offer Documents
Companies wishing to issue shares to the public must submit an offer document (like the
Draft Red Herring Prospectus - DRHP) to SEBI for review and approval.
SEBI reviews these documents to ensure they meet regulatory requirements and contain
adequate disclosures. SEBI may request clarifications or revisions before granting approval.
The prospectus must provide investors with detailed information about the company’s
financial performance, risk factors, future plans, and the pricing of the securities.
3. Ensuring Transparency and Fair Practices
Page 2 of 7
--- page 3 ---
SEBI mandates that all companies issuing securities in the primary market maintain
transparency by providing detailed and timely disclosures.
It ensures that investors have access to critical information that helps them make informed
decisions regarding investment in new issues.
SEBI also ensures that the pricing of securities is done fairly, particularly in book-building
processes, where price discovery occurs through bidding.
4. Protecting Investor Interests
One of SEBI’s primary roles is to safeguard the interests of investors in the primary market. It
ensures that companies do not mislead investors through false claims, overstatement of
financials, or withholding material information.
SEBI has the authority to take action against companies or promoters found guilty of fraud,
misrepresentation, or non-compliance with regulations.
5. Regulating Underwriters, Merchant Bankers, and Intermediaries
SEBI regulates all the intermediaries involved in the primary market, including
underwriters, merchant bankers, brokers, and registrars. These intermediaries play a
crucial role in facilitating the issuance of securities.
SEBI sets qualifications and responsibilities for intermediaries to ensure that they act ethically
and fairly. It also oversees their role in the preparation of the offer documents, pricing of
issues, and marketing to potential investors.
6. Monitoring Price Manipulation
SEBI actively monitors the pricing of shares in the primary market to prevent price
manipulation or insider trading during and after the issuance of securities.
SEBI ensures that no group of investors or insiders can influence the price of a security
unfairly for personal gain at the expense of other investors.

A. Bad Delivery 4 Marks   8
Bad Delivery occurs when the securities delivered to the buyer do not meet the required
standards or specifications set by the stock exchange or clearing house. This can happen due to
various reasons:
Page 3 of 7
--- page 4 ---
·   Incorrect Securities: The delivered securities might not match the ones specified in the
trade. For example, the security delivered could be of a different class or issue than what was
agreed upon.
·   Defective Documentation: If the delivery includes paperwork, such as transfer deeds or
endorsements, and these are not correctly executed or are defective, it can be considered a bad
delivery.
·   Improper Authentication: If the securities are not properly authenticated or are found to
be counterfeit, the delivery would be considered bad.
Consequences: When a bad delivery is identified, the buyer typically rejects the securities, and
the seller must replace them with the correct or acceptable securities. The issue needs to be
resolved promptly to avoid complications in the settlement process.
Short Delivery 4 Marks
Short Delivery occurs when the seller fails to deliver the full quantity of securities that was
agreed upon in the trade. This can happen for several reasons:
·   Insufficient Holdings: The seller might not have enough of the securities to meet the full
delivery obligation. This could be due to an oversight or mismanagement of their portfolio.
·   Operational Errors: Mistakes or errors during the settlement process can lead to a short
delivery, where the seller inadvertently delivers less than what was required.
Consequences: In the event of a short delivery, the buyer is entitled to receive the missing
quantity of securities. The clearing house or depository typically steps in to facilitate the delivery
of the shortfall, often by sourcing the missing securities from the market or by making alternative
arrangements. The seller might face penalties or additional costs for failing to deliver the full
quantity.
B. Valuation Price 4 Marks   8
Valuation Price refers to the price at which securities are valued for the purpose of calculating
margins, mark-to-market (MTM) gains or losses, and overall portfolio value. It is the price used
by the exchange to assess the current value of securities held in a trading account.
·   Purpose: Valuation Price is used to determine the current market value of securities for
margin calculations and to ensure that the value of securities held is sufficient to cover any
potential losses or margin requirements.
·   Calculation: The valuation price is typically based on the latest market price of the
security as quoted on the NSE. It reflects the current trading price of the security.
Valuation Debit 4 Marks
Valuation Debit refers to the debit amount that arises due to changes in the valuation price of
securities in a trading account. It represents the decrease in value or the loss that needs to be
accounted for due to a drop in the valuation price.
·   Purpose: The valuation debit is used to calculate any additional margin requirements or to
adjust the value of the securities in a trading account. It ensures that the trader’s margin
requirements are met and that any potential losses are covered.
Page 4 of 7
--- page 5 ---
·   Impact: When the valuation price of a security decreases, the valuation debit increases.
This may require the trader to either deposit additional funds or liquidate some positions to meet
margin requirements.
Example
Suppose you have a trading account with a portfolio of shares. The valuation price of the shares
is used to determine their current market value. If the market price of a stock falls, the valuation
price decreases, resulting in a valuation debit.
For example:
·   Initial Valuation Price: ₹100 per share
·   Current Valuation Price: ₹90 per share
·   Number of Shares: 1,000
The decrease in valuation price is ₹10 per share. Therefore, the valuation debit is:
·   Valuation Debit: ₹10 x 1,000 shares = ₹10,000
This valuation debit reflects a loss of ₹10,000 in the value of your portfolio due to the decline in
the share price. You might need to take action to address this, such as depositing additional funds
or selling some shares to maintain margin requirements.

A. 1. Settlement Cycle Overview: ( 8 marks for explanation)   8
The settlement cycle is the time taken from the date of a trade (trade date) to the date when the
securities are actually delivered to the buyer and the payment is made to the seller. In India, the
standard settlement cycle for equities is T+2, where:
·   T stands for the trade date.
·   2 stands for the number of days after the trade date by which the settlement must be
completed.
2. Phases of the Settlement Cycle:
a. Trade Date (T):
·   Execution: The date on which the buy or sell order is executed on the stock exchange.
·   Trade Confirmation: The trade details are confirmed by the trading members (brokers)
to their clients and to the stock exchange.
b. Clearing:
·   Clearing Date: The process involves determining the net obligations of each trading
member. This involves calculating the total amount of securities and funds that need to be
delivered or received.
·   Clearing House: The clearing house, such as the National Securities Clearing
Corporation Limited (NSCCL) for NSE and Indian Clearing Corporation Limited (ICCL)
for BSE, facilitates this process. It ensures that the obligations of buyers and sellers are settled
correctly.
·   Netting: The clearing house nets the trades, meaning that it calculates the net amount of
securities and funds that each member has to deliver or receive.
c. Settlement:
·   Settlement Date (T+2): The actual exchange of securities and funds takes place on the
second business day after the trade date.
·   Delivery vs. Payment (DVP): The settlement is conducted using the Delivery vs.
Payment (DVP) mechanism, which ensures that the delivery of securities happens
simultaneously with the payment.
o Delivery: The seller delivers the securities to the clearing house.
o Payment: The clearing house processes the payment and transfers the funds to the seller.
·   Dematerialization: Securities are delivered in electronic form (demat) rather than
physical form, facilitating faster and more secure transactions.
