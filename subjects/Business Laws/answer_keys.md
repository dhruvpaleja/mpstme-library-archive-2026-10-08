something, with a view to obtaining the other’s assent, it is called an offer (or

Here are the essentials of a valid offer under the Indian Contract Act, 1872
1. Intention to Create Legal Relationship – The offer must show willingness to be

2. Definite and Certain Terms – The offer’s terms must be clear and unambiguous.
3. Communication of Offer – The offer must be communicated to the offeree.
4. Made with an Object to Obtain Assent – The offer must be made to get the other
party’s acceptance.
5. May be Specific or General – It can be made to a specific person or the public at
large.

II. ANSWER   5
The case of Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893) is a landmark decision in contract
law, particularly regarding unilateral contracts and the enforceability of advertisements.
The Carbolic Smoke Ball Company, in an advertisement, offered a £100 reward to
anyone who contracted influenza after using their smoke ball according to the directions.
Mrs. Carlill purchased and used the smoke ball as directed but still got sick. The court
upheld the contract, finding that the advertisement constituted a valid offer to the public,
acceptance was by performance (using the smoke ball), and there was sufficient
consideration
Facts:
The Carbolic Smoke Ball Company produced and sold a product called the "smoke
ball," claiming it could prevent influenza and other illnesses.
They advertised that they would pay £100 to anyone who used the smoke ball as
directed and still caught the flu.
The company also stated that they had deposited £1,000 in a bank to show their sincerity
in the offer.
Mrs. Carlill purchased and used the smoke ball as directed but still got sick.
She then claimed the £100 reward, but the company refused to pay.
Issues:
Was the advertisement a legally binding offer, or merely a "sales puff" (a non-binding
exaggeration)?
Did Mrs. Carlill's use of the smoke ball constitute acceptance of the offer?
Was there a valid contract between the parties?
Decision:
The court held that the advertisement was a valid offer, not mere sales puffery, due to the
company's statement about depositing money in the bank.
Mrs. Carlill's use of the smoke ball, as directed, constituted acceptance of the offer.
The court found that there was a valid unilateral contract between Mrs. Carlill and the
company.
Reasoning:
Page 2 of 6
--- page 3 ---
The court reasoned that the advertisement was a unilateral offer, meaning an offer made
to the world at large, which could be accepted by anyone who performed the specified
conditions.
The deposit of £1,000 in the bank demonstrated the company's serious intention to be
bound by their promise.
The court rejected the argument that Mrs. Carlill needed to communicate her acceptance
before using the smoke ball, stating that performance of the conditions of the offer was
sufficient.
The court also found that Mrs. Carlill's inconvenience in using the smoke ball as
directed constituted sufficient consideration for the company's promise.

I. ANSWER Meaning - 3 + Exception 7   10
- The term caveat emptor is a Latin word which means “let the buyer beware”.
- It implies that while purchasing the goods, the buyer must be cautious. This principle
states that, at the time of buying goods, the buyer must make reasonable examination of
the goods to satisfy himself that the goods are suitable for his purpose.
In simple words, it is not the seller’s duty to give to the buyer the goods which are fit
for a suitable purpose of the buyer. It is up to the buyer to make proper selection of
goods according to his needs. If he makes a wrong selection, he cannot blame the seller
if the goods turn out to be defective or do not serve his purpose.
Exceptions to the Doctrine of Caveat Emptor
(1) Where the seller makes a false representation and the buyer relies on it.
(2) When the buyer, relying upon the skill and judgement of the seller, has expressly or
impliedly communicated to him the purpose for which the goods are required.
(3) Fitness as to quality or use: Where the buyer makes known to the seller the
particular purpose for which the goods are required, so as to show that he relies on the
seller’s skill or judgment. it is the duty of the seller to supply such goods as are
reasonably fit for that purpose [Section 16 (1)].
In Bombay Burma Trading Corporation Ltd. vs. Aga Muhammad, timber was
purchased for the express purpose of using it as railways sleepers and when it was
found to be unfit for the purpose, the Court held that the contract could be avoided.
(4) Goods purchased under patent or brand name: In case where the goods are
purchased under its patent name or brand name, there is no implied condition that the
goods shall be fit for any particular purpose [Section 16(1)].
Page 4 of 6
--- page 5 ---
(5) Goods sold by description: Where the goods are sold by description there is an
implied condition that the goods shall correspond with the description [Section 15]. If it
is not so, then seller is responsible.
(6) Goods of Merchantable Quality: Where the goods are bought by description from a
seller then there is an implied condition that the goods shall be of merchantable quality.
(7) Sale by sample: Where the goods are bought by sample, this rule of Caveat Emptor
does not apply if the bulk does not correspond with the sample [Sec17].
(8) Goods by sample as well as description: Where the goods are bought by sample as
well as description, the rule of Caveat Emptor is not applicable in case the goods do not
correspond with both the sample and description or either of the condition [Section 15].
(9) Trade Usage: An implied warranty or condition as to quality or fitness for a
particular purpose may be annexed by the usage of trade [Section 16(3)].
Example: In readymade garment business, there is an implied condition by usage of
trade that the garments shall be reasonably fit on the buyer.
(10) Seller actively conceals a defect or is guilty of fraud: Where the seller sells the
goods by making some misrepresentation or fraud and the buyer relies on it or when
the seller actively conceals some defect in the goods so that the same could not be
discovered by the buyer on a reasonable examination, then the rule of Caveat Emptor
will not apply. In such a case the buyer has a right to avoid the contract and claim
damages.
II. ANSWER   5
No, Mr. Joshi is not legally bound to accept the delivery and pay for it.
Where there is a contract of sale of goods by description, there is an implied condition
that goods delivered by seller shall correspond with such description. If the goods does
not correspond with the description, the buyer is not bound to accept and pay for the
goods.

Liability of product manufacturer
A product manufacturer will be held liable in a product liability action under the
following circumstances:
The product contains manufacturing defects.
The product is defective.

--- page 6 ---
There is a deviation from manufacturing specifications.
The product does not conform to the express warranty.
The product fails to contain adequate information for proper usage.
Liability of product service provider
A product service provider will be held liable in a product liability action under the
following circumstances:
The service provider will be responsible when the service provided by them is faulty or
imperfect.
There was an act of negligence on their part.
The service provider failed to issue adequate instructions and warnings for the services.
The service provider failed to conform to the express warranty or terms and conditions
of the contract.
Liability of product seller
A product seller will be held liable in a product liability action under the following
circumstances:
They altered or modified the product which resulted in being detrimental to the
consumer.
They failed to exercise reasonable care in assembling, inspecting or maintaining such
product
They exercised substantial control over the product which resulted in causing harm to
the consumer.

contracts with minors are void ab initio (void from the beginning) under Indian Contract

The case of Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893) is a landmark decision in contract
law, particularly regarding unilateral contracts and the enforceability of advertisements.
The Carbolic Smoke Ball Company, in an advertisement, offered a £100 reward to
anyone who contracted influenza after using their smoke ball according to the directions.
Mrs. Carlill purchased and used the smoke ball as directed but still got sick. The court
upheld the contract, finding that the advertisement constituted a valid offer to the public,
acceptance was by performance (using the smoke ball), and there was sufficient
consideration
Facts:
The Carbolic Smoke Ball Company produced and sold a product called the "smoke
ball,"
claiming it could prevent influenza and other illnesses. They advertised that they would
pay £100 to anyone who used the smoke ball as directed and still caught the flu. The
company also stated that they had deposited £1,000 in a bank to show their sincerity in
the offer. Mrs. Carlill purchased and used the smoke ball as directed but still got sick.
She then claimed the £100 reward, but the company refused to pay.
Issues:
Was the advertisement a legally binding offer, or merely a "sales puff" (a non-
binding exaggeration)?
Did Mrs. Carlill's use of the smoke ball constitute acceptance of the offer?
Was there a valid contract between the parties?
Decision:
The court held that the advertisement was a valid offer, not mere sales puffery, due to
the
company's statement about depositing money in the bank.
Mrs. Carlill's use of the smoke ball, as directed, constituted acceptance of the offer.
The court found that there was a valid unilateral contract between Mrs. Carlill and the
company.
Reasoning:
The court reasoned that the advertisement was a unilateral offer, meaning an offer made
to the world at large, which could be accepted by anyone who performed the specified
conditions.
The deposit of £1,000 in the bank demonstrated the company's serious intention to be
bound by their promise.
The court rejected the argument that Mrs. Carlill needed to communicate her acceptance
before using the smoke ball, stating that performance of the conditions of the offer was
sufficient.
The court also found that Mrs. Carlill's inconvenience in using the smoke ball as
directed constituted sufficient consideration for the company's promise.
