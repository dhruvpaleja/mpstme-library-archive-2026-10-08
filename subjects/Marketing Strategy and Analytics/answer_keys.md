Q2   a. Steps in Creating a Marketing Strategy (10 marks)   [10]
A marketing strategy is a structured plan that enables a firm to achieve its
business objectives by identifying target customers and delivering value
effectively.
The first step is to define clear objectives. These objectives should be specific,
measurable, and aligned with the overall business goals, such as increasing
market share, improving brand awareness, or driving revenue growth.
The second step is conducting market research and analysis. This involves
understanding customer needs, analyzing competitors, and studying market
trends. Tools like SWOT analysis help identify internal strengths and weaknesses
along with external opportunities and threats.
The third step is applying Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning. The market
is divided into segments based on characteristics such as demographics, behavior,
or lifestyle. The firm then selects the most attractive segments and positions its
offering to create a distinct image in the minds of customers.
The fourth step is developing a value proposition. This defines the unique
benefits that the product or service offers to customers and explains why it is
better than alternatives. For example, Apple positions its products around
innovation and premium quality.
The fifth step is designing the marketing mix. This includes decisions related to
product features, pricing strategy, distribution channels, and promotional
activities. These elements must be aligned with the needs of the target segment.
The sixth step is implementation. The firm allocates resources, assigns
responsibilities, and executes the strategy in a coordinated manner.
The final step is monitoring and control. Performance is evaluated using key
metrics such as sales, customer acquisition, and return on investment. Based on
feedback and market changes, the strategy is refined.
A well-developed marketing strategy ensures efficient use of resources, better
customer alignment, and sustained competitive advantage.
Q2   b. Consumer Utility from Product Features (10 marks)   [10]
Consumer utility refers to the satisfaction or value that a consumer derives from
a product. In modern marketing, utility is derived from individual product
features rather than the product as a whole.
Each product consists of multiple attributes such as price, quality, design, and
functionality. Consumers evaluate these attributes and assign a certain level of
utility to each. The total utility of a product is the combined effect of these
individual utilities.
Consumers derive utility by making trade-offs among features. For example,
some consumers may prefer higher quality even at a higher price, while others
may prioritize affordability. These preferences vary across individuals and
segments.
Techniques such as conjoint analysis and choice-based models are used to
measure the utility associated with different features. These methods help firms
understand which attributes are most valued by customers and how they influence
purchase decisions.
Feature-based evaluation plays a significant role in product design. Firms can
develop products that closely match customer preferences by focusing on the
most valued features. For example, Samsung offers different smartphone variants
with varying combinations of features to cater to diverse customer needs.

Q3   b. Cross-Selling and Use of Analytics (10 marks)   [10]
Cross-selling is a marketing strategy in which firms encourage customers to
purchase additional or complementary products along with their primary
purchase. The objective is to increase the overall value of each transaction,
enhance customer experience, and improve customer lifetime value.
The concept is based on understanding customer needs and identifying products
that naturally complement each other. For example, a customer buying a
smartphone may also be interested in accessories such as earphones or
protective cases. Cross-selling is different from upselling, which involves
persuading customers to buy a higher-end version of the same product.
Firms use customer data and analytics extensively to implement effective cross-
selling strategies. The first step involves collecting data from multiple sources
such as transaction history, browsing behavior, purchase frequency, and
customer demographics. This data helps in building a comprehensive customer
profile.
The next step is analyzing purchasing patterns. Techniques such as market
basket analysis are used to identify products that are frequently bought together.
This helps firms discover associations and generate rules that can be used for
recommending complementary products.
Segmentation plays a key role in cross-selling. Customers are grouped based on
behavior, preferences, and value. Different cross-selling strategies are then
designed for each segment. For example, frequent buyers may receive premium
product recommendations, while occasional buyers may be targeted with
discounts or bundles.
Recommendation systems are widely used to automate cross-selling. These
systems use collaborative filtering and other machine learning algorithms to
suggest products based on similarities between users or items. For instance,
Amazon uses phrases like “customers who bought this also bought” to promote
related items.
Predictive analytics further enhances cross-selling by estimating the likelihood
of a customer purchasing additional products. Models can identify the right
timing and channel for recommendations, increasing the chances of conversion.
Personalization is another important aspect. Firms tailor recommendations
based on individual preferences, past purchases, and real-time behavior. For

form of cross-selling in digital services.
Finally, firms continuously monitor and optimize cross-selling strategies using
performance metrics such as conversion rate, average order value, and customer
retention. A/B testing is often used to evaluate the effectiveness of different
recommendation approaches.
In conclusion, cross-selling is a powerful strategy that, when supported by data
and analytics, enables firms to deliver relevant recommendations, enhance
customer satisfaction, and drive higher revenue.

Q4   b. Logarithmic & Probability Distributions in Purchase Behavior (10 marks)   [10]
Probability distributions are widely used in marketing analytics to model
consumer purchase behavior, particularly purchase frequency and variability.
The logarithmic distribution is useful in modeling situations where a small
number of customers account for a large proportion of purchases. It helps
identify “heavy users” who contribute disproportionately to sales.
The Poisson distribution is commonly used to model the number of purchases
made by a consumer in a fixed time period. It assumes that purchases occur
independently and at a constant average rate.

However, real-world data often shows greater variability than Poisson can
capture. In such cases, the negative binomial distribution is more appropriate
as it accounts for heterogeneity across consumers and over-dispersion in
purchase frequency.
These distributions help firms understand patterns such as frequency of
purchase, variability in demand, and customer heterogeneity.
Probability distributions are important in marketing analytics for several
reasons. They enable demand forecasting, helping firms predict future sales
and plan inventory. They support customer segmentation by identifying
heavy, medium, and light users. They also aid in targeting and promotion
planning, allowing firms to design offers based on expected purchase behavior.
Additionally, these models help in evaluating the effectiveness of marketing
strategies by comparing expected and actual outcomes. They provide a
scientific basis for decision-making rather than relying on intuition.
Overall, probability distributions are essential tools for understanding and
predicting consumer behavior, leading to more efficient and effective marketing
strategies.
Q5   a. Qualitative Market Research – Benefits & Limitations (10 marks)   [10]
Qualitative market research focuses on understanding consumer attitudes,
motivations, and perceptions through non-numerical data such as interviews,
focus groups, and observations.
One major benefit is deep insights. It helps uncover underlying motivations,
emotions, and reasons behind consumer behavior that quantitative methods may
not capture.
Another benefit is flexibility. Researchers can adapt questions and explore new
ideas during the study, leading to richer insights.
Qualitative research is also useful in exploratory research, especially in the
early stages of product development. It helps generate hypotheses and identify
key issues.
It supports product development and innovation by capturing customer
feedback on features, design, and usability.
Additionally, it helps in understanding complex behaviors and perceptions,
such as brand image and customer satisfaction.
However, qualitative research has several limitations. One major limitation is
small sample size, which makes it difficult to generalize findings to the entire
population.
Another limitation is subjectivity and bias. Results may be influenced by the
researcher’s interpretation or the respondent’s opinions.
It also lacks quantitative measurement, making it difficult to perform
statistical analysis or compare results objectively.
Qualitative research can be time-consuming and costly, especially when
conducting in-depth interviews or focus groups.
Finally, findings may not always be replicable, reducing reliability.
In conclusion, qualitative research provides valuable insights but should ideally
be complemented with quantitative methods for robust decision-making.

Q5   b. Strategies in Mature & Declining Markets (10 marks)   [10]
In mature markets, growth slows down as most customers already own the
product and competition is intense. Firms focus on maintaining market share
and improving efficiency.
Key strategies in mature markets include market penetration, where firms try
to increase usage among existing customers through promotions and loyalty
programs. Product differentiation is used to stand out through features,
quality, or branding.
Firms may also adopt product modification strategies, such as improving
features or introducing variations to attract new users. Cost efficiency and
process optimization become important to maintain profitability.
For example, Coca-Cola introduces new variants and packaging to sustain
demand in a mature beverage market.
In declining markets, demand decreases due to technological changes, shifting
consumer preferences, or market saturation.
Firms must make strategic choices such as harvesting, where they reduce
investment and maximize short-term profits. Another option is divestment,
where the firm exits the market completely.
Some firms may choose a niche strategy, focusing on a smaller but profitable
segment that still values the product.
Alternatively, firms may attempt revitalization by repositioning the product or
finding new uses.
For example, traditional media companies have adapted to digital platforms to
survive declining print markets.
In conclusion, strategic choices in mature and declining markets require careful
evaluation of market conditions, competitive dynamics, and firm capabilities to
sustain profitability and long-term viability.

Q7   b. Purchase Incidence Models (10 marks)   [10]
i. Use of Poisson distribution
The Poisson distribution is used to model the number of purchase events
occurring in a fixed time period (e.g., number of grocery orders per month).
It assumes:
• Purchases occur independently
• The average purchase rate (λ) is constant
For FreshMart, Poisson can estimate:
•   Probability that a customer places 0, 1, 2, … orders in a week or month
• Average ordering frequency across customers
It is useful for modeling regular, stable purchasing behavior, such as weekly
grocery buyers.
ii. Why Negative Binomial is more appropriate
In reality, customers are not identical. Some order frequently, while others
order rarely. This creates over-dispersion, where variance exceeds the mean.
The Poisson model assumes mean = variance, which is often unrealistic.
The Negative Binomial distribution:
• Accounts for heterogeneity across customers
• Captures higher variability in purchase frequency
•   Better fits real-world data with irregular buying patterns
Thus, it is more suitable for modeling diverse customer behaviors on
FreshMart.
iii. Role of Logarithmic distribution for heavy users
The logarithmic distribution is useful for modeling skewed purchase behavior,
where:
• A small proportion of users (heavy users) generate a large number of
orders
• Most users contribute relatively few purchases
For FreshMart:
• Helps identify “power users” who order very frequently
• Captures long-tail behavior in customer base
This allows the firm to focus on high-value customers who drive significant
revenue.
iv. Consumer segmentation based on purchase incidence
Customers can be segmented based on predicted purchase frequency:
• Heavy users: Frequent orders (e.g., weekly)
• Moderate users: Occasional but consistent orders
• Light users: Rare or irregular orders
Marketing implications:
• Heavy users → loyalty programs, premium services

Q7   b. Repeat Buying Models (10 marks)   [10]
i. Zero-order model for repeat buying
The Zero-order model assumes that the probability of purchase is constant and
independent over time. Each customer has a fixed probability of repeat
purchase, and past behavior does not influence future behavior.
In the context of AutoCare:
• Every customer has a constant likelihood of visiting for servicing
• No memory of previous visits is considered
Assumptions:
• Independence of purchase events
• Constant purchase probability
• No effect of satisfaction, promotions, or service experience
While simple, this model is useful as a baseline but may not reflect real
servicing behavior where past experience matters.
ii. Markov model for customer state transitions
A Markov model captures how customers move between different states over
time based on transition probabilities.
For AutoCare, states can be:
• Active (regular servicing)
•   Inactive (delayed visits)
• Churned (no return)
The model uses a transition matrix showing probabilities such as:
• Active → Active
• Active → Inactive
•   Inactive → Active
•   Inactive → Churned
It assumes:
• Future state depends only on current state (memoryless property)
This helps AutoCare:
•   Predict customer movement across states
• Estimate churn probability
•   Identify when intervention is needed
iii. Learning model for increasing service frequency
The Learning model assumes that customers learn and adapt over time,
increasing their purchase frequency as they gain experience.
In AutoCare:
•   First-time customers may visit occasionally
• As trust builds, they adopt regular servicing packages
•   Positive experiences increase repeat visits
This model captures:
• Habit formation
• Brand loyalty development
•   Increased service adoption over time
Thus, it reflects dynamic behavior rather than constant probabilities.

iv. Use of repeat buying models for retention programs
AutoCare can use these models to design targeted strategies:
• From Zero-order insights: identify average repeat rates and benchmark

• From Markov model: target customers likely to move from active to
inactive with reminders or offers
• From Learning model: nurture new customers with onboarding offers
and service packages
Retention actions:
•   Service reminders and maintenance alerts
• Loyalty programs and subscription plans
•   Personalized discounts for inactive customers
This ensures timely and effective customer engagement.
v. Limitations and ways to address them
Limitations:
• Zero-order model ignores past behavior
• Markov model assumes no long-term memory beyond current state
• Learning model may oversimplify behavior patterns
• Real-world behavior is influenced by external factors like price,
competition, and convenience
Ways to address:
• Use hybrid models combining multiple approaches
•   Incorporate customer-level data and segmentation
• Apply machine learning models for better prediction
• Continuously update models using real-time data

Repeat buying models provide valuable insights into customer behavior, but
