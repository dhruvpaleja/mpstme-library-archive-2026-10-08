Q1 a. What are parallel imports? When do they develop?
Parallel imports
–Develop when importers buy products from distributors in one country and sell them in another to
distributors who are not part of the manufacturer’s regular distribution system
•Occur whenever price differences are greater than cost of transportation between two markets
Q1 b. What are the three factors which affect demand in industrial markets differently as compared to
consumer markets?
Three factors affect the demand in industrial markets differently than in consumer markets. They are:
–Volatility of industrial demand(demand in industrial markets is by nature more volatile)
–Stages of economic development (stages of industrial and economic development affect demand for
industrial products)
–Technology and market demand (the level of technology of products and services make their sales more
appropriate for some countries than others)

Q3. An African nation is rapidly building textile manufacturing hubs with rising exports and increasing
employment. Infrastructure investments are improving supply chains, and industrial demand is rising
steadily. Name and explain all the stages of Rostow’s model and explain which stage of Rostow’s model
best describes this economy? What kind of demand patterns would firms supplying this market observe?
• Stage 1 – the traditional society (eg. Natural resource extraction in Africe & Middle East)
• Stage 2 – preconditions for takeoff (primary needs are agriculture and infra development,
manufacturing is beginning)
• Stage 3 – take off (Manufacturing has begun, demand for equipment & supplies)
• Stage 4 – drive to maturity (industrialized economies, focus on low-cost manufacturing)
• Stage 5 – the age of mass consumption (service economies)

Q5. A Chinese electronics manufacturer and a European retailer disagree on product defects in a shipment.
The retailer seeks compensation, while the manufacturer denies liability. Both companies agree to involve a
neutral third party to resolve the issue. There are three ways to deal with such international disputes, list
down all three and recommend the best one.
Three ways to deal with international disputes:
–Conciliation
–Arbitration
–Litigation
Conciliationis a nonbinding agreement between parties to resolve disputes by asking a third party to
mediate differences
–Sessions are private
–All conferences between parties and the mediator are confidential
•Although conciliation may be the friendly route to resolving disputes
–It is not legally binding
–An arbitration clause should be included in all conciliation agreements
Arbitration
Conducted under the auspices of one or more formal domestic and international arbitration groups
–Organized specifically to facilitate the resolution of commercial disputes
•The popularity of arbitration has led to a proliferation of arbitral centers
–Established by countries, organizations, and institutions
•Inter-American Commercial Arbitration Commission
•Canadian-American Commercial Arbitration Commission (for disputes between Canadian and U.S.
businesses)
•London Court of Arbitration (decisions are enforceable under English law and English courts)
•American Arbitration Association
•International Chamber of Commerce (select Arbitration)
Contracts and other legal documents should include clauses specifying the use of arbitration to settle
disputes
•Arbitration clauses require agreement on two counts
–To arbitrate in the case of a dispute according to the rules and procedures of some arbitration tribunal
–To abide by the awards resulting from the arbitration
The best advice is to seek settlement
•Deterrents to litigation
–Fear of creating a poor image and damaging public relations
–Fear of unfair treatment in a foreign court
–Difficulty in collecting a judgment that may otherwise have been collected in a mutually agreed settlement
through arbitration
–The relatively high cost and time required when bringing legal action

Q7. A quick-service restaurant chain is entering smaller cities and needs to decide menu localization,
pricing, and store formats. Explain how the company can use the six-step research process to guide its
expansion strategy.
Research process steps
1.Define the research problem and establish research objectives
2.Determine the sources of information to fulfill the research objectives
3.Consider the costs and benefits of the research effort
4.Gather relevant data from secondary or primary sources, or both
5.Analyze, interpret, and summarize the results
6.Effectively communicate the results to decision makers
•Research steps are similar for all countries
–Variations and problems can occur in implementation
•Differences in cultural and economic development
Define the Problem
The major difficulty is converting a series of often ambiguous business problems into tightly drawn and
achievable research objectives
•The first, most crucial step in research is more critical in foreign markets because an unfamiliar environment
tends to could problems definition
•Other difficulties in foreign research stem from failures to establish problem limits broad enough to include all
relevant variables
********** 6 **********
--- page 7 ---
Availability and Reliability of Data
Most countries simply do not have governmental agencies that collect on a regular basis the kinds of secondary
data readily available in the U.S.
•Researchers’ language skills impede access to information
–Requires native speaker of language
•Official statistics are sometimes too optimistic, reflecting national pride rather than practical reality, while tax
structures and fear of the tax collector often adversely affect data
–Less-developed countries prone to optimism
–Willful errors
–“Adjusted reporting”
Validating Secondary Data
Questions to judge the reliability of secondary data sources
–Who collected the data?
–Would there be any reason for purposely misrepresenting the facts?
–For what purposes was the data collected?
–How was the data collected?
–Are the data internally consistent and logical in light of known data sources or market factors?
•Checking the consistency of one set of secondary data with other data of known validity
–An effective and often-used way of judging validity
•The availability and accuracy of recorded secondary data increase with level of economic development
Qualitative research
–If questions are asked, they are almost always open-ended or in-depth
–Seeks unstructured responses that reflect the person’s thoughts and feelings on the subject
•Qualitative research interprets people in the sample
•Qualitative research is helpful in revealing the impact of sociocultural factors on behavior patterns and in
developing research hypotheses
Sampling in field surveys
Problems in sampling stem from the lack of adequate demographic data and available lists from which to draw
meaningful samples
•Affected by a lack of detailed social and economic information
–No officially recognized census information
–No other listings that can serve as sampling frames
–Incomplete and out-of-date telephone directories
–No accurate maps of population centers
Language and Comprehension

--- page 8 ---

•Literacy poses yet another problem
•Marketers use three different techniques to help ferret out translation errors ahead of time
–Back translation
–Parallel translation
–Decentering

e. INCOTERMS rules are grouped into 4 categories. Name all of them.
INCOTERMS rules are grouped into 4 categories and they are as follows:-

• The “D” terms: (DAF, DES, DEQ, DDU and DDP) – Terms where the
seller/exporter/manufacturer is responsible for all costs and risks associated with
bringing the goods to the named place of destination.

▪ Brands should highlight prestige, status, and endorsements from authoritative

o Individualistic cultures (e.g., U.S., U.K., Canada)

▪ Marketing should focus on customization, personal success, and individual

▪   Advertising should highlight community values, family benefits, and social

o Masculine cultures (e.g., Japan, U.S., Germany)
▪ Value achievement, competition, and success.
▪ Marketing should emphasize performance, ambition, and superiority (e.g., “#1

o Feminine cultures (e.g., Sweden, Netherlands)
▪ Value care, quality of life, and relationships.
▪ Marketing should focus on customer well-being, sustainability, and emotional
connection.
4. Uncertainty Avoidance (UAI) – The extent to which a society tolerates ambiguity and risk.
o High UAI cultures (e.g., Greece, Japan, France)
▪   Prefer structure, clear rules, and reliability.

o Low UAI cultures (e.g., U.S., Singapore, Denmark)
▪ More open to innovation and risk-taking.
▪ Marketing can highlight novelty, adventure, and experimentation.

o Long-term oriented cultures (e.g., China, Japan, South Korea)
▪ Value patience, perseverance, and long-term success.

Question 4: Is it ethical to sell a product that is, at best, only mildly effective? Discuss it in the context
of Fair & Lovely.
The ethicality of selling a product like Fair & Lovely, which is at best only mildly effective, raises
significant concerns, especially considering the cultural and societal implications tied to its use and
advertising. The Fair & Lovely case reveals a complex landscape where the demand for such products is
not merely a commercial phenomenon but deeply intertwined with societal norms and values that
associate fair skin with beauty and success.
Ethical Considerations in Product Effectiveness
1. Consumer Expectation vs. Reality: The product claims to deliver "one to three shades of
change" which, according to dermatologists, is unlikely since such creams mainly affect the
upper skin layers and do not alter melanin production. This discrepancy between marketing
promises and actual product efficacy can mislead consumers, fostering false hopes and
potentially wasting their financial resources on ineffective solutions.
2. Exploitation of Aspirations: Many users continue to use the product despite limited or no
results, as evidenced by a user who remarked they should have turned "into Snow White by now"
but saw no change in their skin colour. This persistence is driven by deeply ingrained societal
pressures and aspirations to achieve an often-unattainable beauty standard, which the product
explicitly exploits.
Ethical Considerations in Marketing Practices
1. Reinforcing Harmful Stereotypes: Fair & Lovely's advertising has historically utilized
narratives that reinforce stereotypes linking skin color with success and desirability. Although
recent campaigns have slightly shifted towards themes of self-confidence, the underlying
message still promotes skin lightening as a means to enhance personal success, which can
perpetuate discrimination and undermine social progress towards acceptance of all skin tones.
2. Impact on Social Values: The product and its marketing exploit and reinforce existing racial
and colour biases, thus contributing to a societal structure that values individuals not on their
merit or capabilities but on superficial traits like skin colour. This can have broader implications,
affecting individuals' self-esteem and social dynamics.
Broader Ethical Questions
The sale of Fair & Lovely and similar products also touches on broader ethical issues about the
responsibilities of businesses in society:
• Corporate Responsibility: Companies have a duty to ensure that their products and marketing
practices do not exploit vulnerable consumer groups or reinforce harmful societal norms.
• Consumer Protection: There is a need for stricter regulations to ensure that product claims are
transparent and verifiable, protecting consumers from being misled by hyperbolic and false
advertising.
In conclusion, while it is technically legal to sell a product like Fair & Lovely, the ethicality of doing so
is questionable. Companies should strive for greater honesty in advertising and consider the broader
societal impact of their products, especially in markets where cultural biases regarding skin colour can
lead to significant social and personal consequences.

Question 7: With reference to international marketing research, list the various problems that are
associated with gathering primary data.
With reference to international marketing research, the various problems associated with gathering
primary data include:
• Ability to communicate opinions: The ability to express attitudes and opinions about a product
or concept depends on the respondent’s ability to recognize the usefulness and value of such a
product or concept. It is difficult for a person to formulate needs, attitudes, and opinions about
goods whose use may not be understood, that are not in common use within the community, or
that have never been available.
• Willingness to respond: Cultural differences offer the best explanation for the unwillingness or
the inability of many to respond to research surveys.
• Sampling in field surveys: The greatest problem in sampling stems from the lack of adequate
demographic data and available lists from which to draw meaningful samples. If current, reliable
lists are not available, sampling becomes more complex and generally less reliable.
• Language and comprehension: The most universal survey research problem in foreign countries is the
language barrier. Differences in idioms and the difficulty of exact translation create problems in
eliciting the specific information desired and in interpreting the respondents’ answers.

Question 4: Is it ethical to sell a product that is, at best, only mildly effective? Discuss it in the context
of Fair & Lovely.
The ethicality of selling a product like Fair & Lovely, which is at best only mildly effective, raises
significant concerns, especially considering the cultural and societal implications tied to its use and
advertising. The Fair & Lovely case reveals a complex landscape where the demand for such products is
not merely a commercial phenomenon but deeply intertwined with societal norms and values that
associate fair skin with beauty and success.
Ethical Considerations in Product Effectiveness
1. Consumer Expectation vs. Reality: The product claims to deliver "one to three shades of
change" which, according to dermatologists, is unlikely since such creams mainly affect the
upper skin layers and do not alter melanin production. This discrepancy between marketing
promises and actual product efficacy can mislead consumers, fostering false hopes and
potentially wasting their financial resources on ineffective solutions.
2. Exploitation of Aspirations: Many users continue to use the product despite limited or no
results, as evidenced by a user who remarked they should have turned "into Snow White by now"
but saw no change in their skin colour. This persistence is driven by deeply ingrained societal
pressures and aspirations to achieve an often-unattainable beauty standard, which the product
explicitly exploits.
Ethical Considerations in Marketing Practices
1. Reinforcing Harmful Stereotypes: Fair & Lovely's advertising has historically utilized
narratives that reinforce stereotypes linking skin color with success and desirability. Although
recent campaigns have slightly shifted towards themes of self-confidence, the underlying
message still promotes skin lightening as a means to enhance personal success, which can
perpetuate discrimination and undermine social progress towards acceptance of all skin tones.
2. Impact on Social Values: The product and its marketing exploit and reinforce existing racial
and colour biases, thus contributing to a societal structure that values individuals not on their
merit or capabilities but on superficial traits like skin colour. This can have broader implications,
affecting individuals' self-esteem and social dynamics.
Broader Ethical Questions
The sale of Fair & Lovely and similar products also touches on broader ethical issues about the
responsibilities of businesses in society:
• Corporate Responsibility: Companies have a duty to ensure that their products and marketing
practices do not exploit vulnerable consumer groups or reinforce harmful societal norms.
• Consumer Protection: There is a need for stricter regulations to ensure that product claims are
transparent and verifiable, protecting consumers from being misled by hyperbolic and false
advertising.
In conclusion, while it is technically legal to sell a product like Fair & Lovely, the ethicality of doing so
is questionable. Companies should strive for greater honesty in advertising and consider the broader
societal impact of their products, especially in markets where cultural biases regarding skin colour can
lead to significant social and personal consequences.

Question 6: What are the four forms of law and what are the heritages linked to these forms of law?
• Four heritages form the bases for the majority of the legal systems of the world:
(1) Common law, derived from English law and found in England, the United States, Canada,
and other countries once under English influence.
(2) Civil or code law, derived from Roman law and found in Germany, Japan, France, and non-
Islamic and non-Marxist countries.
(3) Islamic law, derived from the interpretation of the Koran and found in Pakistan, Iran, Saudi
Arabia, and other Islamic states; and
(4) a commercial legal system in the Marxist–socialist economies of Russia and the republics of
the former Soviet Union, Eastern Europe, China, and other Marxist–socialist states whose legal
system centered on the economic, political, and social policies of the state.
Question 7: With reference to international marketing research, list the various problems that are
associated with gathering primary data.
With reference to international marketing research, the various problems associated with gathering
primary data include:
• Ability to communicate opinions: The ability to express attitudes and opinions about a product
or concept depends on the respondent’s ability to recognize the usefulness and value of such a
product or concept. It is difficult for a person to formulate needs, attitudes, and opinions about
goods whose use may not be understood, that are not in common use within the community, or
that have never been available.
• Willingness to respond: Cultural differences offer the best explanation for the unwillingness or
the inability of many to respond to research surveys.
• Sampling in field surveys: The greatest problem in sampling stems from the lack of adequate
demographic data and available lists from which to draw meaningful samples. If current, reliable
lists are not available, sampling becomes more complex and generally less reliable.
• Language and comprehension: The most universal survey research problem in foreign countries is the
language barrier. Differences in idioms and the difficulty of exact translation create problems in
eliciting the specific information desired and in interpreting the respondents’ answers.

Cultural Context and Marketing Ethics
Fair & Lovely has leveraged a deep-seated cultural preference for lighter skin, which is associated with
higher social status, beauty, and professional opportunities in many parts of India. This preference is not
merely a contemporary marketing creation but is rooted in historical, social, and economic contexts that
date back centuries. The caste system, colonial histories, and media portrayals have all reinforced the
desirability of lighter skin.

social inequalities. Fair & Lovely's advertisements, which have suggested that success and social

harmful stereotypes that equate skin colour with worth and capability.
Ethical Considerations

personal and professional success. This is problematic as it can affect the self-esteem and
societal value of individuals with darker skin tones.

skin, dermatologists argue that such products only affect the upper layers of the skin and do not
alter melanin production, suggesting limited efficacy. This raises ethical concerns about the
truthfulness and transparency of the marketing claims.

advertisements can contribute to social divisions and discrimination. This goes against the
ethical responsibility of marketers to avoid campaigns that could exacerbate social inequalities.
