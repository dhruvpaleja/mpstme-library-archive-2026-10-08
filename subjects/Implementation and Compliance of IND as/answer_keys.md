Materiality means that information should be disclosed in financial statements if its omission,
misstatement, or hiding could influence the decisions of users such as investors, lenders, or
regulators. (01 mark)
Materiality depends on both size and nature of the item and must be judged in the context of
the financial statements as a whole.
The three expenses together amount to ₹11.5 crore, which is 27% of Profit Before Tax (₹42
crore). This is a large proportion of profit. Even though each expense is small when seen
individually, their combined amount is significant. Such a large impact on profit is likely to
influence the decisions of investors and lenders. Therefore, the expenses are quantitatively
material.
These expenses arise from unusual and non-recurring events such as flood damage, regulatory
penalty, and inventory write-off. They are also different in nature and do not relate to normal
business operations. Information about such events is important for users to understand
business risks, compliance issues, and operational problems. Hence, even if the amounts were
smaller, their nature makes them qualitatively material. (02 marks)
By size, the expenses are material because they significantly reduce profit.
By nature, they are material because they are unusual and dissimilar items.
Therefore, aggregating them under “Other Expenses” hides material information. Separate
disclosure is required to ensure that the financial statements present a true and fair view. (02
marks)

from the Balance Sheet when the entity loses control over the asset. (1 mark)
