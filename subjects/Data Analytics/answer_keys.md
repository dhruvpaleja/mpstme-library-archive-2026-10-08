Q6   a. Key Assumptions of Linear Regression:   [10]
• Linearity – The relationship between independent variable(s) and the
dependent variable must be linear. This ensures that the model can
accurately capture trends; a non-linear relationship violates this
assumption and leads to biased predictions.
• Independence of Errors – Residuals (errors) should be independent of
each other. This is crucial to avoid autocorrelation, which can make
standard errors unreliable, especially in time series data.
• Homoscedasticity – Residuals should have constant variance across all
levels of the independent variable(s). If variance changes
(heteroscedasticity), it can distort confidence intervals and hypothesis
tests.
• Absence of Multicollinearity (in multivariate regression) –
Independent variables should not be highly correlated. High
multicollinearity inflates standard errors of coefficients, making it
difficult to determine the individual effect of each predictor. Variance
Inflation Factor (VIF) is commonly used to detect it.
Comparison of Evaluation Metrics:
• Mean Squared Error (MSE) – Measures the average squared
difference between actual and predicted values. It heavily penalizes
large errors due to squaring, useful when outliers are important.
• Mean Absolute Error (MAE) – Computes the average absolute
difference between actual and predicted values. Less sensitive to outliers
than MSE and provides an intuitive error magnitude.
• Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) – Square root of MSE, bringing
error to the same unit as the dependent variable. Like MSE, it penalizes
large errors but is more interpretable in real-world units.
• R-squared (R²) – Represents the proportion of variance in the
dependent variable explained by the model. A higher R² indicates better
goodness of fit, but it does not indicate error magnitude. Adjusted R²
accounts for multiple predictors to prevent overestimation.
Role of Regularization Techniques:
********** 13 **********
--- page 14 ---
• Ridge Regression (L2) – Adds a penalty proportional to the sum of
squares of coefficients. It shrinks coefficients, reducing overfitting,
especially when predictors are highly correlated, while keeping all
features in the model.
• Lasso Regression (L1) – Adds a penalty proportional to the sum of
absolute values of coefficients. It can shrink some coefficients to zero,
performing feature selection, improving model simplicity,
interpretability, and generalization.
Overall Benefit – Regularization balances bias-variance tradeoff, prevents
overfitting, and enhances predictive performance on unseen data by penalizing
large or irrelevant coefficients.
