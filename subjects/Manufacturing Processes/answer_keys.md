Evaluation of Arc Welding Principle with Diagram (3 Marks)
Arc welding is a metal joining process that utilizes the heat generated by an electric arc to melt the edges of
two metal pieces. The electrical energy is converted into intense heat energy:
• Arc Generation: An electric arc is produced between a consumable electrode and the workpiece.
The electrode and workpiece are brought close with a small air gap (approximately 3 mm). Current
passed through this gap sustains the arc, reaching temperatures of 5000∘C to 6000∘C.
•   Fusion and Filler Metal: The arc melts both the edges of the workpiece and the electrode, which
acts as the additional filler metal. Both materials fuse to form a single, homogenous joint without
Q4   external pressure. The depth to which the metal is melted and deposited is called the depth of
CO-3;   fusion.
SO-2;   (a)   •   Flux Protection: Electrodes are coated with a flux. The flux's purpose is to prevent the molten metal   [10]
BL-5   from reacting with the atmospheric air, remove impurities, and form a protective slag layer over the
weld seam to slow cooling and prevent oxidation.
• Arc Geometry: The molten metal is forced out by the arc, creating a small depression in the parent
metal called the "arc crater." The distance between the tip of the electrode and the bottom of the arc
crater is the "arc length." For better depth of fusion, the electrode is typically kept at 70∘ inclination
to the vertical.
Common Weld Positions (2 Marks)
Welding positions are standardized based on the orientation of the weld joint. The ease of welding
decreases significantly from flat to overhead, requiring more skill and lower current/heat input to manage
the molten pool.

1. Flat (1G/1F): Welding is performed from the top side of the joint, allowing gravity to assist the
flow of the molten metal. This is the easiest and fastest position.
2. Horizontal (2G/2F): Welding along a horizontal seam on a vertical plane. Molten metal tends to
sag, requiring specific control.
3. Vertical (3G/3F): Welding up or down a vertical seam. Requires careful speed control to prevent

4. Overhead (4G/4F): Welding from the underside of a joint. The most challenging, as gravity pulls
the molten metal away from the joint.

Purpose and Principle (4 Marks)
The primary purpose of a shaper is to machine horizontal, vertical, or angular flat surfaces, including slots
and grooves.
•   Principle: The operation is based on the single-point cutting tool creating the primary cutting motion.
The tool performs a reciprocating motion (linear back-and-forth stroke) across the stationary
workpiece to remove material.
A shaper is a machine tool that uses a single-point cutting tool to remove material from a workpiece. The
cutting tool is reciprocated across the workpiece, and the workpiece is fed perpendicular to the direction of
Q4   travel of the cutting tool. This process is repeated until the desired shape is achieved.
CO-2;   Construction details of a shaper: The main components of a shaper are:
SO-1;   (b)   [10]
BL-1   Base: The base is a heavy casting that supports the entire machine.
Column: The column is a vertical casting that supports the ram and the tool head.
Ram: The ram is a reciprocating member that holds the tool head.
Cross rail: The cross rail is a horizontal member that slides on the column.
Saddle: The saddle is a movable member that slides on the cross rail and supports the table.
Basic Mechanism (3 Marks)
Primary Cutting Motion (Tool): The tool is mounted on the ram and moves forward in a straight line across
the workpiece (cutting stroke). It then returns rapidly in the return stroke (idle, no cutting). This is achieved
by the Quick Return Mechanism to maximize efficiency.
Feed Motion (Workpiece): The workpiece is clamped on the table and is given an intermittent step-feed
motion perpendicular to the cutting direction, typically during the idle return stroke.
Q5   Working Principle and Mechanism (3 Marks)
CO-1;   The true centrifugal casting process relies entirely on rotational dynamics to form the part:
SO-1;   (a)   •   Rotation: A permanent mold is rotated at high speeds (up to 1500 RPM) about a horizontal or vertical [10]
BL-2   axis.
•   Metal Placement: Molten metal is poured directly into the rotating mold cavity.

•   Driving Force: The centrifugal force generated by the rotation drives the metal outward, forcing it
against the mold walls and holding it there until solidification is complete.
•   Solidification Direction: Heat transfer occurs primarily through the mold wall, causing directional
solidification from the outer perimeter inward towards the central axis of rotation.

Justification: Analysis of Defects (5 Marks)
The inward solidification dictated by the working principle creates two critical, unavoidable defects at the
center of the bar:
1. Solidification Shrinkage Void (2.5 Marks): Metals contract (shrink) upon freezing. Since
solidification starts at the outside and progresses inward, the very last metal to freeze is concentrated
at the rotational axis. As this final metal shrinks, there is no liquid metal reservoir (riser) to feed the
center, resulting in a large, central defect known as a shrinkage cavity or axial porosity. This makes
the structural core unsound.
2. Inverse Segregation of Impurities (2.5 Marks): Centrifugal force separates materials based on
density. Dense, clean metal is forced outward to the casting surface, while lighter, less dense material
(including non-metallic inclusions, slag, dross, and low-density alloying elements) is driven inward,
concentrating at the rotation axis. This defect, known as inverse segregation, severely compromises
the mechanical and chemical integrity of the center core.
Reason for Suitability: Because the central portion is inherently weak and filled with defects, true centrifugal
casting is only suitable for manufacturing hollow, axisymmetric parts (pipes, rings, cylinder liners), where
the unsound central core can be removed later by machining/boring.
Defects in Rolling (Sheet and Plate Production) (2 Marks)
Rolling deforms metal between two rotating rollers. Defects typically relate to uneven deformation or
improper roll setting.
•   Irregularities: Leads to a trapping of scales which remain inside as laps.
•   Non-metallic inclusions: These defects are produced, especially during the hot rolling of a thick slab;
crocodile cracks may separate the product into two halves.
•   Internal blow holes in the stock: Results in an elongation of the blow holes, making the product
weaker.
o Minimisation: All the above defects can be minimised by a careful inspection of the billet
and by keeping the roll smooth and clean. To avoid the internal cracks, it is necessary to
design the roll pass properly.
Defects in Forging (Bulk Shaping) (2 Marks)
Forging defects are usually related to improper material flow or insufficient deformation during hammering
or pressing.
•   Scale pits: These are shallow depressions caused by hot scale being worked into the surface of the
Q5   forging from the dies.
CO-3;   •   Mismatch: Occurs in drop forging when the dies are incorrectly aligned, resulting in a lateral
SO-1;   (b)   [10]
BL-2   displacement between portions of the forging.
•   Unfilled section: Similar to misrun in casting, it occurs when metal does not completely fill the die
cavity. It is usually caused by using insufficient metal or insufficient heating of the metal.
•   Defects resulting from improper forging: Such as seams, cracks, and laps.
•   Ingot defects: Such as pipes, cracks, scabs, and segregation are carried through to the forging.
•   Defects resulting from the melting practice: Such as dirt, slag, and blow holes carried over.
•   Defects resulting from improper heating and cooling: Such as burnt metal, decarburised steel, and
flakes.
o Occurrence: These defects are found in all metals which are heated to a plastic stage and then
shaped.
Defects in Extrusion (Producing Long Profiles) (2 Marks)
Extrusion defects are often related to friction, high temperatures, or complex flow patterns near the die exit.
• The most common defect (known as Piping/Extrusion Defect) arises from the back flow of the
material, pushing the end face of the billet into the core of the product. Such a defect weakens the
product since the surface layer is normally contaminated by oxides.
• Sometimes the heat generated due to extrusion may raise the temperature of the job, resulting in the
development of surface cracks.

Defects in Drawing (Wire and Rod) (2 Marks)
Drawing involves pulling metal through a die to reduce its diameter. Defects are generally related to excessive
die forces or lubrication failure.
•   Surface Defects: The typical surface defects in rod and wire drawing are due to a ploughing by hard
particles and local breakdown of the lubricating film.
• Shape Defects: Other kinds of defects include formation of a bulge ahead of the die (with low
reduction and high die angle), and the development of a centre burst (internal cracks) with too large
a deformation gradient along the cross-section.
Defects in Deep Drawing (Cup/Can Production) (2 Marks)
Deep drawing involves punching a blank into a die cavity to form a hollow cup shape. Defects result from
anisotropic material properties or excessive strain during thinning.
•   Wrinkling: An insufficient blank holder pressure causes wrinkles to develop on the flange, which
may also extend to the wall of the cup.
•   Tearing/Fracture: Too much of a blank holder pressure and friction may cause a thinning of the walls
and a fracture at the flange, bottom, and corners (if any).
• Miss Strike: Due to the misplacement of the stock, unsymmetrical flanges may result; this defect is
commonly known as miss strike.
• Orange Peel Effect: The effect of a large grain size is to produce a dull surface (known as the orange
peel effect); this defect is also common in bending operations.
Refractoriness (Thermal Stability) (2.5 Marks)
•   Description: The ability of the sand to resist high temperatures without fusing, melting, or chemically
degrading. It dictates the maximum pouring temperature the sand can safely withstand.
•   Reason/Defect Prevention: The mold must remain stable when exposed to molten metal temperatures
(often 1400∘C to 1600∘C for steel).
•   Defect: Low refractoriness leads to Fusion or Burn-on, where the sand chemically fuses onto the
casting surface, making cleaning difficult.
Permeability (Porosity) (2.5 Marks)
•   Description: This is the ability of the sand mold to allow generated gases—such as air, steam (from
water content), and combustion products—to pass through the mold and escape to the atmosphere.
•   Reason/Defect Prevention: When hot metal is poured, a large volume of steam and gases is quickly
generated. High permeability is essential to vent these gases away from the cavity before they are
trapped in the solidifying metal.
•   Defect: Insufficient permeability leads directly to Blowholes, Gas Porosity, or Pinholes within the
final casting.
Strength, Cohesiveness, and Adhesiveness (3.0 Marks)
Q6   These three properties relate to the mechanical integrity and form stability of the mold:
CO-2;   •   Cohesiveness (Internal Strength): The ability of individual sand grains to stick together (clumping)
SO-1;   (a)   due to the clay binder. Without adequate cohesiveness, the mold walls would lack the necessary [10]
BL-3   internal strength to resist the turbulence and pressure of the molten metal stream.
•   Adhesiveness (External Strength): The ability of the sand mixture to stick to other bodies, particularly
the surface of the molding flask or the core box. This is necessary to maintain the shape of the mold
assembly before and during pouring.
•   Overall Strength: The resulting ability to maintain the mold cavity shape against mechanical handling
stresses and the ferrostatic pressure of the liquid metal.
•   Defect: Low strength, cohesiveness, or adhesiveness can cause Mold Erosion (Sand Wash) or Drops
(mold section collapse), resulting in sand inclusions in the casting.
Collapsibility (2.0 Marks)
•   Description: The crucial ability of the sand mold to readily break down or collapse easily after the
casting has solidified and is undergoing cooling shrinkage. This property is especially important for
surrounding cores.
•   Reason/Defect Prevention: During cooling, the cast metal shrinks volumetrically. If the mold or core
is too rigid, it restrains the metal from shrinking freely, inducing significant tensile stress in the hot,
weak metal.
•   Defect: Low collapsibility causes the formation of internal stresses and leads to major fracture defects
such as Hot Tears or Cracks in the casting.

Non-ferrous metals, particularly aluminum and magnesium, form a highly stable, refractory oxide layer
(e.g., aluminum oxide, Al2O3) on their surface. This oxide layer has a melting point much higher than the
base metal itself.
1. Oxide Barrier: If this oxide layer is not removed, the base metal underneath will melt and flow
while the oxide skin remains intact, preventing a proper fusion weld.
2. Cleaning Action: D.C. Welding allows for polarity control. When using DC Electrode Positive
(DCEP), a phenomenon called "cathodic cleaning" occurs. In this mode, the electron flow from the
work (anode) to the electrode (cathode) effectively bombards and disperses the surface oxide layer.
3. Process Suitability: Because D.C. Welding can provide the necessary cleaning action to break up
this refractory oxide, it is suitable for joining non-ferrous metals. In contrast, A.C. Welding often
lacks the stable, continuous cleaning action required for highly reactive metals (as noted in the
comparison, A.C. is generally stated as "Non-ferrous metals cannot be joined").

Welding Challenges Solved by Flux (3 Marks)
Flux primarily solves three critical challenges that cause weld defects:
1. Atmospheric Contamination (Shielding): When metal is molten, it readily reacts with Oxygen and
Nitrogen in the air, causing porosity and oxidation. Flux vaporizes and decomposes under the arc
heat, creating a dense gas shield that displaces the atmosphere.
2. Impurities (Refinement): The flux contains deoxidizing and cleansing agents. These agents
chemically react with impurities (like oxides and sulfur) in the molten pool, neutralizing them and
floating them to the top to become part of the slag.
3. Rapid Cooling (Slag Formation): The flux melts to form a protective layer of slag over the cooling
CO-3;   weld bead. This slag slows the cooling rate, preventing the formation of brittle microstructures and
SO-1;   (c)   further shielding the metal from the atmosphere as it solidifies.   [05]

Flux-based welding solutions are preferred when traditional gas shielding is impractical or insufficient:
1. Outdoor/Field Work: In windy or draughty conditions, a pure shielding gas (like Argon or CO2) is
easily blown away, leading to porosity. The solid, dense slag shield provided by flux is far more
robust and reliable against air movement.
2. Dirty/Rusty Metals: Flux processes (especially Shielded Metal Arc Welding - SMAW) can tolerate
slightly dirty, rusty, or painted surfaces better than Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW) or Gas
Metal Arc Welding (GMAW) because the flux has dedicated chemical cleansing agents built in.
3. Position Welding: Flux-based electrodes create a more viscous, controllable weld pool, making it
easier to weld in all positions (vertical, overhead) where gravity is working against the welder.

2. Secondary Motion (Feed Motion): The single-point cutting tool moves slowly along a straight path
relative to the workpiece axis.

workpiece.
2. Longitudinal Feed (Turning): The tool moves parallel to the axis of rotation (Z-axis), controlled by
