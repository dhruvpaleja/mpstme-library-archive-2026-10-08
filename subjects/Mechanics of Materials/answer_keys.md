Point A: It is observed from the stress-strain diagram that up to point A, the stress is directly
proportional to the strain giving a straight line OA. Thus, the point A is termed as the
proportional limit. The material remains elastic and linearity of stress-strain holds good up to

this point.

Point B: For a short span beyond point A, the material still behaves elastically up to the point
B. This point B is called the elastic limut. It is that point up to which the load is applied on the

body and upon releasing the load the body springs back to its original state.

Point C and Point D: Beyond elastic limit, point C is reached where the deformation in the

bar no longer remains elastic and plastic deformation initiates in the bar. This point is called

the upper yield point and corresponds to the maximum stress required to initiate yielding.
After that stress falls slightly to a point D, called the lower yield point. The lower yield point
D signifies the minimum stress required to maintain the yielding of the material. If the bar is
loaded beyond the yield point, the specimen does not come back to its original state upon
releasing the load. Some amount of deformation is permanently set within the bar. The stress

corresponding to the yield point is referred as the yield stress of the material.

Point E: lf we keep on applying load beyond yield point, without much increase in the load
we are going to have excessive deformation and we reach to a point E where the stress level
is the maximum, This point is called the ultimate stress point. The stress corresponding to the
ultimate stress point is the maximum stress that the specimen material can withstand without
failure and is termed as the ultimate strength or tensile strength of the material. Just after

point E, neck formation takes place.

Necking: As failure approaches, the material stretches very rapidly, causing the cross section

to narrow down, as shown in Fig. 1.18. This is called as necking of the tensile test specimen.

sectional area. One will observe that at a certain point F the specimen fails with a cup and
cone failure as shown in Fig. 1.19. This point F is called the fracture point. The value of the
