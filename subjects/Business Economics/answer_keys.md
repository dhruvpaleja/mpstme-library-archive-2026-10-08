a. Deflationary Gap: Meaning and Causes   12
Introduction
A deflationary gap occurs when the total demand in an economy is insufficient to purchase
the output produced at full employment. This concept is rooted in Keynesian economics
developed by John Maynard Keynes.
Definition
A deflationary gap is the shortfall of aggregate demand below the level required to achieve
full employment output.
Deflationary Gap = Full Employment Output − Actual Demand
Page 4 of 9
--- page 5 ---
Causes of Deflationary Gap
1. Decline in Consumption Demand
Fall in income or consumer confidence reduces spending.
2. Fall in Investment
Businesses reduce investment due to pessimistic profit expectations.
3. High Taxation
Reduces disposable income and purchasing power.
4. Reduction in Government Expenditure
Cuts in public spending reduce overall demand.
5. Increase in Savings
Excess saving reduces consumption demand.
6. Tight Monetary Policy
High interest rates discourage borrowing and spending.
Effects
Page 5 of 9
--- page 6 ---
Unemployment rises
Production declines
Income levels fall
Economy moves toward recession
Example
During an economic slowdown, reduced consumer spending and business investment lead to
unsold goods and job losses.
Conclusion
Thus, a deflationary gap reflects insufficient demand in the economy, resulting in
unemployment and reduced economic activity.

b. Introduction   12
Economic stability is a key macroeconomic objective, especially during periods of recession
marked by falling output, rising unemployment, and weak demand. Monetary policy plays a
vital role in stabilizing the economy by managing money supply and credit conditions. At the
same time, policymakers often refer to the Phillips Curve to understand the relationship
between inflation and unemployment, though its relevance has evolved in modern economies.
Limitations of the Phillips Curve in Modern Economies
The Phillips Curve suggests an inverse relationship between inflation and unemployment in
the short run. However, its applicability is limited today.
Key Limitations
• Breakdown in the Long Run
In the long run, inflation and unemployment may rise together due to expectations and
structural factors.
• Role of Inflation Expectations
Workers demand higher wages if they expect inflation, weakening the trade-off.
• Stagflation Experience
During the 1970s, many economies experienced high inflation and high unemployment
simultaneously, contradicting the Phillips Curve.
• Supply-Side Shocks
Oil price shocks and global disruptions can cause inflation without reducing unemployment.
• Structural Unemployment
Technological changes and skill mismatches create unemployment unrelated to inflation.
• Globalization & Labour Market Changes
Outsourcing, automation, and gig economy trends weaken wage–inflation dynamics.
Role of Monetary Policy in Achieving Economic Stability During Recession
Page 7 of 9
--- page 8 ---
During a recession, aggregate demand declines, leading to unemployment and reduced
production. The Reserve Bank of India uses expansionary (easy) monetary policy to stimulate
economic activity.
Measures Used
• Reduction in Repo Rate
Lower borrowing costs encourage businesses and consumers to take loans and increase
spending.
• Reduction in Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) & Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)
Banks have more funds available for lending, increasing liquidity in the economy.
• Open Market Operations (OMO)
Purchase of government securities injects money into the banking system.
• Lower Bank Rate & Reverse Repo Rate
Encourages banks to borrow more and lend more.
Effects on the Economy
✔ Encourages investment and consumption
✔ Increases employment opportunities
✔ Boosts production and income levels
✔ Restores business confidence
✔ Helps economic recovery
Example:
During economic slowdowns, central banks reduce interest rates to encourage borrowing and
spending.
Conclusion
While the Phillips Curve helps explain the short-run relationship between inflation and
unemployment, its limitations in modern economies—due to expectations, supply shocks, and
structural changes—mean policymakers must use it cautiously when designing stabilization
policies. Monetary policy plays a crucial role in stabilizing the economy during recessions by
increasing liquidity, reducing interest rates, and stimulating demand.

Under Perfect Competition, price is determined by the interaction of market demand and

Introduction
The concept of Effective Demand was developed by John Maynard Keynes to explain how

Meaning of Effective Demand
Effective demand is the level of demand at which Aggregate Demand (AD) equals Aggregate
Supply (AS).
It represents the point where entrepreneurs expect to receive maximum profits and therefore
decide the level of employment.
👉 Effective Demand occurs at AD = AS
Aggregate Demand (AD)
Aggregate demand refers to total expected spending on goods and services at different levels
of employment.
Components:
Consumption expenditure (C)
Investment expenditure (I)
When AD increases, firms expect higher sales and increase production.
Aggregate Supply (AS)
Aggregate supply refers to the total output firms are willing to produce at different levels of
employment.
Higher employment → higher output → higher costs.
Firms supply goods expecting adequate returns.
Determination of Effective Demand
Entrepreneurs compare expected sales proceeds (AD) with production costs (AS).
Employment level is fixed where AD equals AS.
This equilibrium may occur below full employment.
