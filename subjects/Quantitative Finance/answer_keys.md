a.   Explain the concept of the Efficient Frontier in portfolio theory. How does it help investors in
selecting optimal portfolios?   (5 marks)
Answer: The Efficient Frontier is a key concept in Modern Portfolio Theory developed by Harry
Markowitz. It represents the set of optimal portfolios that provide the highest expected return for a given
level of risk, or alternatively, the lowest risk for a given level of return. Portfolios that lie on the efficient
frontier are called efficient portfolios, while those below the frontier are considered inefficient because
they do not offer sufficient return for the level of risk taken. The efficient frontier is usually represented
as a curved line on a graph where risk (standard deviation) is plotted on the x-axis and expected return on
the y-axis. It helps investors in selecting an optimal portfolio based on their risk-return preferences,
allowing risk-averse investors to choose low-risk portfolios and risk-seeking investors to choose high-
return portfolios, thereby enabling rational and efficient investment decision-making.

4.   Explain how time period and rate of return influence investment choices.   (2 marks)
Answer: The time period and rate of return significantly influence investment decisions. A longer time

b.   Explain the difference between systematic risk and unsystematic risk. Describe the role of beta in
measuring risk and illustrate its application using an example.   (10 marks)
Answer: Systematic risk and unsystematic risk are the two main types of risks in financial markets.
Systematic risk refers to the risk that affects the entire market or economy and cannot be eliminated
through diversification. It arises from macroeconomic factors such as interest rate changes, inflation,
recessions, political instability, or global events. Since it impacts all securities, it is also called market
risk. In contrast, unsystematic risk is specific to a particular company or industry and can be reduced or
eliminated through diversification. It arises from factors such as management decisions, labor issues,
product failure, or industry-specific events.
Beta (β) is a measure of systematic risk in the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM). It indicates how
sensitive a stock’s returns are to changes in the overall market returns. A beta of 1 means the stock
moves exactly with the market, a beta greater than 1 indicates higher volatility than the market, and a
beta less than 1 indicates lower volatility. For example, if a stock has a beta of 1.2, it means that if the
market increases by 10%, the stock is expected to increase by 12%, and if the market falls by 10%, the
stock is expected to fall by 12%. Thus, beta helps investors assess the level of systematic risk and make
informed investment decisions based on their risk appetite.

b.   Explain the Binomial Option Pricing Model. Discuss its basic assumptions and how it is used to
price a European call option.   (10 marks)
Answer: The Binomial Option Pricing Model is a method used to value options by assuming that the
price of the underlying asset can move in only two possible directions over each time period: it can either
increase or decrease. By repeating this process over multiple time steps, a binomial tree is created showing
all possible future price paths of the asset. The model then calculates the value of the option by working
backwards from the final outcomes to the present value.

The basic assumptions of the model are that the market is free from taxes and transaction costs, there are
no arbitrage opportunities, investors can borrow and lend money at a constant risk-free interest rate, and
the stock price can only move up or down in each time period. It also assumes that trading happens in
discrete time intervals and the risk-free rate remains constant.
To price a European call option, the first step is to determine the possible future stock prices at maturity
using the given up and down movements. Then, the payoff of the option is calculated at each final price
by finding the difference between the stock price and the strike price, considering only positive values.
After that, risk-neutral probabilities are used to find the expected value of the option payoff. Finally, this
expected value is discounted back to the present using the risk-free interest rate to obtain the fair price of
the option today.

b.   Define and explain all the Greeks (Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega, Rho). Analyze how each Greek
helps in measuring risk in an option portfolio.   (10 marks)
Answer: The Greeks are risk sensitivity measures used in option pricing to understand how the value of
an option changes with respect to different market variables. They are widely used in risk management
and hedging of option portfolios.

Delta (Δ) measures the sensitivity of the option price to changes in the price of the underlying asset. It
shows how much the option price will change when the stock price changes by one unit. For example, a
Delta of 0.6 means the option price will increase by 0.6 when the stock price increases by 1. Delta helps
investors understand directional risk and is used for hedging by creating a delta-neutral portfolio.
Gamma (Γ) measures the rate of change of Delta with respect to changes in the underlying asset price. It
indicates how stable Delta is when the stock price moves. High Gamma means Delta changes rapidly,
increasing hedging risk. Gamma is important for managing the accuracy of Delta hedging.
Theta (Θ) measures the sensitivity of the option price to time decay. It shows how much value the
option loses as time passes, assuming other factors remain constant. Since options lose value as expiry
approaches, Theta is usually negative for long positions. It helps investors understand time-related risk.
Vega (ν) measures sensitivity of the option price to changes in volatility of the underlying asset. Higher
volatility increases the value of both call and put options. Vega helps measure uncertainty risk, and
traders use it to manage exposure to market volatility changes.
Rho (ρ) measures sensitivity of the option price to changes in interest rates. It shows how the option
value changes when risk-free rates change. Call options generally have positive Rho, while put options
have negative Rho. It helps assess interest rate risk in long-term options.
Overall, the Greeks help investors break down option risk into different components such as price
movement, time decay, volatility, and interest rates, enabling effective hedging and portfolio risk
management.

b.   Define Probability of Default (PD) and Loss Given Default (LGD) models. Explain their
importance in credit risk management.   (10 marks)
Answer: Probability of Default (PD) and Loss Given Default (LGD) are two fundamental components
used in credit risk modelling to measure and manage the risk of lending.
Probability of Default (PD) refers to the likelihood that a borrower will fail to meet their debt obligations
within a specified time period, usually one year. It is expressed as a percentage or probability between 0
and 1. A higher PD indicates a higher chance that the borrower will default, reflecting lower
creditworthiness.
Loss Given Default (LGD) refers to the proportion of the exposure that a lender is expected to lose if a
borrower defaults. It depends on the recovery rate from collateral, legal recovery processes, and asset
quality. It is usually expressed as a percentage of total exposure. For example, if LGD is 40 percent, it
means 40 percent of the loan amount is expected to be lost after recovery.
In credit risk management, PD and LGD are crucial because they help quantify expected credit losses.
They are combined with Exposure at Default (EAD) to compute Expected Loss using the relationship:
Expected Loss equals PD multiplied by LGD multiplied by EAD. This framework helps banks assess
borrower risk, set appropriate interest rates, allocate capital efficiently, and comply with regulatory
requirements such as Basel norms. PD helps in identifying the likelihood of default, while LGD measures
the severity of loss, making them essential tools for informed lending decisions and effective risk control.
Q6. Answer the followings:   (20 marks)
a.   Discuss the Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA) used in operational risk under Basel
framework. Analyze its key components and explain how it improves capital estimation compared to
simpler approaches.   (10 marks)
Answer: The Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA) is a sophisticated method used under the Basel
regulatory framework to measure and quantify operational risk in banks. It allows financial institutions to
use internal models to estimate the amount of capital required to cover potential losses arising from
operational risk events such as fraud, system failures, legal risks, and process errors.
The key components of AMA include internal loss data, external loss data, scenario analysis, and business
environment and internal control factors. Internal loss data refers to historical loss events within the bank,

--- page 7 ---
which help in understanding past operational risk exposure. External loss data is collected from other
institutions and industry databases to capture rare but severe loss events that may not be present in internal
records. Scenario analysis involves expert judgment to estimate potential extreme losses under
hypothetical but plausible risk situations. Business environment and internal control factors assess the
quality of internal processes and risk control systems that influence operational risk exposure.
AMA improves capital estimation compared to simpler approaches such as the Basic Indicator Approach
and Standardized Approach because it is more risk-sensitive and institution-specific. While simpler
methods use fixed percentages of income or business lines, AMA uses actual loss data and advanced
statistical modelling, such as loss distribution approaches, to estimate both frequency and severity of
losses. This leads to more accurate and customized capital requirements, better reflecting the true risk
profile of a bank. As a result, AMA enhances risk management, improves capital allocation efficiency,
and encourages stronger internal risk control systems.
b.   Explain the concept of Value at Risk (VaR). How is VaR used in risk management to measure
potential losses in a portfolio?   (10 marks)
Answer: Value at Risk (VaR) is a statistical risk measure used to estimate the maximum potential loss
in the value of a portfolio over a specified time period, under normal market conditions, and at a given
confidence level. It answers the question: “How much can we lose in the worst expected case over a
given time horizon?”
VaR is commonly expressed in monetary terms or percentage terms. For example, a one-day VaR of ₹1
lakh at 95% confidence level means that there is a 95% probability that the portfolio will not lose more
than ₹1 lakh in one day, and a 5% probability that losses could exceed this amount.
VaR can be calculated using different methods such as the variance-covariance method, historical
simulation, and Monte Carlo simulation. The variance-covariance method assumes normal distribution
of returns, historical simulation uses past data to estimate future risk, and Monte Carlo simulation uses
repeated random sampling to model possible outcomes.
In risk management, VaR is widely used by banks, financial institutions, and portfolio managers to
measure and control market risk. It helps in setting risk limits, allocating capital, and comparing risk
across different portfolios. VaR also supports regulatory compliance under Basel norms by providing a
standardized measure of potential losses. However, it does not capture extreme losses beyond the
confidence level, so it is often supplemented with other risk measures like Expected Shortfall.

b.   Write short notes on the followings:   (10 marks)
1.   Options
2.   Coupon rate
3.   Sharpe ratio
4.   Forwards
Answer: 1. Options
Options are financial derivative contracts that give the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or
sell an underlying asset at a predetermined price within or on a specified date. A call option gives the
right to buy, while a put option gives the right to sell. Options are widely used for hedging risk,
speculation, and arbitrage.
2. Coupon rate
Coupon rate is the fixed annual interest rate paid on a bond by the issuer, expressed as a percentage of
the bond’s face value. It determines the periodic interest payments received by bondholders until
maturity. For example, a coupon rate of 8 percent on a bond with face value of ₹1,000 pays ₹80
annually.
3. Sharpe ratio
Sharpe ratio is a measure of risk-adjusted return of an investment. It shows how much excess return is
earned for each unit of risk taken. It is calculated as (portfolio return minus risk-free rate) divided by
standard deviation of returns. A higher Sharpe ratio indicates better risk-adjusted performance.
4. Forwards
Forwards are customized derivative contracts where two parties agree to buy or sell an asset at a
predetermined price on a future date. Unlike futures, forward contracts are traded over-the-counter and
are not standardized. They are commonly used for hedging price risk in commodities, currencies, and
interest rates.

systematic risk, which affects the entire market, cannot be eliminated through diversification.
Covariance plays a crucial role in portfolio construction because it measures how the returns of two assets
move in relation to each other. If the covariance is low or negative, the assets tend to move differently or
in opposite directions, which enhances diversification benefits and reduces overall portfolio risk. If

b.   Explain the concept of the efficient frontier in portfolio theory. Discuss how it is constructed and
analyze its significance in helping investors choose optimal portfolios under different risk-return
preferences.   (10 marks)
Answer: The efficient frontier is a fundamental concept in Markowitz Portfolio Theory that represents
the set of optimal portfolios offering the highest expected return for a given level of risk, or the lowest
risk for a given level of expected return. These portfolios are considered efficient because no other
portfolio can provide a better risk-return combination. Any portfolio that lies below the efficient frontier
is considered suboptimal since it either offers lower returns for the same level of risk or higher risk for
the same level of return.
The construction of the efficient frontier involves combining different risky assets in various
proportions and calculating the expected return and risk (measured by standard deviation) for each
possible portfolio. By plotting these combinations on a graph with risk on the horizontal axis and return
on the vertical axis, a curve is formed. The upper portion of this curve, which shows the maximum
return achievable for each level of risk, is known as the efficient frontier. This process requires
estimates of expected returns, variances of individual assets, and covariances between assets, since the
interaction between assets affects overall portfolio risk.
The efficient frontier plays a crucial role in helping investors make informed decisions based on their
individual risk preferences. Risk-averse investors prefer portfolios with lower risk, so they select
portfolios on the lower end of the efficient frontier. Investors who are willing to take on more risk in
exchange for higher returns choose portfolios located toward the upper end of the frontier. By
identifying the efficient set of portfolios, investors can avoid inefficient combinations and focus only on
those that provide the best possible trade-off between risk and return. In practice, the efficient frontier is
often combined with a risk-free asset to form the Capital Allocation Line, which helps investors further
refine their choices based on their utility and risk tolerance.

b.   Explain the Binomial Pricing Model and the Binomial Lattice Model. Discuss their significance in
the valuation of options and other financial derivatives.   (10 marks)
Answer: The Binomial Pricing Model is a discrete-time method used to value options by modeling the
possible future movements of the underlying asset price over time. In this model, the price of the

--- page 6 ---
underlying asset is assumed to move to one of two possible values over each small time step: an upward
movement or a downward movement. By constructing a series of these up and down movements over
multiple time steps, a binomial tree is formed that represents all possible price paths of the asset. At each
final node of the tree (at maturity), the payoff of the option is calculated, and then the valuation proceeds
backward through the tree using a risk-neutral probability approach and discounting at the risk-free rate
to determine the present value of the option.
The Binomial Lattice Model is essentially an extension of the binomial framework where the asset price
evolution is represented as a lattice (tree structure) over multiple time periods. At each node in the lattice,
the price can move up or down, creating a branching structure that captures all possible future price
scenarios. The model assumes no arbitrage and uses risk-neutral valuation, meaning expected returns are
adjusted to the risk-free rate rather than the actual expected return of the asset. By working backward
from the terminal nodes, the model computes option values at each node until reaching the present value
at the initial node. This approach is flexible and can be adapted for different types of options, including
American options, which can be exercised at any time before expiration.
The significance of the binomial pricing and lattice models lies in their flexibility and intuitive structure.
They are particularly useful for valuing options where analytical solutions, such as the Black-Scholes
model, may be difficult to apply, especially for American options or options with complex features like
early exercise, dividends, or path dependency. The binomial model can approximate continuous-time
models by increasing the number of time steps, making it highly accurate. Additionally, it provides a clear
visual and computational framework that helps in understanding how option values evolve over time and
how different factors such as volatility, time to maturity, and interest rates influence option pricing.

b.   Discuss the Black-Scholes Model and its significance in financial markets. Explain the key
components and the formula used in the model.   (10 mark s)
Answer: The Black-Scholes Model is a mathematical framework used to determine the theoretical price
of European-style options. It assumes that financial markets are efficient and that the price of the
underlying asset follows a continuous stochastic process with constant volatility and a lognormal
distribution of returns. The model provides a closed-form solution for pricing call and put options,
making it one of the most widely used tools in derivatives pricing.
The significance of the Black-Scholes Model in financial markets lies in its ability to provide a
standardized and theoretically grounded method for option valuation. It helps traders, investors, and
financial institutions price options consistently, manage risk, and identify arbitrage opportunities. The
model also plays a crucial role in hedging strategies, as it forms the basis for the concept of delta
hedging, where positions are adjusted to remain neutral to small price movements in the underlying
asset. Additionally, it has contributed significantly to the development of modern financial engineering
and risk management practices.
The key components of the Black-Scholes Model include the current price of the underlying asset, the
strike price of the option, the time to maturity, the risk-free interest rate, and the volatility of the
underlying asset’s returns. Volatility is particularly important as it measures the uncertainty or risk
associated with the asset’s price movements. The model also assumes that there are no transaction costs,
no dividends (in its basic form), and that markets allow continuous trading.
The Black-Scholes formula for a European call option is expressed as the current stock price multiplied
by the cumulative standard normal distribution of a term minus the present value of the strike price
multiplied by the cumulative standard normal distribution of another term. These terms depend on
variables such as the risk-free rate, volatility, time to maturity, and the ratio of the stock price to the
strike price. For a European put option, a similar formula is used, or it can be derived using put-call
parity.
Overall, the Black-Scholes Model is significant because it provides a theoretical benchmark for option
pricing, improves market efficiency, and serves as a foundation for more advanced pricing models.

b.   Explain how key components such as Probability of Default (PD), Loss Given Default (LGD),
and Exposure at Default (EAD) are used in assessing credit risk.   (10 marks)
Answer: Probability of Default (PD), Loss Given Default (LGD), and Exposure at Default (EAD) are
the three fundamental components used in credit risk assessment to quantify the potential loss a lender
may face if a borrower defaults. Together, they form the basis for measuring expected credit loss and
are widely used by financial institutions for risk management, pricing, and regulatory capital
calculations.
Probability of Default (PD) represents the likelihood that a borrower will default on their obligations
within a specified time horizon, usually one year. It reflects the creditworthiness of the borrower and is
estimated using credit scores, historical default data, financial ratios, and macroeconomic factors. A
higher PD indicates a higher chance of default and therefore higher credit risk.
Loss Given Default (LGD) measures the proportion of the exposure that will not be recovered in the
event of default. It depends on factors such as collateral, seniority of the loan, recovery processes, and
legal environment. For example, a secured loan with strong collateral will generally have a lower LGD
compared to an unsecured loan, because a larger portion of the exposure can be recovered.
Exposure at Default (EAD) refers to the total value exposed to risk at the time of default. It includes the
outstanding loan amount as well as any additional undrawn commitments that the borrower may utilize
before default. EAD helps in estimating the size of the potential loss.

as the product of PD, LGD, and EAD. Expected Loss represents the average loss a lender anticipates

framework to assess borrower risk, set interest rates, determine loan approvals, allocate capital, and

The Operational Risk Management (ORM) Framework is a structured approach used by financial

operational activities and is an essential part of overall enterprise risk management.
The first stage is identification, where an organization recognizes potential sources of operational risk
across its processes, systems, and activities. This can be done through methods such as risk and control
self-assessments, internal audits, incident reporting, and process mapping. For example, a bank may
identify risks such as system downtime in online banking, data entry errors in loan processing, or risks of
fraud in payment systems.
The second stage is assessment, where identified risks are evaluated in terms of their likelihood of
occurrence and the potential impact if they occur. This is often done using qualitative tools such as risk
matrices that classify risks into categories like low, medium, or high. For instance, a cyberattack may
have a low probability but a very high impact, whereas minor clerical errors may occur frequently but
have a low impact.

b.   Answer the followings:

2. Explain why the industry is moving from Value-at-risk (VaR) to Expected Shortfall (ES)?
(5 marks)
Answer: 1. Expected Shortfall (ES) and Coherent Risk Measure
Expected Shortfall (ES), also known as Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR), is a risk measure that
represents the average loss of a portfolio in the worst-case scenarios beyond a specified confidence
level. In simple terms, it measures the expected loss given that losses have exceeded the Value at Risk
(VaR) threshold. Unlike VaR, which only identifies a cutoff point, ES provides information about the
severity of losses in the tail of the distribution. Expected Shortfall is considered a coherent risk measure
because it satisfies all four properties of coherence: monotonicity, sub-additivity, positive homogeneity,
and translation invariance. In particular, it satisfies sub-additivity, meaning the risk of a combined
portfolio is not greater than the sum of individual risks, which makes it consistent with the principle of
diversification.
2. Why the Industry is Moving from VaR to ES
The financial industry is shifting from Value at Risk (VaR) to Expected Shortfall (ES) because ES
provides a more comprehensive and reliable measure of tail risk. VaR only indicates the maximum loss
that will not be exceeded at a given confidence level, but it does not provide any information about the
magnitude of losses beyond that threshold. In contrast, ES takes into account all losses beyond the VaR
level and gives their average, making it more informative about extreme events. Additionally, VaR is

--- page 11 ---
not a coherent risk measure as it can violate the sub-additivity property, which may lead to
underestimation of risk in diversified portfolios. ES, on the other hand, is coherent and better aligned
with diversification principles. Due to these advantages, regulatory frameworks such as Basel III and
the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) have adopted ES for market risk measurement,
encouraging its widespread use in the industry.

Answer: 1. Primary and Secondary Markets
The primary market is the segment of the financial market where new securities are issued and sold for
the first time. In this market, companies raise fresh capital directly from investors through instruments
such as initial public offerings or bond issuances. The funds raised go to the issuing company. In
contrast, the secondary market is where already issued securities are traded among investors without the
involvement of the issuing company. Stock exchanges facilitate these transactions by providing
liquidity, price discovery, and transparency. The primary market helps in capital formation, while the
secondary market ensures liquidity and enables investors to buy and sell securities easily.
2. Forwards
Forwards are customized financial contracts between two parties to buy or sell an asset at a specified
price on a future date. These contracts are traded over-the-counter and are not standardized, allowing
flexibility in terms of contract size, maturity, and settlement terms. Forwards carry counterparty risk
because there is no central clearing mechanism, meaning one party may default on its obligation. They
are commonly used for hedging purposes, such as locking in exchange rates in foreign currency
transactions or stabilizing future prices of commodities.
3. Futures

Futures are standardized contracts similar to forwards, but they are traded on organized exchanges.
These contracts obligate the buyer and seller to transact an asset at a predetermined price on a future
date. Futures are marked to market daily, meaning gains and losses are settled each day through a
clearinghouse, which significantly reduces counterparty risk. Margin requirements are also imposed to
ensure contract performance. Futures are widely used for hedging, speculation, and arbitrage in markets
such as commodities, currencies, and financial indices.
4. Options
Options are derivative contracts that give the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an
underlying asset at a specified price before or on a certain date. A call option provides the right to buy,
while a put option provides the right to sell. The buyer of the option pays a premium to the seller for
this right. Options are useful for hedging risk, generating income, and speculative strategies. Unlike
forwards and futures, options do not create an obligation for the holder, which limits downside risk to
the premium paid while allowing participation in favorable price movements.
