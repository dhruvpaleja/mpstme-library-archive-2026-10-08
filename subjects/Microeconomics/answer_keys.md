In this scenario, the pharmaceutical company operates as the sole provider
of a unique product, shielded by legal protections. Below is a detailed
justification of this identification based on the specific features mentioned
in the case.
Justification of Monopoly Market Structure
1. Single Seller and Industry Identity
The case states that the firm is the "sole producer in the market." In a
monopoly, there is no distinction between the firm and the industry.
Because this company is the only one manufacturing the drug for this rare
disease, it has total control over the market supply, allowing it to dictate
terms without fearing immediate competition.
2. Lack of Close Substitutes
The drug is used to treat a "rare disease" and "no close substitutes are
available." This is a defining feature of a monopoly. If a substitute existed,
consumers would switch to a different brand when the price increased.
Here, the cross-elasticity of demand is zero or near-zero, meaning the
firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve that is relatively inelastic
(since the drug is life-saving).
3. Strong Barriers to Entry (Patents)
The firm "holds a patent" for the drug. This is a legal barrier to entry
created by the government. Patents prevent other companies from legally
replicating the chemical formula for a set number of years. Without the
ability to enter the market, competitors cannot erode the firm’s profits,
allowing the monopoly to persist even when charging high prices.
4. Price Maker Status
The company has "recently increased the price of the drug despite
criticism." Unlike a perfectly competitive firm (which is a price taker), a
monopolist is a price maker. It can choose the price level that maximizes
its profits. The firm’s management justifies this by citing the need to
recover "research and development (R&D) costs," which is a common
characteristic of monopolistic firms in high-tech or medical sectors.
5. Profit Maximization in the Long Run
Because of the patent protection, the firm can maintain supernormal
profits in the long run. In other market structures, high profits would
attract new entrants, but the patent barrier ensures this firm remains the
only beneficiary of the high prices it sets to fund "future innovations."

Apply   A perfectly competitive market and an oligopoly market represent two
contrasting market structures in microeconomics. They differ fundamentally in
terms of the number of firms, nature of products, pricing power, entry barriers,
and strategic behavior. Understanding these differences is crucial for analyzing
real-world markets and business strategies.
Perfectly Competitive Market
A perfectly competitive market is an idealized market structure characterized by
a large number of small firms, homogeneous products, and free entry and exit.
No single firm has the power to influence the market price.
Key Features
a) Large Number of Buyers and Sellers
There are numerous firms and consumers in the market. Each firm produces
only a small portion of total output, so its individual actions do not affect the
overall market.
b) Homogeneous Products
All firms sell identical products. Consumers perceive no difference between
products offered by different sellers.
********** 6 **********
--- page 7 ---
c) Price Taker
Firms are price takers, meaning they accept the market-determined price. The
equilibrium price is set by the interaction of industry-wide demand and supply.
d) Free Entry and Exit
There are no significant barriers to entering or exiting the market. This ensures
that firms earn only normal profits in the long run.
e) Perfect Information
All buyers and sellers have complete knowledge about prices, products, and
production methods.
f) No Selling Costs
Since products are identical, firms do not spend on advertising or marketing.
Oligopoly Market
An oligopoly market is a market structure dominated by a few large firms. Each
firm has significant market power and must consider competitors’ actions when
making decisions.
Key Features
a) Few Firms
Only a small number of firms dominate the market. Each firm holds a
substantial share.
b) Interdependence
Firms are highly interdependent. The decision of one firm affects others.
c) Barriers to Entry
There are significant barriers such as:
• High capital requirements
• Brand loyalty
• Government regulations
•   Patents and technology
d) Product Differentiation
Products may be:
• Homogeneous (e.g., steel)
•   Differentiated (e.g., cars, telecom services)

when the stock of money increases. This is because money is a medium of
exchange which is used to satisfy various wants. However, according to
some economists, this law is applicable to money too. For example,
marginal utility of money is more to a poor person than to a rich person.

The Kinked Demand Curve (dRD')
The demand curve is characterized by a "kink" at the prevailing market price
P1 (Point R) because of how competitors react to price changes:
Price Increases (Segment dR): If a firm raises its price above P1, it
assumes rivals will not follow. Consumers will switch to the now-cheaper
competitors, making the demand highly elastic (flatter). A small price
increase leads to a large loss in sales.
Price Decreases (Segment RD'): If a firm lowers its price below P1, it
assumes rivals will immediately match the cut to prevent losing their
market share. Because everyone is now cheaper, the firm gains very few
new customers, making the demand inelastic (steeper).
2. The Discontinuous Marginal Revenue (MR) Curve
The sharp change in the elasticity of demand at the kink results in a unique
MR curve that is broken into two separate parts:
The Upper Segment (D-M): This corresponds to the elastic portion of the
demand curve (dR).
