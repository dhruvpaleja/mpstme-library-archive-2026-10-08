Marketing Objectives and Strategies for New Product Pioneers
The strategy used by Dyson in this case is the Skimming Strategy (Market Skimming). Market   [10]
skimming occurs when a company introduces an innovative product at a high initial price to
target early adopters who are willing to pay a premium for new technology and superior
features. The goal is to recover research and development costs quickly and maximize profits
from customers who value innovation and quality.
In the case of Dyson’s Dyson Supersonic:
• The product was launched with a significantly higher price than traditional hair
dryers.
•   It targeted premium customers and professional hairstylists, who value advanced
performance and technology.
• The product emphasizes unique technological innovation and superior design,
attracting early adopters willing to pay more.
Therefore, Dyson’s approach clearly reflects a Skimming Strategy, where the firm charges a
high price initially to capture value from customers who are less sensitive to price and more
interested in innovation.

Marking scheme- 2 marks per point stated.
How should managers decide when the time has come to restructure an organization, and
what new structure should replace the old one?
There are five key drivers with such decisions:
(1) customer needs,
(2) informational requirements of the sales and marketing personnel charged with
meeting those needs,
(3) ability of a given structure to motivate and coordinate the kinds of activities that
market conditions require,
(4) available competencies and resources, and
(5) costs.
1. Changing Customer Needs
Managers should assess whether the existing organizational structure allows the company
to respond effectively to evolving customer preferences. In Unilever’s case, consumers
increasingly demand personalized products, innovation, and consistent global brand
experiences. If the current structure makes it difficult to understand or respond to these
needs, restructuring may be necessary.
2. Informational Requirements of Sales and Marketing Personnel
Sales and marketing teams need timely and accurate market data, customer insights, and
competitive information to make effective decisions. If the current structure limits
information flow between regions or departments, managers may need to restructure to
improve communication, coordination, and knowledge sharing.
3. Ability of the Structure to Motivate and Coordinate Activities
Managers must consider whether the existing structure effectively motivates employees
and coordinates marketing and sales activities across markets. For example, regional
divisions at Unilever may sometimes duplicate efforts or slow down decision-making. A
new structure might improve collaboration and efficiency.
4. Available Competencies and Resources
Managers should evaluate the company’s internal capabilities, digital marketing
expertise, supply chain strengths, and technological resources. If the firm has developed
new competencies such as advanced digital marketing capabilities, the structure should
be adjusted to fully utilize these resources.
5. Costs and Operational Efficiency
Finally, managers must consider whether the current structure leads to high operational
costs, duplication of work, or inefficient use of resources. If restructuring can reduce
costs and improve efficiency while maintaining effectiveness, it may be the appropriate
strategic decision.

How should managers decide when the time has come to restructure an organization, and
what new structure should replace the old one?
There are five key drivers with such decisions:
(1) customer needs,
(2) informational requirements of the sales and marketing personnel charged with
meeting those needs,
(3) ability of a given structure to motivate and coordinate the kinds of activities that
market conditions require,
(4) available competencies and resources, and
(5) costs.
1. Changing Customer Needs
Managers should assess whether the existing organizational structure allows the company
to respond effectively to evolving customer preferences. In Unilever’s case, consumers
increasingly demand personalized products, innovation, and consistent global brand
experiences. If the current structure makes it difficult to understand or respond to these
needs, restructuring may be necessary.
2. Informational Requirements of Sales and Marketing Personnel
Sales and marketing teams need timely and accurate market data, customer insights, and
competitive information to make effective decisions. If the current structure limits
information flow between regions or departments, managers may need to restructure to
improve communication, coordination, and knowledge sharing.
3. Ability of the Structure to Motivate and Coordinate Activities
Managers must consider whether the existing structure effectively motivates employees
and coordinates marketing and sales activities across markets. For example, regional
divisions at Unilever may sometimes duplicate efforts or slow down decision-making. A
new structure might improve collaboration and efficiency.
4. Available Competencies and Resources
Managers should evaluate the company’s internal capabilities, digital marketing
expertise, supply chain strengths, and technological resources. If the firm has developed
new competencies such as advanced digital marketing capabilities, the structure should
be adjusted to fully utilize these resources.
5. Costs and Operational Efficiency
Finally, managers must consider whether the current structure leads to high operational
costs, duplication of work, or inefficient use of resources. If restructuring can reduce
costs and improve efficiency while maintaining effectiveness, it may be the appropriate
strategic decision.

How should managers decide when the time has come to restructure an organization, and
what new structure should replace the old one?
There are five key drivers with such decisions:
(1) customer needs,
(2) informational requirements of the sales and marketing personnel charged with
meeting those needs,
(3) ability of a given structure to motivate and coordinate the kinds of activities that
market conditions require,
(4) available competencies and resources, and
(5) costs.
1. Changing Customer Needs
Managers should assess whether the existing organizational structure allows the company
to respond effectively to evolving customer preferences. In Unilever’s case, consumers
increasingly demand personalized products, innovation, and consistent global brand
experiences. If the current structure makes it difficult to understand or respond to these
needs, restructuring may be necessary.
2. Informational Requirements of Sales and Marketing Personnel
Sales and marketing teams need timely and accurate market data, customer insights, and
competitive information to make effective decisions. If the current structure limits
information flow between regions or departments, managers may need to restructure to
improve communication, coordination, and knowledge sharing.
3. Ability of the Structure to Motivate and Coordinate Activities
Managers must consider whether the existing structure effectively motivates employees
and coordinates marketing and sales activities across markets. For example, regional
divisions at Unilever may sometimes duplicate efforts or slow down decision-making. A
new structure might improve collaboration and efficiency.
4. Available Competencies and Resources
Managers should evaluate the company’s internal capabilities, digital marketing
expertise, supply chain strengths, and technological resources. If the firm has developed
new competencies such as advanced digital marketing capabilities, the structure should
be adjusted to fully utilize these resources.
5. Costs and Operational Efficiency
Finally, managers must consider whether the current structure leads to high operational
costs, duplication of work, or inefficient use of resources. If restructuring can reduce
costs and improve efficiency while maintaining effectiveness, it may be the appropriate
strategic decision.
