Concept of Standard Business Model
A Standard Business Model refers to a well-established and commonly used framework that
defines how a business creates, delivers, and captures value. It is “standard” because it follows
a tested and widely accepted structure that many firms adopt successfully across industries.
In simple terms:
It is a time-tested way of doing business where revenue generation, cost structure, and
customer interaction are clearly defined.
Companies using a standard business model usually face less uncertainty because the model
has been proven effective.
Key Features of a Standard Business Model
Page 1 of 14
--- page 2 ---
1. Predictable Structure – Follows established patterns (e.g., manufacturer–retailer–
consumer).
2. Clear Revenue Streams – Profit earned through known sources like sales, subscriptions, or
commission.
3. Low Risk – Since it is tried and tested, the chances of failure are relatively lower.
4. Replicable – Can be used in different industries with minor modifications.
5. Customer Orientation – Focus on satisfying customer needs in a proven way.
Examples of Standard Business Models
1. Manufacturing Model: Businesses produce goods and sell them to wholesalers, retailers, or
directly to consumers. Example: Maruti Suzuki manufactures cars and sells them through a
dealer network.
2. Retail Model: Retailers purchase goods from manufacturers/wholesalers and sell them at a
margin to consumers. Example: Reliance Fresh and Big Bazaar operate on this model.
3. Franchise Model: A parent company (franchisor) allows others (franchisees) to use its
brand, products, and processes in exchange for
fees/royalty. Example: McDonald’s and Domino’s Pizza follow the franchise model.
4.   Subscription Model: Customers pay a regular fee (monthly/annual) to access a product or
service. Example: Netflix and Amazon Prime.
5.   Commission-Based Model: The business earns a commission by connecting buyers and
sellers. It acts as an intermediary and charges a fee on every transaction made through its
platform.Example: OLX and Justdial earn by listing sellers and service providers.
6.   Advertising Business Model: The business provides free or low-cost products/services to
attract a large user base and earns revenue by selling advertising space to third parties who
want to reach that audience. Example: Google and Facebook provide free services but generate
income through targeted advertisements.
7.   Auction Business Model: The business earns revenue by allowing sellers to auction their
products, and buyers bid against each other to purchase them. The platform earns by charging
listing fees or taking a percentage of the final sale price. Example: eBay is a classic platform
where products are sold through online auctions.
8. Bricks and Clicks Model: The business combines offline (physical stores) and online (e-
commerce websites/apps) operations to reach more customers and maximize
convenience. Example: Reliance Retail and Tata’s Croma operate physical outlets along with
online shopping platforms.

9. Freemium Business Model: The business offers basic services for free to attract users,
while advanced or premium features are available at a cost. This model builds a large user base
first and then converts a portion into paying customers. Example: Spotify and LinkedIn
provide free basic access but charge for premium features.
10. Peer-to-Peer (P2P) Business Model: The business directly connects individuals who want
to share, sell, or exchange products and services, usually through an online platform, without
involving traditional intermediaries. Example: Airbnb connects homeowners with travelers,
and Uber connects drivers with passengers.

printers at low prices but makes money from ink cartridges.

1. Vision – Definition
A Vision is a future-oriented statement that describes what an organization aspires to become
or achieve in the long run.
It reflects the dream, aspirations, and ultimate goal of the enterprise.
It answers the question: “Where do we want to be in the future?”
Example:
Google’s vision: “To provide access to the world’s information in one click.”
Tata Group’s vision: “To be the most admired and responsible integrated power company
with international footprint.”
2. Mission – Definition
A Mission is a present-oriented statement that defines the purpose, scope, and core activities of
a business.
It reflects what the company does, who it serves, and how it creates value.
It answers the question: “What is our purpose and how do we operate today?”
Example:
Google’s mission: “To organize the world’s information and make it universally accessible
and useful.”

3. How Vision and Mission Guide Business Direction

Together, they ensure that every decision and strategy aligns with the desired future.
Example: Tesla’s vision of “accelerating the world’s transition to sustainable energy” guides

1. Definition of SWOT Analysis
SWOT Analysis is a strategic tool used by businesses to evaluate their internal strengths
and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats.
S = Strengths: Internal factors that give the business a competitive advantage.
W = Weaknesses: Internal limitations or areas where the business is lacking.
O = Opportunities: External factors that the business can exploit for growth.
T = Threats: External challenges or risks that could harm the business.
In simple terms: SWOT helps a business understand what it does well, what it lacks, where
it can grow, and what risks it faces.
Page 6 of 14
--- page 7 ---
2. Role of SWOT Analysis in Assessing Business Opportunities and Threats
A. Identifying Opportunities
By analyzing external factors, SWOT helps businesses spot potential markets, trends, or
unmet needs that can be capitalized on.
Example:
Flipkart identified the growing internet penetration in India as an opportunity to
expand online retail.
Tesla recognized the rising global demand for eco-friendly vehicles as a business
opportunity.
B. Identifying Threats
SWOT highlights external risks that could negatively impact the business, allowing
managers to take preventive actions.
Example:
Ola and Uber faced threats from government regulations on ride-sharing.
Small manufacturers face threats from large multinational competitors entering the
market.
C. Linking Internal Strengths to Opportunities
Businesses can leverage their strengths to exploit opportunities effectively.
Example: Reliance Jio used its financial strength (internal) to expand 4G services rapidly,
exploiting the opportunity in India’s digital market.
D. Mitigating Threats and Weaknesses
Identifying weaknesses and threats helps in developing strategies to overcome challenges.
Example: A company with weak online presence (internal weakness) can adopt digital
marketing strategies to counter competition (external threat).
3. Benefits of SWOT Analysis in Strategic Planning
1. Provides a comprehensive overview of internal and external factors.
2. Helps in setting realistic and achievable objectives.
3. Facilitates informed decision-making.

More affordable,

Meaning of Hierarchy of Objectives
The Hierarchy of Objectives refers to the structured arrangement of goals in an organization,
starting from the broad vision and mission at the top, down to departmental, team, and
individual objectives at the bottom.
It ensures that every small objective supports higher-level goals, creating alignment
throughout the business.
In simple words:
Corporate (overall) objectives → Functional (departmental) objectives → Operational
(day-to-day) objectives.
Levels in the Hierarchy of Objectives
1. Vision and Mission (Top Level)
Broad, long-term aspirations and purpose of the organization.
Example: “To be the market leader in electric vehicles globally.”
2. Corporate Objectives
Derived from vision/mission; focus on growth, profitability, and market leadership.
Example: “Achieve 20% market share in the EV segment within 5 years.”
3. Functional Objectives (Department Level)
Page 3 of 17
--- page 4 ---
Set for different functions like marketing, production, HR, finance.
Example:
Marketing Objective: “Increase brand awareness by 30% through digital campaigns.”
Production Objective: “Reduce manufacturing costs by 10% through automation.”
4. Business Unit or Divisional Objectives
Specific to business units/divisions within large corporations.
Example: Tata Motors (Passenger Vehicles Division): “Launch 3 new EV models in the
next 2 years.”
5. Operational / Individual Objectives (Bottom Level)
Short-term, day-to-day goals for teams and employees.
Example:
Sales team target: “Sell 1,000 EV units this quarter.”
HR target: “Recruit 200 engineers for the EV division in 6 months.”

Definition of Business Environment
The business environment refers to the sum total of all internal and external factors,
forces, and conditions that affect a business’s operations, decisions, and performance.
Page 7 of 17
--- page 8 ---
It includes economic, social, political, technological, legal, and cultural factors.
Example: Government policies, consumer preferences, competitor actions, and
technological changes form part of a company’s business environment.
In short: Business environment = All external and internal influences that affect how a
business operates.
Key Characteristics of Business Environment
1. Totality of External and Internal Forces
The environment includes all factors inside (internal) and outside (external) the
organization that influence business decisions.
Example: Internal: Company culture, financial resources; External: Market trends,
government regulations.
2. Dynamic Nature
Business environment is constantly changing, requiring businesses to adapt quickly.
Example: Technological advancements (like AI) or changing consumer preferences can
alter market demand rapidly.
3. Relative Nature
The impact of environmental factors varies for different businesses, industries, and
regions.
Example: A rise in fuel prices affects transportation companies more than IT firms.
4. Complex Nature
The environment consists of interrelated and interdependent factors that influence each
other.
Example: Economic slowdown can affect consumer spending, which in turn impacts sales
and production.
5. Uncertainty and Risk
Businesses cannot always predict environmental changes, which creates risk and
uncertainty.
