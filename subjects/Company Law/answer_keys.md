II. Fact - 3 Judgement - 2   5
The case of Lee vs. Lee’s Air Farming Ltd. (1961) is a landmark decision in company
law, particularly concerning the principle of the separate legal entity and the dual role of
a director as an employee.
Case Background
Mr. Lee formed Lee’s Air Farming Ltd. in 1954, holding 2999 of the 3000 shares and
serving as the sole managing director and chief pilot. Tragically, Mr. Lee died in a plane
crash while performing his duties as a pilot for the company. His widow, Mrs. Lee,
sought compensation under the New Zealand Workers’ Compensation Act, 1922,
claiming that Mr. Lee was an employee of the company.
Legal Issue - The central issue was whether Mr. Lee, as the majority shareholder and
managing director, could also be considered an employee of the company, thus entitling
his widow to compensation.
Judgment - The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council ruled in favor of Mrs. Lee,
establishing that:
- Separate Legal Entity: The company is a separate legal entity distinct from its
shareholders and directors. This means that Mr. Lee, despite his significant control over
the company, was legally separate from it.
- Dual Role: Mr. Lee could simultaneously be a director and an employee of the
company. The Court held that there was a valid contract of employment between Mr.
Lee and the company, making him an employee under the Workers’ Compensation Act.

A buyback of shares is buying back of own shares by a company that was issued
earlier. It is a corporate action event wherein a company makes a public announcement
for the buyback offer to acquire the shares from existing shareholders within a given
timeframe. The buyback of shares is also known as a stock buyback or repurchase of
shares. The company announces an offer price for the buyback that is generally higher
than the current market price.
Reason for Buyback of shares - Recently, we have seen a spur in the buyback offers by
the company. Do you know why do the companies buy back stock that was once issued
by them? There are several reasons associated with it that urge a company to announce
a buyback.
1. Undervalued stock This is one of the main reasons why companies opt to buy back
their shares. When the management feels that their stock is undervalued, they adopt the
buyback route to rectify the stock price. The stock buyback reduces the number of
shares in the market and thus gives a price boost to the remaining shares in the market.
2. Excess Cash with not many projects opportunities A company with free reserves in
hand but not many project opportunities would prefer to go for a buyback. The
company would use the cash to reward the shareholders rather than keeping it idle in
the bank account over the required amount.
3. Tax-efficient method of rewarding shareholders The dividends get taxed at two
levels. First, at the company level and a second time in the hands of the shareholders.
However, in the case of a buyback, only the company is liable to pay a buyback tax.
The capital gains tax on the income from the buyback of shares is exempted for the
investor. Thus, buybacks prove to be a more tax-effective way of distributing rewards
to the shareholder.
4. Strengthen promoter holding in the company The company promoters can increase
its stake in the company by forfeiting the buyback offer. This strengthens their hold
over the company and acts as a defense strategy in the case of hostile takeovers.
5. To achieve optimum capital structure The capital structure of a company gets
represented by its debt-equity ratio. Each industry has a different capital structure
requirement. Some industries may not be suitable to rely on more debts, whereas some
other business models may require large debts to run their business. Thus, as per the
company requirement, a company may opt for buyback as a tool and repurchase its
equity from the market to achieve an optimum capital structure.

the termination of the group's chairman. The reason given for Cyrus Mistry's dismissal

company had no such mismanagement.

chairman of TATA Sons and gave TATA a four-week period to file an appeal against the
NCLAT judgement. The Supreme Court then issued an injunction against the NCLAT's
order, stating that it has gaps and several flaws. The Supreme Court ordered that the
matter be thoroughly investigated.

law firm about possible steps ahead of his dismissal. Because of Cyrus Mistry's
dismissal from his post, the corporate sector was stunned, and the company's stocks
plunged 3.16% in the stock market.

Ashbury Railway Carriage and Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche (1875)
Background: In this case, Ashbury Railway Carriage and Iron Co. Ltd. (the plaintiff)
was a company formed to manufacture and sell railway carriages and wagons. The
company entered into a contract with Mr. Riche (the defendant) to build a railway line in
Belgium. The company's Memorandum of Association (MOA) authorized it to
manufacture and sell railway carriages, wagons, and other related activities. However,
constructing a railway line was not explicitly mentioned in the MOA.
Issue: The main issue was whether the contract to build a railway line in Belgium was
ultra vires the company's MOA and therefore void.
Application of the Doctrine of Ultra Vires: The doctrine of ultra vires was central to this
case. The principle was that a company's actions must be within the scope of its MOA. If
an action was beyond the objects mentioned in the MOA, it would be considered ultra
vires and void.
Court's Decision: The Court held that the contract for building a railway line was
beyond the company's authorized scope as per its MOA. Since the MOA only allowed
the company to manufacture and sell railway carriages and wagons, constructing a
railway line was not a permitted activity.
Impact and Significance: The case reinforced the principle that a company's actions
must be in line with its MOA's objects. Any action beyond the scope of the MOA was
considered ultra vires and void.   This case played a role in highlighting the need for
companies to have clear and specific objects outlined in their MOA. If a company
wished to engage in activities beyond its original scope, it had to alter its MOA with
shareholder approval. The case also emphasized the doctrine's effect on contracts. If
an action was ultra vires, the contract related to that action would be void and
unenforceable.

objectives, even if not explicitly mentioned in the MOA. Additionally, third parties
