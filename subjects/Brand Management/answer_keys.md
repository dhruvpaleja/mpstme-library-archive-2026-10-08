Q1 c. Explain what are flankers and cash cows in the Brand portfolio and what role do they play? Give an
example of each.
Flankers:
Protective or Fighter Brands
• To create stronger points-of-parity with competitors’ brands.
• Fighter brands must not be so attractive that they take sales away from their higher-priced
comparison brands.
•   If they are connected to other brands in the portfolio, they must not be designed so cheaply
that they reflect poorly on other brands
• Example 1: Surf Excel, Rin and Wheel
• Example 2: Westside, Zudio

Despite dwindling sales, some brands are retained:
• Due to their sustainability without any kind of marketing.

Q2. Name and explain the Six Brand Building Blocks in the Brand Resonance Pyramid with an example
of your choice from the Automobile industry.
Brand Performance describes how well the product or service:
• Meets customers’ more functional needs.
• Rate on objective assessments of quality.
• Extent to which it satisfies utilitarian, aesthetic, and economic customer needs and wants in
the product or service category .
Five important types of attributes and benefits often underlie brand performance:
1. Primary ingredients and supplementary features (eg. KitKat)
2. Product reliability, durability and serviceability (eg. Toyota)
3. Service effectiveness, efficiency and empathy (eg. Hospitals, Airlines)
4. Style and design ( Jaguar E type, Rolex Submariner)
5. Price (relatively expensive or inexpensive)
• Brand Imagery depends on the extrinsic properties of the product or service.
•   It is the way people think about the brand abstractly, rather than what they think the brand
actually does.
• Four main intangibles are:
• User profile/imagery (type of person/ organization who uses the brand eg. Nykaa)
********** 3 **********
--- page 4 ---
• Purchase and usage situations/imagery (tells the consumer under what conditions or
situations they can or should buy and use the brand eg. Domino’s 30 min delivery, Zepto)
• Brand personality and values (brands may take on personality traits or human values eg.
BMW)
• Brand history, heritage, and experiences (Burberry, Johnnie Walker)
• Brand Judgements are customer’s personal opinions and evaluations of the brand.
• Four types of judgements are particularly important:
• Brand Quality: The most important consumer attitudes towards a brand relate to its
perceived quality and to customer value and satisfaction. Eg. Taj Hotels
• Brand Credibility: Extent to which customers see the brand as credible in terms of three
dimensions: perceived expertise, trustworthiness, and likability. Eg. Samsung
• Brand Consideration: Brand attitudes and perception of credibility is important but not important
enough if customers don’t consider the brand for purchase or use.
• Brand Superiority: Extent to which customers see the brand as unique and better than other
brands.
•   Customers’ emotional responses and reactions to the brand.
•   Relate to the social currency evoked by the brand.
•   Feelings can be:
• Experiential and immediate, increasing in level of intensity.
• Private and enduring, increasing in level of gravity.
• Six important brand building feelings: Warmth, Fun, Excitement, Security, Social
approval, Self respect
• Brand Resonance describes the nature of this relationship and the extent to which consumers
feel that they are “in sync” with the brand.
•   Characterised by intensity of the psychological bond as well the level of activity due to this
loyalty, can be broken down into:
• Behavioural loyalty (how often and how much do they purchase?)
•   Attitudinal attachment (love the brand/ one of their favourite possessions)
• Sense of community (customers feel an affiliation with other people associated with the
brand eg. H.O.G, Cultfit)
•   Active engagement (customers are engaged or willing to invest time, energy, money, other
resources beyond purchase or consumption)

Q3. PepsiCo has recently launched the Kurkure Jowar Puffs which has the Power of Millet, Taste of
Kurkure and is baked and not fried. They are using Sara Ali Khan as the Brand Ambassador and are using
the tagline “isse accha kya hoga?” Now the company wants to do some Qualitative Research to identify
possible brand associations and sources of brand equity. Name the explain in detail the different Qualitative
Techniques that can be used for this task.
❑ Qualitative Research Techniques often identify possible brand associations and sources of brand
equity.
❑ These are relatively unstructured measurement approaches that permit a range of both questions and
answers and so can be a useful first step in exploring consumer, brand and product perceptions.
The simplest and the most powerful way to profile brand associations.
Without any specific probe, consumers narrate:
• What comes to their mind when they think about the brand or the associated product
category.
Help form a rough mental map for the brand.
Indicate the relative strength, favourability, and uniqueness of brand associations.
Diagnostic tools to uncover the true opinions and feelings of consumers when:
• They are unwilling or unable to express themselves.
• Present consumers with ambiguous stimulus and ask them to make sense of it.
• Example: When Nestle first launched Nescafe

--- page 6 ---
ZMET is based on a belief that consumers often have subconscious motives for their purchasing behaviour.
Uncovers hidden thoughts and feelings which can be expressed using metaphors.
Elicits interconnected constructs that influence thought and behavior.
Construct: An abstraction created by the researcher to capture common ideas or themes expressed by
customers.
Metaphor
• Defining one thing in terms of other .
• Represents thoughts that are tacit, implicit, and unspoken.
• Example: ease of use might capture statements “simple to operate”, “works without hassle”
and “you don’t really have to do anything”
• Some Marketing Research companies bypass any verbal response from consumers to literally
get inside the minds of consumers through various neural research methods.
• Neuromarketing: Study of how the brain responds to marketing stimuli, including brands.
•   Research indicates that consumer buying decision is a unconscious habitual process.
• Even basic decisions like purchasing petrol seem to be influenced by brain activity at the
subrational level.
Brand personality: Human characteristics or traits that consumers can attribute to a brand.
The Big five: Brand personality scale used to measure:
• Sincerity (honest, light-hearted and sensible)
• Excitement (daring and creative)
• Competence (responsible, brilliant and ambitious)
• Sophistication (elegant and pleasant)
• Ruggedness (resilient and sporty)
• Use “thick description” based on participant observation.
•   Extract and interpret the deep cultural meaning of events and activities through various
research techniques such as consumer immersion, site visits, shopalongs, embedded research
etc.
• Example: sending researchers to consumers homes in the morning to see how they approach
their days.
• Another example for service companies is a mystery shopper.

Q4. XStep is a new footwear brand being conceptualized for India which will launch a wide range of
affordable sneakers. It is a part of a larger Chinese conglomerate; however, they are open to relook at their
Brand Hierarchy. Help them to explore the different levels of Brand Hierarchy that they could go with and
the advantages and disadvantages of each one. Explain all the options in detail.
Highest level of hierarchy
Corporate image: The consumer associations to the company or corporation making the product or
providing the service.
• Relevant when the corporate or company brand plays a prominent role in the branding
strategy.
• Example 1: Alphabet and subsidiaries like Google, YouTube, Fitbit etc.
• Example 2: Nestle and individual brands such as Nescafe, KitKat, Maggi etc
Used in more than one product category but is not necessarily the name of the company or corporation.
• Also called a range brand or an umbrella brand.
If the corporate brand is applied to a range of products, then it functions as a family brand too.
If the products linked to the family brand are not carefully considered, the associations to the family brand
may become weaker.
Example: Nescafe Classic, Gold, Roasted, Cappuccino etc.
•   Restricted to essentially one product category, although multiple product types may differ.
•   Customization of the brand and all its supporting marketing activity.
•   If the brand runs into difficulty or fails, the risk to other brands and the company itself is
minimal.
•   Disadvantages of difficulty, complexity, and expense of developing separate marketing
programs.
• Example: HUL has Dove, Horlicks, Surf Excel, Bru, Lakme, Kwality Walls, Close-Up,
Cornetto, Knorr, Comfort, Axe etc.
Brands should distinguish according to the different types of items or models.
• Modifier: Designate a specific item or model type or a particular version or configuration of
the product
• Function of modifiers is to show how one brand variation relates to others in the same
brand family.
• Help make products more understandable and relevant to consumers.

• Example: Johnnie Walker Red Label, Black Label, Gold Label and Blue Label.
Helps consumers understand what the product is and does.
• Helps define the relevant competition in consumers’ minds.
In the case of a truly new product, introducing it with a familiar product name may facilitate basic
familiarity and comprehension.
Example: Audi
• A Series, Q Series, S Series etc.

3. Economies of Scale: As Patanjali diversifies into different product lines, it can benefit

4. Increased Revenue Streams: By introducing products in multiple categories, Patanjali

product categories.
Arguments Against Over-Extension of Patanjali Brand:

2. Quality Control Issues: Rapid expansion may result in quality control problems,
particularly in new product lines or categories, which can harm the brand's reputation for
consistency and reliability.
3. Consumer Confusion: Introducing too many products may lead to consumer confusion,

The Brand Value Chain (BVC) is a theoretical framework that maps the relationship between
brand-building investments and outcomes such as consumer perceptions, market performance,
and financial success. This framework helps brand managers make strategic decisions in
brand development and positioning.
Developed by Kevin Lane Keller and Donald R. Lehmann, the BVC demonstrates how
marketing activities influence consumer attitudes, market outcomes, and shareholder value.
For a brand like Disney, understanding the BVC can reveal how its investments in new
content, theme parks, and consumer experiences directly drive brand loyalty and financial
gains.
Structure of the BVC (4 marks)
1. Marketing Program Investment: This initial stage focuses on resources dedicated to
brand growth. For Disney, this includes investments in advertising, film production, theme
parks, and merchandise. Disney’s marketing activities, such as promoting a new movie or
opening an attraction, lay the foundation for building consumer excitement and brand
engagement.
2. Customer Mindset: Marketing investments shape how consumers perceive Disney. Key
elements include brand awareness (familiarity with Disney characters and franchises), brand
associations (associating Disney with family-friendly entertainment and magic), and brand
attitudes (positive feelings towards Disney). For instance, a Disney marketing campaign for a
new film can enhance brand attachment by evoking nostalgia and happiness.
3. Market Performance: When Disney successfully builds a positive consumer mindset, it
often leads to stronger market performance. This includes increased ticket sales, merchandise
purchases, and brand loyalty. For example, high anticipation for a new Disney film translates
into box office success and higher merchandise sales, showcasing Disney’s strong market
position.
4.   Shareholder Value: The final stage measures financial outcomes, such as stock price
growth, revenue increases, and profit margins. Disney’s successful brand strategy often results
in increased shareholder value, as the popularity of its films, parks, and products drive long-
term financial returns.

The royalty method of brand valuation is an income-based approach that estimates a brand’s
value by calculating potential royalty income if the brand were licensed to another company.
This method is particularly valuable for assessing brands with strong licensing potential, such
as Nike, where brand strength and consumer loyalty drive substantial revenue. By focusing on
projected licensing income, the royalty method helps quantify the brand’s equity and market
position in monetary terms.
2. Key Principles of the Royalty Method (4 marks)
(i) Royalty Rate Determination (1.5 marks)
The first principle involves determining an appropriate royalty rate, which reflects the brand’s
relative strength and market position. For Nike, this rate would likely be set higher than
average, given its global presence, loyal customer base, and high-quality perception. Industry-
standard rates, historical data from licensing deals, or similar brand comparisons help set this
rate. For instance, premium sportswear brands often command royalty rates ranging between
5% and 10%, a range Nike could justify based on its strong brand equity and consumer reach.
(ii) Projected Sales (1.5 marks)
The next principle involves estimating the brand’s projected sales volume, which could
include both core revenue streams and anticipated growth. For Nike, sales projections would
consider major revenue sources like footwear, athletic apparel, and collaborations with high-
profile athletes (e.g., LeBron James and Serena Williams). Seasonal trends, product launches,
and expansion into emerging markets (e.g., Asia and South America) could also impact sales
projections.
(iii) Calculating Brand Value (1 mark)
The final principle is calculating the brand value by multiplying the royalty rate by projected
sales. For example, if Nike’s projected annual revenue for a region is $5 billion and the
royalty rate is set at 6%, Nike’s brand value can be calculated as:
Brand Value=Projected Sales × Royalty Rate
((5,000,000,000×0.06 = 300,000,000))
Thus, Nike’s brand value in that context would be approximately $300 million, representing
the potential income from licensing Nike’s brand in that market.

The Brand Value Chain (BVC) is a theoretical framework that maps the relationship between
brand-building investments and outcomes such as consumer perceptions, market performance,
and financial success. This framework helps brand managers make strategic decisions in
brand development and positioning.
Developed by Kevin Lane Keller and Donald R. Lehmann, the BVC demonstrates how
marketing activities influence consumer attitudes, market outcomes, and shareholder value.
For a brand like Disney, understanding the BVC can reveal how its investments in new
content, theme parks, and consumer experiences directly drive brand loyalty and financial
gains.
Structure of the BVC (4 marks)
1. Marketing Program Investment: This initial stage focuses on resources dedicated to
brand growth. For Disney, this includes investments in advertising, film production, theme
parks, and merchandise. Disney’s marketing activities, such as promoting a new movie or
opening an attraction, lay the foundation for building consumer excitement and brand
engagement.
2. Customer Mindset: Marketing investments shape how consumers perceive Disney. Key
elements include brand awareness (familiarity with Disney characters and franchises), brand
associations (associating Disney with family-friendly entertainment and magic), and brand
attitudes (positive feelings towards Disney). For instance, a Disney marketing campaign for a
new film can enhance brand attachment by evoking nostalgia and happiness.
3. Market Performance: When Disney successfully builds a positive consumer mindset, it
often leads to stronger market performance. This includes increased ticket sales, merchandise
purchases, and brand loyalty. For example, high anticipation for a new Disney film translates
into box office success and higher merchandise sales, showcasing Disney’s strong market
position.
4.   Shareholder Value: The final stage measures financial outcomes, such as stock price
growth, revenue increases, and profit margins. Disney’s successful brand strategy often results
in increased shareholder value, as the popularity of its films, parks, and products drive long-
term financial returns.

The royalty method of brand valuation is an income-based approach that estimates a brand’s
value by calculating potential royalty income if the brand were licensed to another company.
This method is particularly valuable for assessing brands with strong licensing potential, such
as Nike, where brand strength and consumer loyalty drive substantial revenue. By focusing on
projected licensing income, the royalty method helps quantify the brand’s equity and market
position in monetary terms.
2. Key Principles of the Royalty Method (4 marks)
(i) Royalty Rate Determination (1.5 marks)
The first principle involves determining an appropriate royalty rate, which reflects the brand’s
relative strength and market position. For Nike, this rate would likely be set higher than
average, given its global presence, loyal customer base, and high-quality perception. Industry-
standard rates, historical data from licensing deals, or similar brand comparisons help set this
rate. For instance, premium sportswear brands often command royalty rates ranging between
5% and 10%, a range Nike could justify based on its strong brand equity and consumer reach.
(ii) Projected Sales (1.5 marks)
The next principle involves estimating the brand’s projected sales volume, which could
include both core revenue streams and anticipated growth. For Nike, sales projections would
consider major revenue sources like footwear, athletic apparel, and collaborations with high-
profile athletes (e.g., LeBron James and Serena Williams). Seasonal trends, product launches,
and expansion into emerging markets (e.g., Asia and South America) could also impact sales
projections.
(iii) Calculating Brand Value (1 mark)
The final principle is calculating the brand value by multiplying the royalty rate by projected
sales. For example, if Nike’s projected annual revenue for a region is $5 billion and the
royalty rate is set at 6%, Nike’s brand value can be calculated as:
Brand Value=Projected Sales × Royalty Rate
((5,000,000,000×0.06 = 300,000,000))
Thus, Nike’s brand value in that context would be approximately $300 million, representing
the potential income from licensing Nike’s brand in that market.

Nike’s strong reputation for quality, innovation, and celebrity endorsements supports a high
royalty rate. This brand loyalty and recognition help justify a higher percentage when
licensing its brand.
