b.   Describe the payment acceptance and processing cycle in online transactions.
Explain each stage from customer payment initiation to settlement.
The payment acceptance and processing cycle in online transactions refers to the
sequence of steps through which a customer’s payment is authorized, processed,
and settled into the merchant’s account. It involves multiple entities such as the
customer, merchant, payment gateway, acquiring bank, issuing bank, and card
network.
Stages of Online Payment Processing
1. Payment Initiation (Customer Stage)
The customer selects products/services and proceeds to checkout.
Payment details (card, wallet, net banking, etc.) are entered.
The transaction request is initiated.
2. Payment Gateway Processing
The Payment Gateway securely captures and encrypts payment information.
It acts as an intermediary between the merchant and banks.
CO-1; BL-3   [10]
Sensitive data is protected using SSL/TLS encryption.
3. Authorization Request
The payment gateway sends the transaction request to the acquiring bank
(merchant’s bank).
The acquiring bank forwards it to the card network (e.g., Visa/Mastercard).
The request is then sent to the issuing bank (customer’s bank).
4. Authorization Response
The issuing bank verifies:
Card validity
Available balance/credit
Fraud detection checks
It sends an approval or decline message back through the same channel.
5. Transaction Confirmation
The payment gateway informs the merchant and customer about the transaction
status.
If approved, the order is confirmed; otherwise, the customer is asked to retry.
6. Clearing
Transaction details are sent in batches to the acquiring bank.
********** 11 **********
--- page 12 ---
The acquiring bank requests funds from the issuing bank via the card network.
7. Settlement
Funds are transferred from the issuing bank to the acquiring bank.
The acquiring bank deposits the amount into the merchant’s account after
deducting fees.
Settlement usually takes 1–3 business days.
Key Entities Involved
Customer (Cardholder)
Merchant (E-commerce website)
Payment Gateway
Acquiring Bank (Merchant’s Bank)
Issuing Bank (Customer’s Bank)

instantly, while static catalogs rely on manual editing.

whereas static catalogs are simpler but less adaptive to changing market or
inventory conditions.

Online merchants employ multiple layers of security and fraud prevention
measures to safeguard transactions, protect customer data, and maintain
trust. These measures can be critically evaluated as follows:
Online merchants typically use encryption technologies such as SSL/TLS to
secure data transmitted between customers and servers. This ensures
confidentiality of sensitive information like credit card details. However, its
effectiveness depends on proper certificate management and server
configuration; outdated or self-signed certificates can still expose
vulnerabilities.
Authentication mechanisms like multi-factor authentication (MFA) and
CAPTCHA protect against unauthorized access and automated bot attacks.
While effective, these methods may reduce user convenience, leading to
potential cart abandonment.
Payment security is enhanced through tokenization and PCI-DSS
compliance, which minimize exposure of card data by replacing it with
secure tokens. Nevertheless, compliance requires ongoing audits, and
smaller merchants often struggle to maintain the necessary technical
standards.
********** 6 **********
--- page 7 ---
Fraud detection systems use AI-driven behavioral analytics to identify
unusual transaction patterns, geolocation mismatches, or repeated failed
logins. Though powerful, these systems can generate false positives,
occasionally flagging legitimate customers and affecting user experience.
Merchants also employ address verification systems (AVS), CVV
validation, and 3D Secure protocols for added authentication during
checkout. These reduce card-not-present (CNP) fraud but can be bypassed
through stolen credentials or phishing scams if users lack awareness.
Lastly, data protection laws such as GDPR and CCPA require merchants to
safeguard customer privacy and notify users of breaches. Compliance
enhances transparency, but frequent breaches in smaller e-commerce firms
reveal gaps in employee training, patch management, and vendor oversight.
In conclusion, while modern online merchants adopt strong technical and
procedural safeguards—encryption, authentication, AI-based fraud
monitoring—their overall effectiveness depends on continuous system
updates, employee vigilance, and user education. Security must evolve
dynamically to combat new threats such as phishing, account takeovers, and
synthetic identity fraud, ensuring a balanced trade-off between protection
and user convenience.

A small business selecting between shared hosting and dedicated hosting for
its online store must evaluate factors such as cost, performance, control, and
scalability to align hosting choices with business needs and growth
potential.
In shared hosting, multiple websites share the same server resources (CPU,
memory, bandwidth). It is cost-effective, easy to set up, and ideal for
startups with low traffic and limited budgets. However, performance may
degrade if other hosted sites consume more resources, and customization or
advanced security configurations are restricted. For example, a small local
boutique selling handcrafted products online can begin with shared hosting
to minimize operational expenses.
In contrast, dedicated hosting provides an entire server exclusively for one
business, offering high performance, stronger security, and full
administrative control. It is suitable for online stores expecting high traffic,
handling sensitive transactions, or requiring specialized applications. The
downside is the higher cost and need for technical expertise to manage the
server.
Thus, a small business should choose shared hosting for affordability and
simplicity during initial growth stages. As the business scales and traffic
increases, transitioning to dedicated hosting ensures better reliability, data
protection, and long-term scalability for e-commerce operations.

Core: multi-region cloud architecture with separate planes for B2C and
B2B, plus optional “edge” presence and selective private interconnects to
ERP/partners.
Architecture (high level)
• Global entry: Anycast DNS + Global Load Balancer; CDN for
static/media; WAF + bot management; geo-routing for data
residency.
• B2C tier (elastic):
• Frontends on managed Kubernetes (EKS/AKS/GKE) or serverless
(Fargate/Cloud Run/Functions) with autoscaling by QPS and latency.
• API layer behind the same cluster, rate-limited per region.
• Sessionless design (JWT) + edge caching; feature flags and canary
releases.
• B2B tier (steady/isolated):
• Separate VPC/VNet and clusters; private API gateways; partner
access via IP allowlists, mTLS, and site-to-site VPN/Direct
Connect/ExpressRoute.
• Dedicated message broker (managed Kafka/Event Hubs/PubSub)
with QoS and replay for integrations.
• Data:
• Regional primary databases (managed SQL/NoSQL) with read
replicas at edge regions; sharding by region/tenant to meet data-
residency.
• Object storage per region with cross-region replication; KMS-
backed encryption.
•   Observability & SRE:
• Centralized logging/metrics/tracing; SLOs per surface (p95 latency,
availability); error budgets govern release velocity.
•   Security:
• Zero-trust access, IAM least privilege, secret vault, tokenization for
PII/PCI, DDoS protection, CSPM posture checks.
Scalability reasoning
• B2C elasticity via HPA/KPA and serverless concurrency caps; CDN
offload ≥80% of static QPS; autoscale policies tied to p95 latency
and queue depth.
• B2B stability via fixed capacity + controlled partner windows; burst
handled by queues, not synchronous fan-out.
• Regional failover with active-active for read, active-passive for write
(RTO ≤ 30 min, RPO ≤ 5 min) using managed cross-region
replication.
4) Where self-hosting/dedicated still fit (hybrid hooks)
• Low-latency plant/warehouse systems or regulated data staying on-
prem: expose via private links to cloud VPC.

Bottom line
Adopt a cloud-first, multi-region design for B2C elasticity and global reach,
