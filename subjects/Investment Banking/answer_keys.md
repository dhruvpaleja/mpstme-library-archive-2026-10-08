Organized Segment: Regulated by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI),

2   a Given:
   Face value of the bill = ₹100
   Discount rate = 15% per annum
 Time period = 2 months = 2/12 years = 1/6 years
i) The Amount of Discount
The discount is calculated as the interest charged on the face value for the
remaining maturity at the given discount rate.
Formula: Discount = Face Value × Discount Rate × Time Period
Discount = ₹100 × 0.15 × (2/12) = ₹100 × 0.15 × 0.1667 = ₹2.50
Answer: The discount amount is ₹2.50.   (2 Marks)
ii) The Net Amount the Bank Will Receive
The net amount received by the bank is the face value minus the discount
amount.
Formula: Net Amount = Face Value − Discount
Net Amount = ₹100 − ₹2.50 = ₹97.50
Answer: The bank will receive ₹97.50.   (1 Marks)
iii) The Effective Yield (Annualized)
The yield represents the annualized return earned by the bank on the
amount it pays (net amount) to receive the face value at maturity.
Formula: Yield = (Discount / Net Amount) × (12 / Time in Months) ×
100
Yield = (₹2.50 / ₹97.50) × (12 / 2) × 100 = 0.02564 × 6 × 100 = 0.1538 ×
100 = 15.38%
Answer: The effective yield is 15.38% per annum.   (2 Marks)

4   The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) regulates public   10
issues through the SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure
Requirements) Regulations, 2018, commonly known as ICDR
Regulations. These provisions ensure transparency, investor protection,
and orderly capital market development.
Key Regulatory Provisions:
1. Eligibility Criteria:
o   Issuer must have net tangible assets of ₹3 crore and net
worth of ₹1 crore for the past 3 years.
o Minimum average operating profit of ₹15 crore over the
last 3 years.
2. Types of Public Issues:
o   Initial Public Offer (IPO)
o Further Public Offer (FPO)
o Rights Issue
o   Preferential Issue
o Qualified Institutional Placement (QIP)
3. Disclosure Requirements:
o   Issuers must file a Draft Red Herring Prospectus
(DRHP) with SEBI.
o Disclosures include financials, risk factors, business
details, and legal proceedings.
4. Pricing Mechanism:
o Fixed Price or Book Building method.
o Book building involves price discovery through investor
bids.
5. Minimum Public Shareholding:
o At least 25% of post-issue capital must be offered to the
public.
6. Lock-in Requirements:
o Promoters’ minimum contribution is locked in for 3 years.
Procedural Steps:
1. Appointment of Intermediaries:
o Merchant bankers, registrars, legal advisors, and
underwriters are appointed.
2. Preparation and Filing of DRHP:
o   Filed with SEBI for review and observations.
3. Stock Exchange Approval:
o   In-principle approval from stock exchanges is obtained.
4. Marketing and Roadshows:
o Conducted to attract investor interest.

5. Issue Opening and Bidding:
o   Investors apply via ASBA; UPI is mandatory for retail
investors.
6. Allotment and Listing:
o Shares are allotted and listed within 6 working days of
issue closure.
Conclusion:
SEBI’s regulatory framework ensures that public issues are conducted in
a fair, transparent, and efficient manner, balancing the interests of issuers
and investors. These steps are crucial for companies aiming to raise
capital and build investor confidence.

6   a   Private placement refers to the issuance of securities by a company to a 5
select group of investors, rather than through a public offer. As per Section
42 of the Companies Act, 2013, private placement allows companies to
raise capital by offering securities to not more than 200 persons in a
financial year, excluding Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) and
employees under ESOP schemes.
This mechanism is particularly significant for Private Equity (PE) and
Venture Capital (VC) investors, as   it enables them to invest in
companies—especially startups and growth-stage enterprises—without the
procedural complexities of a public issue. Through private placement,
PE/VC firms can negotiate terms, valuations, and rights directly with the
company.
The regulatory framework ensures transparency and investor protection by
mandating:
Use of Private Placement Offer-cum-Application Letter (PPOAL) in
Form PAS-4.
Disclosure of pricing methodology, valuation reports, and control changes.
Filing of return of allotment in Form PAS-3 within 15 days of allotment.
Thus, private placement serves as a flexible and efficient route for PE and
VC investments, balancing ease of capital raising with regulatory
oversight.

b Loan Syndication is a process where a group of lenders (usually banks)   5
come together to provide a large loan to a single borrower, typically a
corporation or government entity. This is done when the loan amount is
too large or risky for a single lender to handle alone.
Process of Loan Syndication:
********** 7 **********
--- page 8 ---
1. Lead Arranger Appointment: A lead bank or financial
institution is appointed to structure the deal and coordinate with
other lenders.
2. Loan Structuring: Terms such as interest rate, repayment
schedule, and collateral are finalized.
3. Invitation to Participate: Other banks are invited to join the
syndicate and contribute a portion of the loan.
4. Documentation and Disbursement: Legal agreements are
signed, and the loan is disbursed to the borrower.
Significance:
•   Enables financing of large projects like infrastructure, mergers, or
expansions.
•   Diversifies risk among multiple lenders.
•   Provides access to global capital and expertise.
Loan syndication is especially useful for companies in emerging markets
like India, where large-scale funding may not be available from domestic
sources alone.

Detailed Explanation
1. Definition and Purpose
Primary Market: This is where companies, governments, or other
entities issue new securities to raise fresh capital. The primary market is
used for funding long-term projects, expansion, or debt repayment. For
example, a company launches an Initial Public Offering (IPO) to raise
funds from the public.
Secondary Market: This is where previously issued securities are bought
and sold among investors. The secondary market provides liquidity,
allowing investors to trade shares, bonds, or derivatives, and facilitates
price discovery based on market dynamics.
2. Nature of Transaction
Primary Market: Transactions involve the issuer (e.g., a company)
directly selling securities to investors, either through public offerings
(IPOs, FPOs), rights issues, or private placements.
Secondary Market: Transactions occur between investors without the
issuer’s involvement. The company does not receive funds from trades in
this market; instead, investors exchange securities for profit or portfolio
adjustments.
3. Securities Involved
Primary Market: Involves new securities, such as equity shares through
IPOs or Follow-on Public Offerings (FPOs), bonds, debentures, rights
issues, or preferential allotments. Recent additions include green bonds
and Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs).
Secondary Market: Deals with existing securities already listed on stock
exchanges, including stocks, bonds, and derivatives (e.g., futures,
options).
4. Participants
Primary Market: Key participants include issuers (corporates,
governments), investors (retail, institutional, foreign portfolio investors -
FPIs), and intermediaries like merchant bankers, underwriters, and
registrars who facilitate the issuance process.
Secondary Market: Involves investors (retail, institutional, FPIs),
stockbrokers, depository participants, and stock exchanges (e.g., BSE,
NSE). Issuers are not directly involved.
5. Pricing
Primary Market: The price of securities is predetermined, either fixed
by the issuer or determined through a book-building process where
investors bid within a price band.
Secondary Market: Prices are determined by market forces of supply and
demand, fluctuating in real-time based on trading activity and market
sentiment.
6. Regulation
Primary Market: Regulated by the Securities and Exchange Board of
India (SEBI), which mandates strict disclosure norms, prospectus filings,
********** 4 **********
--- page 5 ---
and compliance with the Companies Act, 2013, to protect investors during
issuance.
Secondary Market: Also regulated by SEBI, with additional oversight by
stock exchanges like BSE and NSE. Recent reforms include T+0
settlement for top stocks (introduced in January 2025) to enhance
efficiency.
7. Liquidity
Primary Market: Offers limited liquidity since securities are not yet
traded. Investors must wait for listing on exchanges to sell their holdings.
Secondary Market: Provides high liquidity, with active trading platforms
enabling investors to buy or sell securities instantly during market hours.
8. Capital Flow
Primary Market: Funds raised go directly to the issuer for business
purposes, such as capital expenditure, debt repayment, or acquisitions.
Secondary Market: Funds are exchanged among investors, with no direct
financial benefit to the issuing company. However, active trading can
influence the company’s market valuation.
9. Examples
Primary Market: Examples include the IPO of a startup listing on NSE,
a government issuing 10-year bonds, or a rights issue by a listed company
to existing shareholders.
Secondary Market: Examples include trading of Reliance Industries
shares on BSE or government bonds on the NSE’s debt segment.
10. Market Size and Impact
Primary Market: Contributes to capital formation, with significant
activity in 2025 driven by IPOs and bond issuances. For instance, India’s
capital market raised substantial funds through 41% FPI-backed IPOs in
2024.
Secondary Market: Drives market capitalization (₹459 lakh crore as of
June 2025) and supports investor wealth creation, with 19.9 crore demat
accounts reflecting broad participation.

Detailed Explanation
1. Definition and Purpose
Primary Market: This is where companies, governments, or other
entities issue new securities to raise fresh capital. The primary market is
used for funding long-term projects, expansion, or debt repayment. For
example, a company launches an Initial Public Offering (IPO) to raise
funds from the public.
Secondary Market: This is where previously issued securities are bought
and sold among investors. The secondary market provides liquidity,
allowing investors to trade shares, bonds, or derivatives, and facilitates
price discovery based on market dynamics.
2. Nature of Transaction
Primary Market: Transactions involve the issuer (e.g., a company)
directly selling securities to investors, either through public offerings
(IPOs, FPOs), rights issues, or private placements.
Secondary Market: Transactions occur between investors without the
issuer’s involvement. The company does not receive funds from trades in
this market; instead, investors exchange securities for profit or portfolio
adjustments.
3. Securities Involved
Primary Market: Involves new securities, such as equity shares through
IPOs or Follow-on Public Offerings (FPOs), bonds, debentures, rights
issues, or preferential allotments. Recent additions include green bonds
and Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs).
Secondary Market: Deals with existing securities already listed on stock
exchanges, including stocks, bonds, and derivatives (e.g., futures,
options).
4. Participants
Primary Market: Key participants include issuers (corporates,
governments), investors (retail, institutional, foreign portfolio investors -
FPIs), and intermediaries like merchant bankers, underwriters, and
registrars who facilitate the issuance process.
Secondary Market: Involves investors (retail, institutional, FPIs),
stockbrokers, depository participants, and stock exchanges (e.g., BSE,
NSE). Issuers are not directly involved.
5. Pricing
Primary Market: The price of securities is predetermined, either fixed
by the issuer or determined through a book-building process where
investors bid within a price band.
Secondary Market: Prices are determined by market forces of supply and
demand, fluctuating in real-time based on trading activity and market
sentiment.
6. Regulation
Primary Market: Regulated by the Securities and Exchange Board of
India (SEBI), which mandates strict disclosure norms, prospectus filings,
********** 4 **********
--- page 5 ---
and compliance with the Companies Act, 2013, to protect investors during
issuance.
Secondary Market: Also regulated by SEBI, with additional oversight by
stock exchanges like BSE and NSE. Recent reforms include T+0
settlement for top stocks (introduced in January 2025) to enhance
efficiency.
7. Liquidity
Primary Market: Offers limited liquidity since securities are not yet
traded. Investors must wait for listing on exchanges to sell their holdings.
Secondary Market: Provides high liquidity, with active trading platforms
enabling investors to buy or sell securities instantly during market hours.
8. Capital Flow
Primary Market: Funds raised go directly to the issuer for business
purposes, such as capital expenditure, debt repayment, or acquisitions.
Secondary Market: Funds are exchanged among investors, with no direct
financial benefit to the issuing company. However, active trading can
influence the company’s market valuation.
9. Examples
Primary Market: Examples include the IPO of a startup listing on NSE,
a government issuing 10-year bonds, or a rights issue by a listed company
to existing shareholders.
Secondary Market: Examples include trading of Reliance Industries
shares on BSE or government bonds on the NSE’s debt segment.
10. Market Size and Impact
Primary Market: Contributes to capital formation, with significant
activity in 2025 driven by IPOs and bond issuances. For instance, India’s
capital market raised substantial funds through 41% FPI-backed IPOs in
2024.
Secondary Market: Drives market capitalization (₹459 lakh crore as of
June 2025) and supports investor wealth creation, with 19.9 crore demat
accounts reflecting broad participation.

Detailed Explanation
1. Definition and Purpose
Primary Market: This is where companies, governments, or other
entities issue new securities to raise fresh capital. The primary market is
used for funding long-term projects, expansion, or debt repayment. For
example, a company launches an Initial Public Offering (IPO) to raise
funds from the public.
Secondary Market: This is where previously issued securities are bought
and sold among investors. The secondary market provides liquidity,
allowing investors to trade shares, bonds, or derivatives, and facilitates
price discovery based on market dynamics.
2. Nature of Transaction
Primary Market: Transactions involve the issuer (e.g., a company)
directly selling securities to investors, either through public offerings
(IPOs, FPOs), rights issues, or private placements.
Secondary Market: Transactions occur between investors without the
issuer’s involvement. The company does not receive funds from trades in
this market; instead, investors exchange securities for profit or portfolio
adjustments.
3. Securities Involved
Primary Market: Involves new securities, such as equity shares through
IPOs or Follow-on Public Offerings (FPOs), bonds, debentures, rights
issues, or preferential allotments. Recent additions include green bonds
and Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs).
Secondary Market: Deals with existing securities already listed on stock
exchanges, including stocks, bonds, and derivatives (e.g., futures,
options).
4. Participants
Primary Market: Key participants include issuers (corporates,
governments), investors (retail, institutional, foreign portfolio investors -
FPIs), and intermediaries like merchant bankers, underwriters, and
registrars who facilitate the issuance process.
Secondary Market: Involves investors (retail, institutional, FPIs),
stockbrokers, depository participants, and stock exchanges (e.g., BSE,
NSE). Issuers are not directly involved.
5. Pricing
Primary Market: The price of securities is predetermined, either fixed
by the issuer or determined through a book-building process where
investors bid within a price band.
Secondary Market: Prices are determined by market forces of supply and
demand, fluctuating in real-time based on trading activity and market
sentiment.
6. Regulation
Primary Market: Regulated by the Securities and Exchange Board of
India (SEBI), which mandates strict disclosure norms, prospectus filings,
********** 4 **********
--- page 5 ---
and compliance with the Companies Act, 2013, to protect investors during
issuance.
Secondary Market: Also regulated by SEBI, with additional oversight by
stock exchanges like BSE and NSE. Recent reforms include T+0
settlement for top stocks (introduced in January 2025) to enhance
efficiency.
7. Liquidity
Primary Market: Offers limited liquidity since securities are not yet
traded. Investors must wait for listing on exchanges to sell their holdings.
Secondary Market: Provides high liquidity, with active trading platforms
enabling investors to buy or sell securities instantly during market hours.
8. Capital Flow
Primary Market: Funds raised go directly to the issuer for business
purposes, such as capital expenditure, debt repayment, or acquisitions.
Secondary Market: Funds are exchanged among investors, with no direct
financial benefit to the issuing company. However, active trading can
influence the company’s market valuation.
9. Examples
Primary Market: Examples include the IPO of a startup listing on NSE,
a government issuing 10-year bonds, or a rights issue by a listed company
to existing shareholders.
Secondary Market: Examples include trading of Reliance Industries
shares on BSE or government bonds on the NSE’s debt segment.
10. Market Size and Impact
Primary Market: Contributes to capital formation, with significant
activity in 2025 driven by IPOs and bond issuances. For instance, India’s
capital market raised substantial funds through 41% FPI-backed IPOs in
2024.
Secondary Market: Drives market capitalization (₹459 lakh crore as of
June 2025) and supports investor wealth creation, with 19.9 crore demat
accounts reflecting broad participation.

Primary Market: This is where companies, governments, or other
entities issue new securities to raise fresh capital. The primary market is
used for funding long-term projects, expansion, or debt repayment. For
example, a company launches an Initial Public Offering (IPO) to raise
funds from the public.
Secondary Market: This is where previously issued securities are bought
and sold among investors. The secondary market provides liquidity,
allowing investors to trade shares, bonds, or derivatives, and facilitates
price discovery based on market dynamics.
2. Nature of Transaction
Primary Market: Transactions involve the issuer (e.g., a company)
directly selling securities to investors, either through public offerings
(IPOs, FPOs), rights issues, or private placements.
Secondary Market: Transactions occur between investors without the
issuer’s involvement. The company does not receive funds from trades in
this market; instead, investors exchange securities for profit or portfolio
adjustments.
3. Securities Involved
Primary Market: Involves new securities, such as equity shares through
IPOs or Follow-on Public Offerings (FPOs), bonds, debentures, rights
issues, or preferential allotments. Recent additions include green bonds
and Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs).
Secondary Market: Deals with existing securities already listed on stock
exchanges, including stocks, bonds, and derivatives (e.g., futures,
options).
4. Participants
Primary Market: Key participants include issuers (corporates,
governments), investors (retail, institutional, foreign portfolio investors -
FPIs), and intermediaries like merchant bankers, underwriters, and
registrars who facilitate the issuance process.
Secondary Market: Involves investors (retail, institutional, FPIs),
stockbrokers, depository participants, and stock exchanges (e.g., BSE,
NSE). Issuers are not directly involved.
5. Pricing
Primary Market: The price of securities is predetermined, either fixed
by the issuer or determined through a book-building process where
investors bid within a price band.
Secondary Market: Prices are determined by market forces of supply and
demand, fluctuating in real-time based on trading activity and market
sentiment.
6. Regulation
Primary Market: Regulated by the Securities and Exchange Board of
India (SEBI), which mandates strict disclosure norms, prospectus filings,
********** 4 **********
--- page 5 ---
and compliance with the Companies Act, 2013, to protect investors during
issuance.
Secondary Market: Also regulated by SEBI, with additional oversight by
stock exchanges like BSE and NSE. Recent reforms include T+0
settlement for top stocks (introduced in January 2025) to enhance
efficiency.
7. Liquidity
Primary Market: Offers limited liquidity since securities are not yet
traded. Investors must wait for listing on exchanges to sell their holdings.
Secondary Market: Provides high liquidity, with active trading platforms
enabling investors to buy or sell securities instantly during market hours.
8. Capital Flow
Primary Market: Funds raised go directly to the issuer for business
purposes, such as capital expenditure, debt repayment, or acquisitions.
Secondary Market: Funds are exchanged among investors, with no direct
financial benefit to the issuing company. However, active trading can
influence the company’s market valuation.
9. Examples
Primary Market: Examples include the IPO of a startup listing on NSE,
a government issuing 10-year bonds, or a rights issue by a listed company
to existing shareholders.
Secondary Market: Examples include trading of Reliance Industries
shares on BSE or government bonds on the NSE’s debt segment.
10. Market Size and Impact
Primary Market: Contributes to capital formation, with significant
activity in 2025 driven by IPOs and bond issuances. For instance, India’s
capital market raised substantial funds through 41% FPI-backed IPOs in
2024.
Secondary Market: Drives market capitalization (₹459 lakh crore as of
June 2025) and supports investor wealth creation, with 19.9 crore demat
accounts reflecting broad participation.

Uncertain Impact: Shareholders do not receive guaranteed benefits as prices vary.
Longer Duration: It can take more time to execute the buyback.
Weaker Signaling: Market perception of undervaluation is less pronounced compared
to a fixed price buyback.

Companies with steady cash flow aiming for a long-term impact on share price and
reduced equity base without creating a significant price spike.

The Net Asset Value (NAV) of a mutual fund represents the per-unit value of the fund’s assets
after accounting for its liabilities. The NAV changes daily and is influenced by several
factors:
1. Market Performance of the Underlying Assets
Equity Funds: Changes in the prices of stocks in the portfolio directly impact the NAV. If
the stock market performs well, the NAV is likely to increase, and vice versa.
Debt Funds: The NAV is affected by interest rate movements. When interest rates fall,
bond prices typically rise, leading to an increase in NAV. Conversely, rising interest rates
can decrease NAV.
2. Income Generated by the Fund
Mutual funds may earn dividends, interest, or other income from their investments, which
contributes to the fund's total assets and increases the NAV.
3. Expenses of the Fund
Management fees, administrative costs, and other operating expenses reduce the NAV.
Higher expenses can have a significant impact on the NAV over time.
4. Inflows and Outflows
Page 12 of 13
--- page 13 ---
Although inflows (investments) and outflows (redemptions) do not directly affect the
NAV, they can lead to rebalancing of the portfolio, which might influence the overall value
depending on market conditions.
5. Market Volatility
General market trends and volatility can impact the value of the fund’s holdings. For
example, economic or geopolitical events might cause sharp market movements, affecting
NAV.
6. Fund-Specific Actions
Dividend Payouts: If a mutual fund pays dividends, the NAV decreases by the dividend
amount on the ex-dividend date.
Rebalancing: Changes in the portfolio composition (e.g., buying or selling assets) can
impact the NAV.
7. Macroeconomic Factors
Inflation, changes in currency exchange rates, and overall economic conditions can
influence the performance of the assets in the portfolio, thereby affecting the NAV.
8. Regulatory or Tax Changes
Changes in tax laws or regulations can impact the value of investments, influencing the
NAV.

B. Fixed Price Buyback:   5
1. Mechanism:
The company offers to buy back shares from shareholders at a predetermined price,
typically at a premium to the current market price.
2. Advantages:
Certainty for Shareholders: Shareholders are assured of a specific price for their
shares, often higher than the prevailing market price.
Signaling Effect: A fixed price buyback at a premium sends a strong signal to the
market about the company's confidence in its intrinsic value.
Targeted Execution: The buyback can be completed quickly and with clear terms.
Page 9 of 13
--- page 10 ---
3. Disadvantages:
Higher Cost: Offering a premium makes it more expensive for the company.
Limited Flexibility: The fixed price and quantity reduce the company's ability to adapt
to market fluctuations during the process.
4. Best Suited For:
Companies aiming to reward shareholders directly or boost investor confidence in
undervalued stocks.
Open Market Repurchase:
1. Mechanism:
The company buys back shares gradually from the open market over a specific period
at prevailing market prices.
2. Advantages:
Flexibility: The company can adjust the repurchase based on market conditions and
cash flow availability.
Cost Efficiency: Shares are bought at market prices, which may be lower than a fixed
price.
Less Disruption: Gradual repurchase minimizes sudden impact on the stock price.
3. Disadvantages:
Uncertain Impact: Shareholders do not receive guaranteed benefits as prices vary.
Longer Duration: It can take more time to execute the buyback.
Weaker Signaling: Market perception of undervaluation is less pronounced compared
to a fixed price buyback.
4. Best Suited For:
Companies with steady cash flow aiming for a long-term impact on share price and
reduced equity base without creating a significant price spike.

The Net Asset Value (NAV) of a mutual fund represents the per-unit value of the fund’s assets
after accounting for its liabilities. The NAV changes daily and is influenced by several
factors:
1. Market Performance of the Underlying Assets
Equity Funds: Changes in the prices of stocks in the portfolio directly impact the NAV. If
the stock market performs well, the NAV is likely to increase, and vice versa.
Debt Funds: The NAV is affected by interest rate movements. When interest rates fall,
bond prices typically rise, leading to an increase in NAV. Conversely, rising interest rates
can decrease NAV.
2. Income Generated by the Fund
Mutual funds may earn dividends, interest, or other income from their investments, which
contributes to the fund's total assets and increases the NAV.
3. Expenses of the Fund
Management fees, administrative costs, and other operating expenses reduce the NAV.
Higher expenses can have a significant impact on the NAV over time.
4. Inflows and Outflows
Page 12 of 13
--- page 13 ---
Although inflows (investments) and outflows (redemptions) do not directly affect the
NAV, they can lead to rebalancing of the portfolio, which might influence the overall value
depending on market conditions.
5. Market Volatility
General market trends and volatility can impact the value of the fund’s holdings. For
example, economic or geopolitical events might cause sharp market movements, affecting
NAV.
6. Fund-Specific Actions
Dividend Payouts: If a mutual fund pays dividends, the NAV decreases by the dividend
amount on the ex-dividend date.
Rebalancing: Changes in the portfolio composition (e.g., buying or selling assets) can
impact the NAV.
7. Macroeconomic Factors
Inflation, changes in currency exchange rates, and overall economic conditions can
influence the performance of the assets in the portfolio, thereby affecting the NAV.
8. Regulatory or Tax Changes
Changes in tax laws or regulations can impact the value of investments, influencing the
NAV.
