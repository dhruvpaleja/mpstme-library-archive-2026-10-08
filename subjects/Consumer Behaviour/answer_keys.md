A customer is the one who purchases the product.
A consumer is the one who finally uses or consumes the product.
A person can be both a customer and consumer (e.g., buying for self).
Or, they may differ (e.g., parent as customer, child as consumer).
Consumers can be classified into habitual, impulsive, brand-loyal, variety-seeking, and
price-sensitive types.
Consumer diversity refers to wide differences in needs, preferences, and behavior across
individuals.
Age groups create diversity (kids, teens, adults, seniors).
Income levels create diversity (budget vs. premium buyers).
Cultural and lifestyle values also shape consumer diversity.
Thus, brands face diverse consumer profiles and must adapt offerings accordingly.

In the 21st century, digitalization has made consumer behaviour more complex.
Marketers need to track changing lifestyles and preferences quickly.
For example, health-conscious consumers have increased demand for organic foods.
Another example is online shopping, which changed buying patterns globally.
Consumer involvement refers to the level of effort and interest a buyer shows in decision-
making.
High involvement is seen in buying expensive products like cars or laptops.
Low involvement is seen in routine items like soap or milk.
By studying involvement, marketers can design communication and promotions
accordingly.
B. 3 marks for each trait   15
Personality refers to unique traits that influence how consumers behave.
One trait is innovativeness, where some consumers adopt new products quickly.
Materialism reflects the desire to own possessions and status items.
Self-confidence drives consumers to make independent purchase decisions.
Sociability influences buying products that promote social interaction.
Risk-taking describes consumers who enjoy trying new or untested products.
These traits affect product choices and brand preferences.
For example, innovators often try the latest tech gadgets.
Risk-averse consumers prefer tested and trusted brands.
Understanding traits helps marketers target segments with tailored strategies.
C. 15 marks for detailed explanation   15
Consumer decision making also applies to adopting new innovations.
The process begins with awareness, e.g., hearing about a new smartwatch.
Next is interest, where consumers seek more information.
Evaluation follows, assessing usefulness and price.
Trial allows consumers to try the innovation, like testing a smartwatch in a store.
Adoption occurs if satisfaction is met, and the product is purchased regularly.
Some consumers adopt early (innovators), while others wait (laggards).
Marketers target early adopters to build credibility.
Adoption depends on perceived benefits, social influence, and ease of use.
Example: Early users buying and recommending the Tesla Model 3 before mass adoption.
