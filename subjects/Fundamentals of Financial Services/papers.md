valued deeply after witnessing her father struggle financially due to inadequate insurance
coverage. On a rainy Monday morning, Neha walked into her familiar bank branch to
prematurely close a fixed deposit to fund her sister’s wedding.

manager, Mr. Rohan Mehta, approached her with a warm smile and an urgent tone. He spoke
about a “special protection plan” being offered in partnership with SecureLife Insurance
Company, exclusively for long‑standing customers like her. He assured her that the policy
was “safe, flexible, and fully endorsed by the bank.”
Neha hesitated. She had always associated insurance with agents, not bank counters. Sensing
her doubt, Rohan emphasized that purchasing insurance through the bank—bancassurance, as
he called it—was more reliable than dealing with independent agents. He claimed the policy
would secure her family’s future and required minimal paperwork, all of which could be
handled instantly at the branch.
Pressed for time and reassured by the bank’s reputation, Neha signed the documents. She was
handed a welcome kit and told that all policy details would soon be emailed. As she stepped
out into the drizzle, she felt a quiet relief—until days turned into weeks with no clear
communication from the insurer.
The suspense unfolded a month later when Neha finally read the policy document in detail.
She realized that the policy had restrictions, exclusions, and premium commitments that were
never clearly explained to her. The policy was not as flexible as suggested; surrender charges
were high, and coverage benefits were conditional.

"RajanFinance — A Tale of Systemic Challenges in the Indian Financial System"
Background:
RajanFinance Ltd. is a mid-sized Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC) registered with
RBI, operating in Maharashtra. It mobilises short-term deposits from the public and lends to
small businesses, housing, and agriculture. The company also invests surplus funds in money
market instruments like Commercial Paper and Treasury Bills.
In 2023, RajanFinance faced a severe liquidity crisis. Several of its large borrowers (real
estate companies) defaulted, turning a significant portion of its loan book into Non-
Performing Assets (NPAs). The company struggled to repay its depositors and was unable to
roll over its short-term borrowings in the money market. Its credit rating was downgraded,
and it was unable to issue fresh Commercial Paper.
Meanwhile, one of the company's directors secretly purchased put options (derivatives) on the
company's shares before the NPA news was made public — a classic case of insider trading,
later investigated by SEBI.
The company also had significant exposure to a crypto investment fund that had invested in
various Virtual Digital Assets. The collapse of a major cryptocurrency wiped out a portion of
this investment.
RajanFinance had issued GDRs on the Luxembourg Stock Exchange three years earlier to
raise foreign capital. As news of the crisis spread, GDR prices crashed, and Foreign Portfolio
Investors pulled out their investments.
