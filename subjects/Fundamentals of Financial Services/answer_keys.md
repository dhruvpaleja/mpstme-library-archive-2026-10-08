Money Market Instruments:

1. Call /Notice-Money: Call/Notice money is the money borrowed or lent on
demand for a very short period. When money is borrowed or lent for a day, it is
known as Call (Overnight) Money. Intervening holidays and/or Sunday are
excluded for this purpose. Thus money, borrowed on a day and repaid on the
next working day, (irrespective of the number of intervening holidays) is "Call
Money". When money is borrowed or lent for more than a day and up to 14
days, it is "Notice Money". No collateral security is required to cover these
transactions.

2. Inter-Bank Term Money: Inter-bank market for deposits of maturity beyond
14 days is referred to as the term money market. The entry restrictions are the
same as those for Call/Notice Money except that, as per existing regulations,
some specified entities are not allowed to lend beyond 14 days.

3. Treasury Bills: Treasury Bills are short term (up to one year) borrowing
instruments of the union government. It is a promise by the Government to pay
a stated sum after expiry of the stated period from the date of issue
(14/91/182/364 days i.e. less than one year). They are issued at a discount to the
face value, and on maturity the face value is paid to the holder. The rate of
discount and the corresponding issue price are determined at each auction.

A) Capital Market Instruments:

1) Traditional instruments:

a. Equity shares - Equity shares are instruments issued by companies to raise capital and it
represents the title to the ownership of a company. You become an owner of a company by
subscribing to its equity capital (whereby you will be allotted shares) or by buying its shares
from its existing owners. As a shareholder, you bear the entrepreneurial risk of the business
venture and are entitled to benefits of ownership like share in the distributed profit (dividend)
etc. The returns earned in equity depend upon the profits made by the company. company’s
future growth etc.

b. Preference shares — They entitle you to receive dividend at a fixed rate. Importantly, this

dividend had to be paid to you before dividend can be paid to equity shareholders. In the event
of liquidation of the company, your claim to the company’s surplus will be higher than that of
the equity holders, but however, below the claims of the company’s creditors, bondholders /
debenture holders.

c. Debentures - Debentures are instrument issued by companies to raise debt capital. As an
investor, you lend you money to the company, in return for its promise to pay you interest at a
fixed rate (usually payable half yearly on specific dates) and to repay the loan amount on a
specified maturity date say after 5/7/10 years (redemption). Normally specific assets of the
company are held (secured) in favour of debenture holders. This can be liquidated, if the
company is unable to pay the interest or principal amount. Unlike loans, you can buy or sell
these instruments in the market.

d. Bonds- They are broadly similar to debentures. They are issued by companies, financial
institutions, municipalities or government companies and are normally not secured by any
assets of the company (unsecured).

In spite of the many advantages of insurance, it has certain limitations. Due to these
limitations, the benefits of insurance cannot be fully availed by everyone.
a. All Risks Cannot Be Insured
Insurance does not cover all types of risks. Only pure risks, such as fire, accident, theft, or
death, are insurable. Speculative risks, like losses from business competition, gambling, or
stock market fluctuations, cannot be insured because they involve chances of profit as well as
loss.
b. Presence of Insurable Interest Is Necessary
Insurance suffers from the problem of moral hazard. Some insured persons may act
dishonestly by giving false information, exaggerating losses, or making fraudulent claims to
gain undue advantages. Controlling such behavior increases the cost and complexity of
insurance operations.

I. Types of Fire Insurance Policies (any 3)
Fire insurance covers loss or damage caused by fire and allied perils. The main types are:

1. Valued Policy
In a valued policy, the value of the insured property is agreed upon in advance between the
insurer and the insured. In the event of total loss, this agreed value is paid, irrespective of the
market value at the time of loss. This policy is common for items whose value is difficult to
assess after loss, such as artwork or antiques.
2. Unvalued (Open) Policy
Under this policy, the value of the loss is determined after the fire occurs. Compensation is
paid based on the actual loss suffered, subject to the maximum sum insured. This is the most
common form of fire insurance policy.
3. Specific Policy
A specific policy provides insurance for a fixed amount on a particular property. The insurer’s
liability is limited to the sum insured, even if the actual value of the property is higher.
4. Floating Policy
A floating policy covers goods located at different places under a single sum insured. It is
useful for traders and manufacturers with stock spread across various locations. The insured
must periodically declare the location and value of goods.
5. Average Policy
This policy contains an average clause, which means if the property is under‑insured, the
insured must bear a proportionate share of the loss. The insurer pays only a proportion of the
total loss.
6. Replacement or Reinstatement Policy
Under this policy, instead of paying cash compensation, the insurer reinstates or replaces the
damaged property with new property of the same type and value.
II. Types of Marine Insurance Policies (any 3)
Marine insurance covers losses related to marine trade, including ships, cargo, and freight.
1. Voyage Policy
A voyage policy covers risks for a specific voyage, from one port to another. The policy comes
to an end when the ship reaches its destination. It is suitable for occasional shipments.
2. Time Policy
A time policy covers risks for a specific period, usually one year, regardless of the number of
voyages undertaken during that time. It is generally used to insure ships.
3. Mixed or Time‑Voyage Policy
This policy combines the features of both voyage and time policies. The insurance remains
valid either until the voyage is completed or the time period expires, whichever occurs later.
4. Valued Policy
In a valued marine policy, the value of the subject matter is agreed in advance. In case of
total loss, the insurer pays the agreed amount without further valuation.
5. Unvalued Policy

6. Floating Policy
A floating policy covers frequent shipments under one policy for a fixed amount. Details of

b. Primary vs Secondary Market and Reforms   6
Distinction:
Primary Market: Known as the New Issue Market. Companies sell securities for the first time
to the public (e.g., an IPO). The money flows from the investor to the company.
Secondary Market: Known as the Stock Exchange. Investors trade already issued securities
among themselves (e.g., buying Reliance shares on NSE). The money flows between investors.
Recent Reforms:
ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount): In the primary market, this ensures
money leaves the investor's account only after share allotment, reducing fraud.
T+1 Settlement: In the secondary market, trades are now settled within 24 hours, making the
market highly efficient and liquid compared to the older T+2 or T+5 cycles.
Synoptic:
Distinction (3 Marks)
Nature of Issue: The Primary Market (New Issue Market) handles the first-time sale of
securities like IPOs, whereas the Secondary Market (Stock Exchange) facilitates the trading
of existing securities.
Fund Flow: In the Primary Market, capital flows directly from investors to the company for
expansion; in the Secondary Market, money remains within the investor community.

Pricing Mechanism: Prices in the Primary Market are typically determined by management

Deregulation of Interest Rates: Banks are now free to set their own lending and deposit rates

Introduction of LAF (Liquidity Adjustment Facility): This allowed the RBI to manage daily

New Instruments: Introduction of Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS) and TREPS (Tri-party
Repo) to make borrowing safer.
Impact: These reforms have integrated the Indian market with global standards, reduced the
"spread" between borrowing and lending, and ensured that the government can borrow at
