a) Explanation about principle of Electromyography (EMG). --- 03 M
Electromyography (EMG) measures muscle response or electrical activity in response to a nerve’s
stimulation of the muscle. The test is used to help detect neuromuscular abnormalities. During the test,
one or more small needles called electrodes are inserted through the skin into the muscle. The electrical
activity picked up by the electrodes is then displayed on an oscilloscope. An audio-amplifier is used
so the activity can be heard. EMG measures the electrical activity of the muscle during rest, slight
contraction, and forceful contraction. Muscle tissue does not normally produce electrical signals during
rest. When an electrode is inserted, a brief period of activity can be seen on the oscilloscope, but after
that, no signal should be present. After an electrode has been inserted, the subject is asked to contract
the muscle, for example, by lifting or bending the leg. The action potential that this creates on the
oscilloscope provides information about the ability of the muscle to respond when the nerves are
stimulated. As the muscle is contracted more forcefully, more and more muscle fibers are activated,
producing action potentials.
Discussion about applications of EMG. --- 02 M
• To diagnose neuromuscular disorders such as muscle weakness, nerve damage, and
conditions like muscular dystrophy.
• To assess nerve and muscle function in patients with paralysis or injury.
• In rehabilitation to monitor muscle recovery and guide physiotherapy.
• In ergonomics and sports science to study muscle performance and fatigue.
• In prosthetics to control artificial limbs using muscle signals.

Explanation of its operation. --- 03 M
A glucometer is a device used to measure blood glucose levels based on an electrochemical principle.
When a small drop of blood is placed on the test strip, the glucose present in the blood reacts with an
enzyme such as glucose oxidase or glucose dehydrogenase coated on the strip. This reaction produces
electrons, generating an electrical current proportional to the glucose concentration. The electrodes in
the strip detect this current, and the glucometer’s internal circuitry measures and processes the signal.
The processed value is then converted into a readable glucose level and displayed on the screen in
units such as mg/dL. Thus, the glucometer converts a biochemical reaction into an electrical signal to
provide a quick and accurate measurement of blood glucose levels.
d) Limitations of Conventional Audiometry in Infants. --- 02 M
Conventional audiometry (such as pure-tone audiometry) requires active participation from the
subject, like pressing a button or indicating when a sound is heard.
In infants, this is not possible due to:
• Lack of voluntary response: Infants cannot understand or follow instructions.
• Unreliable behavioural responses: Any reactions like head movement are inconsistent and
cannot be accurately interpreted.
•   Inability to maintain attention: Infants cannot focus on test signals for extended periods.
• Subjective nature of the test: Results depend on patient feedback, which is absent in infants.
Because of these limitations, conventional methods cannot provide accurate hearing thresholds in such
subjects.
Suitable Alternative Technique and its justification. --- 03 M
The most appropriate technique is Evoked Response Audiometry (ERA), specifically Auditory
Brainstem Response (ABR/BERA).
The selection is justified because it is an objective method that does not require any active response
from the infant, making it suitable for subjects who cannot communicate or cooperate. It is based on
physiological signals, as it measures the electrical activity (EEG) generated in response to auditory
stimuli. The signals are recorded using electrodes placed on the scalp, ensuring non-invasive
acquisition of brain responses. A signal averaging technique is used to extract consistent evoked
responses from background EEG noise, thereby improving accuracy and reliability. Additionally, this
method helps in assessing the functioning of the auditory pathway, including the cochlea, auditory
nerve, and brainstem, making it highly effective for diagnosing hearing impairments.

b) Analysis of differences between transmittance and reflectance type Pulse oximeter. --- 03 M
A transmittance probe has an LED on one side and a light detector such as photodiode on the other.
The tissue to be imaged commonly a finger or an ear is inserted between the two. A reflectance probe
has the LED and the photodiode on the same side. It must be placed over a point with underlying bone.
Light is emitted by the LED, passes through tissue and blood vessels, reflects off bone, passes through
the tissues again, and is then detected. A significant amount of light will reflect off the skin in the
reflectance setup, and, unlike in the transmittance setup, this light will be detected. Thus, reflectance
probes have a high offset and a lower signal-to-noise ratio than the transmittance probes. Reflectance
setups also require a significantly greater amount of light. Thus, either more LEDs or more photodiodes
need to be used. Transmittance probes are commonly placed on a finger or ear and are very convenient
to attach and remove. Reflectance probes can be placed on the forehead or the sternum. Their
advantage is that, regardless of the patient's size infants to very large adults, the attachment site is
always similar. Both the transmittance and the reflectance probes are used clinically, though the
transmittance probe is more common due to the simplicity of signal analysis and convenience of
attachment.

b) Explanation about working of a spirometer and its application for asthma diagnosis. --- 10 M
A spirometer is a diagnostic instrument used to measure lung function by assessing the volume and
flow of air during breathing. It plays a crucial role in the diagnosis of respiratory conditions such as
asthma, which is characterized by airway narrowing and obstruction. The device works on the principle
of measuring airflow and lung volumes, converting these measurements into electrical signals that can
be analyzed and displayed.
The spirometer consists of a mouthpiece, a flow sensor such as a pneumotachograph or turbine, a
transducer, a signal processing unit, and a display system. The patient breathes through the
mouthpiece, and the flow sensor measures the rate of airflow during inhalation and exhalation. This
airflow is converted into electrical signals by the transducer, which are then processed and displayed
in the form of numerical values and graphical representations called spirograms.
During the operation, the patient is first prepared by being seated comfortably and fitted with a nose
clip to prevent air leakage. The patient is instructed to take a deep breath and then exhale forcefully
and completely into the spirometer. The device records important parameters such as Forced Vital
Capacity (FVC) and Forced Expiratory Volume in one second (FEV₁). These values are essential in
assessing lung function and detecting abnormalities.
In the diagnosis of asthma, spirometry is particularly useful because it can identify airflow limitation.
In asthmatic patients, the airways are inflamed and narrowed, resulting in reduced airflow. This is
reflected as a decrease in FEV₁ and the FEV₁/FVC ratio. To further confirm asthma, a bronchodilator
test is performed in which the patient is given medication to open the airways, and the test is repeated.
A significant improvement in FEV₁ after medication indicates reversible airway obstruction, which is
a hallmark of asthma.
Thus, the spirometer is an effective, non-invasive tool that not only helps in diagnosing asthma but
also in monitoring the progression of the disease and evaluating the effectiveness of treatment.

The GFCI isa circuit breaker in the sense that the hot wire and the neutral wire do not have equal
currents. The circuit will break, that is, the hot wire opens up when an inequality of currents is detected
between the hot and neutral wires. Figure shows where the hot wire and the neutral wire pass through

the net flux equals zero. However, when the current is unbalanced (not equal), a net flux is induced
across the third relay coil of the transformer. The relay then opens up the normally closed switch to an
open position in the hotwire.

b) Explanation about Korotkoff sounds.--- 04 M
Korotkoff sounds are the characteristic sounds heard while measuring blood pressure using a
sphygmomanometer and stethoscope. These sounds are produced due to turbulent blood flow in an
artery as the external pressure applied by the cuff is gradually released. When the cuff is inflated above
systolic pressure, the artery is completely occluded and no blood flows, hence no sound is heard. As

--- page 7 ---
the cuff pressure is slowly reduced, blood begins to flow intermittently through the partially
compressed artery, creating audible sounds known as Korotkoff sounds.
There are five phases of Korotkoff sound heard during cuff deflation.
Phase 1- The onset of sound is indication of systolic pressure. This is the first sound heard when the
falling pressure is slightly less than systolic pressure. The sound is soft in the beginning of the
measurement and increases in intensity.
Phase 2- This is a phase of murmur like sound. It may fade quickly and may not be detected as the cuff
pressure decreases.
Phase 3- This is the phase of the thumping sound and is very loud.
Phase 4- This is close to diastolic pressure and is muffled sound.
Phase 5- With the cuff pressure continually decreasing, this is the phase when the sound disappears
completely. This is usually slightly below the diastolic pressure.
Diagram of blood pressure measurement using sphygmomanometer. --- 03 M
Explanation of its working. --- 03 M
The measurement of blood pressure using Korotkoff sounds involves placing an inflatable cuff around
the upper arm and positioning a stethoscope over the brachial artery. The cuff is inflated to a pressure
higher than the expected systolic pressure, occluding the artery and stopping blood flow. As the cuff
pressure is gradually released, blood starts flowing through the artery in spurts, producing the
Korotkoff sounds. The pressure at which the first sound is heard (Phase I) is recorded as the systolic
blood pressure. As the pressure continues to decrease, the sounds change in character through different
phases. Finally, when the cuff pressure falls below the diastolic pressure, the artery is fully open, blood
flow becomes smooth laminar, and the sounds disappear (Phase V), which is recorded as the diastolic
pressure. This method provides a reliable and widely used technique for non-invasive blood pressure
measurement by correlating the appearance and disappearance of Korotkoff sounds with systolic and
diastolic pressures respectively.

a) Explanation about various settings and operation modes of a pacemaker. --- 10 M
A pacemaker is an electronic medical device used to regulate the heart rate by delivering electrical
impulses to the heart when the natural pacing system fails. The functioning of a pacemaker depends
on various settings and modes, which determine how the device senses cardiac activity and delivers
stimulation. These modes are commonly described using a standardized coding system that indicates
the chamber paced, chamber sensed, response to sensing, programmability, and rate modulation.
The most basic modes include asynchronous modes, such as fixed-rate pacing, where the pacemaker
delivers impulses at a constant rate irrespective of the heart’s natural activity. An example is the VOO
mode, in which the ventricle is paced continuously without sensing intrinsic cardiac signals. These
modes are simple but may lead to competition between natural and artificial rhythms, making them
less commonly used except in specific situations like surgical procedures.
In contrast, demand or synchronous modes are more advanced and widely used. In these modes, the
pacemaker monitors the heart’s intrinsic activity and delivers pulses only when necessary. For
example, in VVI mode, the pacemaker paces and senses the ventricle and inhibits pacing when a
natural heartbeat is detected. These modes conserve battery life and reduce unnecessary stimulation.
More sophisticated are the dual chamber modes, such as DDD and DVI, where both atrium and
ventricle are involved. These modes coordinate the timing between atrial and ventricular contractions,
closely mimicking the natural conduction system of the heart. They improve cardiac efficiency by
maintaining proper atrioventricular synchrony, which is especially beneficial in patients with
conduction block between atria and ventricles.
Another important category is rate-responsive modes, such as VVIR, where the pacemaker adjusts the
pacing rate based on the patient’s activity level. Sensors within the pacemaker detect physical activity
or physiological parameters like respiration or body movement and increase the heart rate accordingly.
This feature is particularly useful for patients whose heart rate does not increase naturally during
exercise.
Pacemakers also include several programmable settings, such as output voltage, pulse duration,
sensitivity, and refractory period. These settings are adjusted based on patient requirements to ensure
effective pacing while minimizing energy consumption and avoiding inappropriate sensing or
stimulation.

a) Discussion about the working of cochlear implants used for curing sensorineural deafness along
with neat diagram. --- 10 M
The cure for sensorineural deafness (Presbycusis) is cochlear implants. Sensory-neural deafness can
be caused either by cochlear damage or by damage within the auditory nerve or to the neurons of the
central auditory system. The hair cells are sensory cells that transduce mechanical motion into signals
that can be recognized by auditory neurons. The auditory neurons carry information from the hair cells
to the cochlear nucleus in the brainstem and, via the cochlear nucleus, to higher nuclei in the brain.
The normal cochlea and the associated neurons of the central auditory system provide information
about both the frequency content and intensity of the auditory signal. Information is conveyed to the
acoustic nerve about frequency content by the mechanically tuned properties of the basilar membrane.
The inner hair cells, which connect to the vast majority of afferent neurons, are thought to be the
sensory cells of the cochlea whereas the role of the outer hair cells is still under investigation. The
location of hair cells along the cochlea determines their optimal response to frequency: hair cells at the
apex are responsive to low frequencies, while hair cells at the base are responsive to high frequencies.
The distribution of frequencies along the spiral is logarithmic. If the damage to the auditory system is
peripheral in the inner ear, then a cochlear implant can be used. In the general design of a cochlear
implant, the sound is decomposed into frequency bands of use for the transmission and reception of
speech, and critical features of the signals within those frequency bands are delivered to auditory
neurons via an array of electrodes.

a) Discussion and justification of various causes for the ECG waveform showing excessive noise
and unclear peaks. --- 05 M
An ECG waveform with excessive noise and unclear peaks P, QRS and T indicates poor signal quality.
Since ECG signals are very small in millivolts, they are highly susceptible to interference and artifacts.
The possible causes for this are:
• Poor patient preparation
• Environmental interference such as disturbance or noise around the ECG machine or the ECG
machine is too close to transformers or large metal conductors carrying a 60-Hz signal.
• Defective electrodes or poor electrode-skin contact or interface.
• Defective lead wires or ECG cables.
• Electrode misplacement on the body.
• Faulty acquisition module, computer system, or instrumentation error.
All the above factors reduce the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the ECG. Thus, actual cardiac signal
becomes difficult to distinguish from noise Important features like P wave, QRS complex, and ST
segment may be hidden. This leads to misinterpretation or incorrect diagnosis.
b) Comparison between analog and digital hearing aid. --- 05 M
Analog and digital hearing aids differ significantly in function, features, and benefits. Analog hearing
aids amplify all sounds uniformly, providing continuous amplification with minimal processing. They
are simple, affordable, and suitable for individuals with mild to moderate hearing loss. However, they
lack advanced noise reduction and customization features, making it difficult to distinguish speech in
noisy environments. Additionally, they have a higher risk of feedback whistling sounds due to basic
amplification.
In contrast, digital hearing aids convert sound into digital signals, allowing for advanced signal
processing. They offer features such as adaptive noise cancellation, feedback suppression, and
directional microphones, which improve speech clarity in noisy settings. Digital models are highly
customizable based on the user’s specific hearing profile and provide better control over sound
adjustments. Many also include Bluetooth and wireless connectivity, enabling integration with
smartphones and other devices. However, these advanced features result in higher costs and increased
power consumption compared to analog devices. Overall, digital hearing aids provide superior sound
quality and enhanced functionality, making them suitable for a wide range of hearing impairments.

nature of blood flow. A light source is placed on one side of the finger, and a light sensitive transducer
like LDR (Cadmium Sulfide (CdS) cell) or a photo diode or a phototransistor, on the other side. By
monitoring variations in the output of the transducer an indication of blood flow in the finger is
obtained.
A typical Pulse Oximeter uses the basic principle of a pair of small LEDs operating at two different
wavelengths; one red LED with a wavelength of 660nm, the other, an infrared LED with a wavelength
of 910nm. The LEDs are designed to be placed opposite a photodiode that detects the light from the
LEDs. As the amount of blood in the capillaries depends on the actual blood pressure, which varies
around the heart the heart pulse cycle, the heart rate can also be measured. The two wavelengths are
chosen for the reason that deoxygenated hemoglobin has a higher absorption at around 660nm and at
910m oxygenated hemoglobin has a higher absorption. The oxygenated hemoglobin allows red light
to transmit through and absorbs more infrared light while the deoxygenated hemoglobin allows
infrared to transmit through and absorbs more red light. Usually, a finger is placed between the source
(LEDs) and the receiver (photodiode) acting as a translucent site with good blood flow. The photodiode
produces current linearly proportional to the intensity of light striking it. A photodiode cannot
distinguish between red and infrared light, but to accommodate this, the microprocessor system
alternately turns each LED on and off. The pulse oximeter repeatedly samples the photodiode output
while the red LED is on, while the infrared LED is on and while both are off. By sampling with both
LED's off, the pulse oximeter is able to subtract any ambient light that is present.

Discussion about the physiological conditions under which an implantable pacemaker can be
recommended. --- 05 M
An implantable pacemaker is recommended under physiological conditions where the heart’s natural
electrical conduction system fails to maintain an adequate rate or rhythm to support normal circulation.
• One of the primary conditions is bradycardia, where the heart rate is abnormally low, leading
to symptoms such as fatigue, dizziness, or fainting. This may occur due to dysfunction of the
sinoatrial (SA) node, commonly referred to as sick sinus syndrome, where the natural
pacemaker of the heart fails to generate impulses properly.
• Another important condition is atrioventricular (AV) block, where there is impaired conduction
of electrical signals from the atria to the ventricles. In complete heart block (third-degree AV
block), there is no communication between atrial and ventricular activity, resulting in a
dangerously slow and unreliable ventricular rhythm. In such cases, a pacemaker is essential to
maintain coordinated heart function.
• Pacemakers are also recommended in patients with bundle branch block or other conduction
abnormalities that disrupt the normal sequence of ventricular activation. Additionally,
conditions such as atrial fibrillation with slow ventricular response may require pacing support
to maintain an adequate heart rate.
• In some patients, the heart may not respond appropriately to physiological demands, such as
during exercise. This condition, known as chronotropic incompetence, prevents the heart rate
from increasing when needed, and a rate-responsive pacemaker can help restore normal
function.
• Pacemakers may also be used in cases of recurrent syncope (fainting) caused by transient
pauses in heart rhythm or hypersensitive carotid sinus syndrome. Furthermore, in advanced
heart failure, specialized pacemakers used in cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) help
improve the coordination of ventricular contractions.

relationship between decelerations and the onset and the end of the uterine contraction, decelerations
are classified into three main groups: variable, early and late.

a) Block diagram of Bekesy audiometer. --- 04 M
Explanation of its working. --- 06 M
George Van Bekesy, a Hungarian scientist, designed an automatic audiometric testing method for
plotting the hearing threshold based on the patient’s signal. A principal feature of the method,
differentiating it from conventional pure-tone audiometric techniques, is the interdependence of the
patient’s response and stimulus intensity. Responses govern intensity and are affected by changes they
introduce in it. An audiogram traced by the Bekesy method represents the absolute threshold values at
all frequencies in the range tested. In addition, it shows the difference, in decibels, between levels at
which the patient just hears a signal of increasing intensity and those at which he just ceases to hear
the signal when its intensity is decreasing. This latter characteristic often varies significantly with the
type of hearing impairment and can aid in establishing the site of lesion within the auditory system.
On the basis of the audiograms, one can easily separate the conduction and perceptive hearing
deficiencies from each other. Audiometers Bekesy are relatively simple for the patient to operate. The
instrument generates a pure-tone signal, which is presented to him through an air-conduction earphone.
The subject is told to press a switch when the tone is heard and to release the switch when it is not
heard. This switch controls the motor-driven attenuator of the audiometer: when it is pressed, signal
intensity decreases and when it is released, signal intensity increases. A pen connected to the attenuator
traces a continuous record of the patient’s intensity adjustments on an audiogram chart, producing a
graphic representation of the subject’s threshold. The test signal may be presented in a variety of ways,
each suited to the investigation of a particular problem.

The incubator consists of a transparent cabinet in which the baby is kept in a neutral environment for
medical care. Neutral means, that the surrounding air has the optimal temperature and moisture, and
the baby only needs minimum of energy to maintain normal body temperature. The infant incubator
controls the temperature, controls the humidity, can deliver additional oxygen, protects against
infections/diseases and protects against noise. In order maintain the body temperature of the baby (36

In a typical infant incubator, the baby lies on a mattress in a closed, transparent cabinet. The ambient
air is fully controlled by the incubator. The cabinet has portholes so that the nurse has access to the
baby without opening the cabinet. Under the cabinet is the compartment with the technology and the
control panel. The whole incubator is mounted on a trolley.
All infant incubators work on the same principle. A fan blows filtered ambient air over a heating
element and a water container. Through a control valve additional oxygen can be supplied to the air.
The moistened, heated and enriched air now flows into the above cabinet with the baby. One part of
the air escapes from the cabinet through vent holes, another part gets back into the air processing.
The cabinet is made from transparent Plexiglas (Perspex). A hinged hood can be opened to put the
baby in or take it out. Through portholes in the front the nurse has easy access to the baby without
letting out the warm air by opening the hood. After the desired temperature is set by the nurse, it is
kept stable automatically by the incubator. Humidity and oxygen concentration are usually controlled

Explanation of its operation. --- 04 M
HRV analysis provides great insights into the functioning of the heart, and many cardio pathological
inferences can be drawn based on the inputs. The functional block diagram for HRV evaluation process
is represented in the figure below. The HRV analysis process begins with the acquisition of ECG
signals in real time. The major modules for HRV analysis include the preprocessing block, the learning
module, the detection and classification block, and the HRV analysis module. These stages ensure
selection of reasonably noise-free ECG data segments and automated detection and classification of
ECG features for further analysis.
The first stage of data preprocessing module comprises of signal amplification, band-pass filtering,
artifact removal, and digitization of the signal. The learning module does initialization of a threshold
value for QRS peak detection based on averaging concept and also initializes the parameters required
for the classification of ECG data. The RR intervals are also averaged for final calculation. After the
learning phase is over, the algorithm designed for detection based on the QRS detector concept by Pan-
Tompkins is utilized to find the locations of the peak QRS and then the classification of the ECG
waveform is performed as normal or abnormal heartbeat. Classification is based on the rhythmic
behavior of heart and the amplitudes related to the QRS peak. Standard RR interval is evaluated by the
interval in between QRS peaks of normal beats. RR interval calculation has its implication in analyzing
the HRV. HRV analysis is accomplished using linear or nonlinear techniques. Linear methods include
analysis both in time and in frequency domain. Cardio pathological and diagnostic inferences can be
drawn from HRV analysis.
