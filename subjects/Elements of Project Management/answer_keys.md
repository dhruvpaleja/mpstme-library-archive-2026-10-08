b   Analyze the uses and significance of risk register in relation to project risk planning &
implementation of response to risks in medium size and big/mega projects
Answer:
-Risk register is a document that contains the results of various risk management
processes and that is often displayed in a table or spreadsheet format
-Risk register is a tool for documenting potential risk events and related information   [10]
-In other words Risk Register is a central document in project risk management that
records all identified risks, their characteristics, ownership, planned responses, and
current status   -In medium-size and big/mega projects, where uncertainty, complexity,
and stakeholder involvement are high, the risk register plays a critical role in both risk
planning and implementation of risk responses
********** 4 **********
--- page 5 ---
Key Uses in Risk Planning
-Systematic Risk Identification:
The risk register captures all identified risks, including their sources and categories
(technical, financial, legal, environmental, etc.), ensuring no major risk area is
overlooked
-Risk Analysis and Prioritization:
Each risk is assessed for probability and impact, allowing prioritization.
In big and mega projects, this helps focus management attention and resources on
high-exposure risks
-Decision-making Support:
By linking risks to project objectives (cost, time, scope, quality), the risk register
supports informed trade-off analysis during planning.
-Planning for Risk Ownership:
Assignment of risk owners ensures accountability, which is essential in medium and
large projects with multiple functional teams.
Significance in Large Projects
In mega projects, incomplete or informal risk planning often results in cost overruns and
delays. A detailed risk register provides visibility and traceability, making risk planning
more realistic and proactive
Beyond planning, the risk register becomes an active control instrument during project
execution.
Key Uses in Risk Response Implementation
-Link between Risks and Responses:
The register documents response strategies (avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept) along with
contingency and fallback plans, enabling systematic implementation.
-Monitoring Response Effectiveness:
Risk status (open, closed, escalated) is regularly updated, allowing project managers to
analyze whether response actions are effective or need revision.
-Early Warning Mechanism:
In medium and large projects, changes in risk indicators help in early detection of
emerging threats, preventing escalation into crises.
-Management of Secondary Risks:
Implementation of responses often creates new risks. The risk register ensures these
secondary risks are identified and analyzed promptly
Significance of Risk Register
-Without a risk register, risk management remains reactive and fragmented
-With a well-maintained register, risk management becomes continuous, transparent, and
measurable
- It acts as a living document, linking planning assumptions with real-time execution
outcomes
Introduction to risk register: 02 Marks
Key uses in risk planning: 03 Marks
Key uses in risk implementation: 03 Marks
Significance: 02 Marks

b   Define WBS- Work Breakdown Structures and examine following statement
“Components or full project failures, when they do occurs, can often be traced to a
poorly developed or nonexistent WBS”
Answer:
-PMBOK defines Work Breakdown Structures as a deliverable oriented grouping of
activities that organizes and defines the total scope of project
-WBS forms the foundation for effective project planning, execution, and control
-The given statement highlights the critical role of WBS in preventing project failures
1. Role of WBS in Defining Project Scope
A well-developed WBS ensures that all project deliverables and activities are clearly
identified. If the WBS is poorly developed or missing:
•   Important tasks may be omitted
• Scope becomes unclear or incomplete
This often leads to component failures, as certain required outputs are never planned or
executed properly.
2. Impact on Cost and Schedule Estimation
The WBS is the basis for accurate time and cost estimation. Without a proper WBS:
•   Efforts are underestimated or duplicated
• Resource allocation becomes inefficient
As a result, projects face schedule delays and cost overruns, which are common causes
of overall project failure.
[08]
3. Effect on Responsibility and Accountability
Each work package in a WBS is usually assigned to a responsible individual or team. In
the absence of a clear WBS:
•   Responsibilities are poorly defined
• Tasks fall through gaps or are assumed to be someone else’s responsibility
This lack of accountability often causes partial or full project failures.
4. Influence on Risk Identification and Control
A detailed WBS helps in identifying risks at the activity or component level. A weak or
non-existent WBS limits visibility into potential problem areas, making risk management
reactive rather than proactive. This increases the chances of unforeseen failures.
5. Impact on Monitoring and Control
Performance measurement tools such as progress tracking and earned value analysis
depend on a structured WBS. Without it:
•   Progress cannot be accurately measured
•   Deviations are detected too late
This leads to loss of control and eventually project failure.
-The statement is valid because a poorly developed or non-existent WBS undermines
almost every aspect of project management, including scope definition, estimation,
responsibility assignment, risk management, and control. Consequently, many
component-level and full project failures can indeed be traced back to weaknesses in the
********** 7 **********

WBS   -Therefore, a well-structured WBS is essential for successful project

c   Relate Tuckman’s model of team building and building project team in project
management
Answer:
-Tuckman’s model of team building explains how teams develop through distinct stages,
namely Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, and Adjourning
-This model closely relates to the process of building and managing a project team in
project management
1. Forming Stage and Project Team Formation
In project management, the forming stage corresponds to team acquisition and
orientation. Team members come together, roles and responsibilities are introduced, and
project objectives are explained. The project manager plays a key role by providing
direction and establishing expectations.
2. Storming Stage and Team Development Challenges
During this stage, conflicts may arise due to differences in opinions, work styles, or
misunderstood roles. In project teams, this is common when members start working on
tasks. The project manager must apply conflict management and communication skills to
resolve issues and keep the team focused on project goals.
3. Norming Stage and Team Cohesion
[04]
As the project progresses, team members begin to cooperate, trust each other, and accept
their roles. In this stage, project teams develop effective working relationships, follow
defined processes, and improve coordination, enhancing overall project performance.
4. Performing Stage and Project Execution
In the performing stage, the project team operates at high efficiency and productivity.
Members are motivated and capable of handling tasks independently. This stage aligns
with successful project execution, where the team delivers project outputs with minimal
supervision.
5. Adjourning Stage and Project Closure
At project completion, the team is disbanded, and members move on to new
assignments. The project manager ensures proper project closure, documentation, and
recognition of team efforts, consistent with the adjourning stage.
-Tuckman’s model provides a useful framework for understanding team behaviour
during different stages of a project
-By relating these stages to building a project team, project managers can apply
appropriate leadership and management strategies, thereby improving team effectiveness
and project success.

a   Define ‘Quantitative Risk’ and Explain ‘Identify risks’ as one of the seven processes
in risk management in project
Answer:
-Quantitative risk refers to the process of assigning numerical values to potential risks,
using data and statistical methods to assess the probability of a risk occurring and its
[08]
potential impact on a project's cost, schedule, or scope, essentially translating the risk
into measurable terms
-According to PMBOK (Project Management Body of Knowledge), Identify Risks is one
of the seven key processes of Project Risk Management
Identify Risks’ Process in Project Risk Management (PMBOK-Aligned)
********** 8 **********
--- page 9 ---
1. Purpose of the Process
The primary purpose of Identify Risks is to recognize all potential events or
conditions that could positively or negatively impact project objectives such as
scope, time, cost, quality, and performance.
2. Inputs Used for Risk Identification
This process uses important project documents such as the project charter, project
management plan, scope baseline, schedule, cost estimates, stakeholder register,
and historical information from similar projects.
3. Techniques for Identifying Risks
PMBOK recommends techniques such as expert judgment, brainstorming,
interviews, checklists, SWOT analysis, and document review. These techniques
help systematically identify a wide range of potential risks.
4. Stakeholder and Team Involvement
Risk identification involves project team members, stakeholders, subject-matter
experts, and sponsors. Their experience and perspectives increase the accuracy
and completeness of identified risks.
5. Risk Register as Output
The main output of this process is the Risk Register, which includes a list of
identified risks along with their causes, potential impacts, and initial risk owners.
This register becomes a key document for subsequent risk processes.
6. Continuous and Iterative Nature
PMBOK emphasizes that Identify Risks is not a one-time activity. It is performed
throughout the project as new risks may emerge and existing risks may change
over time.

-For medium-size and large software projects, estimation models must handle
[08]
complexity, multiple components, technology factors, and team productivity. Among the
various models available, COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model)—
especially Intermediate and Detailed COCOMO, and Function Point Analysis (FPA) are

********** 9 **********

Reasons-
1. COCOMO Model (Intermediate and Detailed)
COCOMO is highly suitable for medium and big projects because it is based on
empirical data and considers multiple cost drivers.
Reasons for suitability:
•   Intermediate COCOMO includes cost drivers such as product complexity,

Well-suited for projects with relatively stable and well-defined requirements

business and enterprise applications.
Reasons for suitability:
•   Estimation is based on functional requirements, not lines of code, making it
technology-independent.
•   Suitable for projects with changing technologies or mixed programming
environments.
• Allows early estimation during the requirement analysis phase, useful for large
projects where early planning is critical.
• Supports better comparison and benchmarking across projects.

a   List and briefly explain steps to avoid negative consequences of project
termination/closure
Answer:
-Project termination or closure, if not handled properly, can lead to financial loss,
stakeholder dissatisfaction, legal issues, and loss of organizational learning.
Steps to avoid or
minimize negative consequences:
1. Formal Project Closure Documentation
Officially document the reasons for termination or completion, approval from
management, and closure decisions. This avoids ambiguity and future disputes
regarding the project status.
2. Clear Communication to Stakeholders
All stakeholders, including clients, team members, suppliers, and sponsors,   [08]
should be informed clearly and timely about the project closure to avoid
confusion, rumors, or dissatisfaction.
3. Financial Settlement and Account Closure
Ensure proper settlement of all financial matters such as payments to vendors,
contractors, and staff. Unused funds should be returned, and accounts should be
formally closed.
4. Proper Resource Release and Reassignment
Project resources (human, equipment, and facilities) should be released in a
planned manner and reassigned to other projects to avoid idle resources and
employee dissatisfaction.
5. Documentation of Lessons Learned
Capture and document lessons learned, including reasons for success or failure,

so that similar mistakes are avoided in future projects and organizational learning
is enhanced.
6. Completion of Legal and Contractual Obligations
Review and close all contracts, ensure compliance with legal requirements, and
obtain formal acceptance where required to prevent legal complications.
7. Secure and Archive Project Records
All project documents, reports, designs, and data should be properly archived for
future reference, audits, or legal needs.
8. Post-Project Review and Evaluation
Conduct a post-termination or post-project review to assess performance, identify
gaps, and suggest improvements for future project management practices.

Answer:
Importance of Project Budget as a Tool for Cost Control and Decision Making in Project
Management:
-A project budget is a detailed financial plan that estimates the total cost required to
complete a project within a defined scope and time
-In medium-size and large/mega projects, budgeting plays a critical role in effective cost
control and managerial decision-making due to the scale, complexity, and resource
intensity involved.
1. Foundation for Cost Planning and Control
The project budget provides a baseline against which actual costs are measured.
By comparing planned and actual expenditures, project managers can identify
cost overruns early and apply corrective actions. This is especially important in
large projects where small deviations can lead to significant financial losses.
2. Supports Informed Decision-Making
A well-prepared budget helps management evaluate different alternatives such as
technology selection, outsourcing decisions, scheduling options, and resource
allocation. In mega projects, decisions like adding manpower, changing
suppliers, or re-scoping work are guided by budgetary impact analysis.   [08]
3. Efficient Resource Allocation
Budgets ensure optimal utilization of resources such as labor, materials,
equipment, and subcontractors. In medium and large projects with multiple
activities running in parallel, budgeting helps prioritize critical tasks and prevents
wasteful spending.
4. Risk Management and Contingency Planning
Large projects face higher uncertainty and risks. Budgeting includes contingency
reserves for unforeseen events such as delays, price escalation, or technical
issues, helping maintain financial stability throughout the project lifecycle.
5. Performance Measurement and Control
Budgets enable the use of cost control tools such as variance analysis and earned
value management (EVM) to assess project performance. This improves
accountability and transparency in large project environments.
6. Stakeholder Confidence and Financial Discipline
A realistic and controlled budget enhances confidence among stakeholders,
sponsors, and investors. It demonstrates financial discipline, which is crucial for
approval, funding continuity, and long-term organizational credibility.
-In medium-size and big/mega projects, the project budget acts as a central tool for

-Tuckman’s model of team building explains how teams develop through distinct stages,
namely Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, and Adjourning
-This model closely relates to the process of building and managing a project team in
project management
1. Forming Stage and Project Team Formation
In project management, the forming stage corresponds to team acquisition and
orientation. Team members come together, roles and responsibilities are introduced, and
project objectives are explained. The project manager plays a key role by providing
direction and establishing expectations.
2. Storming Stage and Team Development Challenges
During this stage, conflicts may arise due to differences in opinions, work styles, or

resolve issues and keep the team focused on project goals.

[04]
As the project progresses, team members begin to cooperate, trust each other, and accept
their roles. In this stage, project teams develop effective working relationships, follow
defined processes, and improve coordination, enhancing overall project performance.
4. Performing Stage and Project Execution
In the performing stage, the project team operates at high efficiency and productivity.
Members are motivated and capable of handling tasks independently. This stage aligns
with successful project execution, where the team delivers project outputs with minimal
supervision.
5. Adjourning Stage and Project Closure
At project completion, the team is disbanded, and members move on to new
assignments. The project manager ensures proper project closure, documentation, and
recognition of team efforts, consistent with the adjourning stage.
-Tuckman’s model provides a useful framework for understanding team behaviour
during different stages of a project
-By relating these stages to building a project team, project managers can apply
appropriate leadership and management strategies, thereby improving team effectiveness

c   Relate Tuckman’s model of team building and building project team in project

Answer:
-Tuckman’s model of team building explains how teams develop through distinct stages,
namely Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, and Adjourning
-This model closely relates to the process of building and managing a project team in
project management
1. Forming Stage and Project Team Formation
In project management, the forming stage corresponds to team acquisition and
orientation. Team members come together, roles and responsibilities are introduced, and
project objectives are explained. The project manager plays a key role by providing
direction and establishing expectations.
2. Storming Stage and Team Development Challenges
During this stage, conflicts may arise due to differences in opinions, work styles, or

resolve issues and keep the team focused on project goals.

[04]

their roles. In this stage, project teams develop effective working relationships, follow
defined processes, and improve coordination, enhancing overall project performance.
4. Performing Stage and Project Execution
In the performing stage, the project team operates at high efficiency and productivity.
Members are motivated and capable of handling tasks independently. This stage aligns
with successful project execution, where the team delivers project outputs with minimal
supervision.
5. Adjourning Stage and Project Closure
At project completion, the team is disbanded, and members move on to new
assignments. The project manager ensures proper project closure, documentation, and
recognition of team efforts, consistent with the adjourning stage.
-Tuckman’s model provides a useful framework for understanding team behaviour
during different stages of a project
-By relating these stages to building a project team, project managers can apply
appropriate leadership and management strategies, thereby improving team effectiveness
and project success.

-This model closely relates to the process of building and managing a project team in

supervision.

assignments. The project manager ensures proper project closure, documentation, and
recognition of team efforts, consistent with the adjourning stage.

-This model closely relates to the process of building and managing a project team in

In project management, the forming stage corresponds to team acquisition and

[04]

with successful project execution, where the team delivers project outputs with minimal
supervision.
5. Adjourning Stage and Project Closure
At project completion, the team is disbanded, and members move on to new
assignments. The project manager ensures proper project closure, documentation, and
recognition of team efforts, consistent with the adjourning stage.

issues, helping maintain financial stability throughout the project lifecycle.

accountability and transparency in large project environments.

-Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA) FMEA is the application of a

-It is straightforward and extensively used, particularly in engineering, and is

monitoring/control systems and “10” means it is practically certain that failure
will not be detected in time to avoid or mitigate it.
5. Find the Risk Priority Number (RPN) where RPN S L D.
6. Consider ways to reduce the S, L, and D for each cause of failure with a
significantly high RPN.

2. Evaluate the severity (S) of the impact of each type of failure on a 10-point

monitoring/control systems and “10” means it is practically certain that failure
will not be detected in time to avoid or mitigate it.
5. Find the Risk Priority Number (RPN) where RPN S L D.
6. Consider ways to reduce the S, L, and D for each cause of failure with a
significantly high RPN.

b   Explain in detail ‘Risk efficiency’ when one studies project risk management.
Answer:
-Risk efficiency is a key concept in project risk management that refers to achieving
project objectives by maintaining an optimal balance between risk exposure and expected
benefits   -It emphasizes managing risks in
a cost-effective and value-oriented manner, rather than trying to eliminate all risks
-In real projects, especially large and complex ones, risk is unavoidable   -Risk

-Risk efficiency plays an important role throughout the risk management process, including
risk identification, analysis, response planning, and monitoring
-Risk efficiency helps project managers prioritize significant risks based on probability and

project resources such as time, cost, and manpower
-Risk efficiency concept is often explained using a risk–return trade-off, Very low risk

-Risk efficiency aims to operate at an optimal risk level, where project performance and
returns are maximized
-Risk efficiency also guides the selection of appropriate risk response strategies—
avoidance, mitigation, transfer, or acceptance—so that response costs do not exceed the
potential loss   –Risk efficiency supports better
decision-making, improved project value, and exploitation of positive risks (opportunities).
Risk efficiency is closely linked with organizational risk appetite and risk tolerance,
ensuring that project risks remain within acceptable limits
Thus, risk efficiency ensures that risks are managed wisely, resources are optimally used,
and project objectives of cost, time, quality, and performance are achieved successfully

-Project budgeting is the process of estimating, allocating, and controlling the costs
associated with a specific project
Significance of Budgeting in Project Management-
1. Cost Estimation and Planning
Budgeting helps in estimating the total cost of the project, including materials,
labor, equipment, and overheads. This enables realistic financial planning and helps
the project manager apply cost estimates while planning project activities.
2. Effective Resource Allocation
A well-prepared budget ensures that funds are allocated appropriately to various
project tasks and phases. This prevents over allocation or underutilization of
resources and ensures smooth execution of the project.
3. Cost Control and Monitoring
Budgeting provides a financial baseline against which actual project expenditures
can be compared. This helps in monitoring costs, detecting deviations, and taking
[08]
corrective actions to keep the project within approved financial limits.
4. Decision Making Support
Budget information supports managerial decision-making, such as choosing
between alternative methods, prioritizing activities, or approving scope changes,
based on their cost implications.
5. Risk Identification and Management
Through budgeting, potential financial risks such as cost overruns or price
fluctuations can be identified in advance. Contingency reserves included in the
budget help in managing unforeseen expenses.
6. Performance Measurement
Budgeting enables evaluation of project performance using cost-related indicators
like cost variance and cost performance index, helping assess efficiency and
effectiveness of project execution.   -Budgeting plays a vital role in
ensuring financial discipline, efficient resource utilization, and successful
completion of projects within approved cost constraints.

-It is straightforward and extensively used, particularly in engineering, and is

1. List the possible ways a project might fail.
2. Evaluate the severity (S) of the impact of each type of failure on a 10-point
scale where “1”is “no effect” and “10” is “very severe.”
3. For each cause of failure, estimate the likelihood (L) of its occurrence on a 10-

a 10-point scale,“1” means detectability is almost certain using normal
monitoring/control systems and “10” means it is practically certain that failure
will not be detected in time to avoid or mitigate it.
5. Find the Risk Priority Number (RPN) where RPN S L D.
6. Consider ways to reduce the S, L, and D for each cause of failure with a
significantly high RPN.
