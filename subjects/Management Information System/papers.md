mobile number the same as before. The Federal Commission Ruling had recently
mandated the telecom service providers to ensure that this service should be provided to
all customers.

Jack had placed his service request by calling at the Customer Care Help Line. After a
week of placing his service request, when he visited the H.T website, he was greeted
with a message : “We could not find a service request for this number in the system”

Over the next two months, Jack made 15 to 20 phone calls the H.T., send nearly as
many emails, and spent 60 hours over the phone dealing with customer care
representatives (CSR) or waiting on hold — with the line often going dead when Horizon’s
customer service lines became overloaded.

After being routed all over the company, Jack finally discovered what was going on. A

S major CRM system had crashed during an Upgrade, and CSRs could not set up or
access new accounts. The system breakdowns, which continued through June 2004,
swamped Horizon’s systems, gridlocked customer service phone banks, and sent
furious customers scurrying to other providers.

Horizon’s CRM upgrade was hamstrung from almost the beginning by rumors of
outsourcing deals and future payoffs. Horizon undertook a difficult upgrade that affected
roughly 15 systems just before it was faced with an immovable deadline — the Federal
mandated December 15 number portability date. Horizon was forced to move forward
even as it became apparent that its upgrade would not be completed in time.

When H.T. began its CRM upgrade in 2003, it had slipped from unquestioned market
leader to middle of the pack. Its overall market share slid from 25% in 2001 to 15% in
2003 and competition from company, FirstTalk was showing up in the numbers.

H.T had been providing services using the TDMA technology but realized the potential of
GSM (Global System for Mobile communications) since TDMA could not handle data
transfers over cell phones — the next big thing for business customers.

GSM was a great opportunity for H.T but also a huge CRM challenge. The company had
to convince its old customers to move off TDMA, which worked as well as most other
carriers’ networks for voice calls, onto GSM. But customers didn’t buy the pitch.

Everyone at H.T. agreed that the company would keep its existing customers and add
more new ones if it’s CSR’s could handle more calls and get customers up and running
faster. CSR’s currently needed about 20 mins, on avg, to work through 5-6 screens that
fetched information from about 15 legacy systems, say former employees.

The CRM would be the front-end of its customer service process. The back-end,
however, was a complex mishmash of systems. Telco billing systems were stuffed full of
different rate plans and arcane metering processes. Systems that tracked calls and set
up new phone numbers communicated with hundreds of thousands of different
telephone switches around the country and the world.

To work with H.T., the CRM solution had to be highly customized. Though the software
came with integration tools, consultants usually reported to writing point-to-point scripts
to hook the systems together. In spring 2003, the company decided to use the upgraded
version of the CRM system to support the GSM & GPRS (General Packet Radio
Service) while the TDMA customers would continue to be serviced from the older legacy
CRM system until they could be moved over to GSM and the new CRM system.

Though the upgrade promised gains, getting there was neither simple nor speedy. The
back-end systems-integration work was so complex that it wasn’t unusual to see teams
of 20 or more people assigned to write connections for a single system.

“In other projects I’ve worked on, the project manager would freeze code while the
teams did revisions to their pieces so you could test everything against the same code
base.” says a former employee.

Meanwhile rumors of layoffs and offshore outsourcing began swirling and people started
looking for other work. “I know | was looking for other work when | should have been
testing” says another former employee of H.T.

As the Dec 15 deadline approached, the upgrade was going so badly that there were
talks on the project of trying to roll back to the older CRM version before the porting
date. But project managers hadn’t preserved enough of the old system to make it
feasible. Project members estimated that the team needed two or three months of bug
fixing and testing to get the system running reliably.
