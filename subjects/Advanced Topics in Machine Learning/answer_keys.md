using previously generated outputs and encoder representations.

ability to capture complex dependencies.

1) Why traditional supervised learning may fail with small datasets
Overfitting / high variance: Deep models have many parameters; with few examples they memorize

Unreliable validation/tuning: Small sets make hyperparameter selection noisy.
Domain shift & covariate variation: Real-world test-time conditions (weather, occlusion) produce
examples not covered by tiny training sets.
Because of these, standard supervised training yields models with poor calibration, brittle decision boundaries,
and low sample efficiency.

Signature verification is fundamentally a verification (same vs different) task, often with few examples

Answer: The given scenario represents zero-shot learning which is a learning paradigm where a model
performs tasks it has never been trained on, without any additional fine-tuning or retraining. In this case, the neural
machine translation model is trained on English-French sentence pairs but is directly tested on English-Hindi
translation. Since the model was never exposed to Hindi data during training, it is expected to generalize its learned
language mapping abilities to a completely unseen language pair.
This fits the characteristics of zero-shot learning because the model leverages the shared semantic representation
of languages it learned during training. It understands the meaning of English sentences and attempts to generate
semantically equivalent sentences in Hindi, even though Hindi data was not included during training. Such
generalization is possible when models are trained in a multilingual or cross-lingual setup where knowledge can
transfer implicitly between languages.
In contrast, transfer learning would require retraining or fine-tuning the model using some English-Hindi parallel
data to adapt it to the new language pair. Zero-shot learning, however, eliminates this need for retraining by directly
applying existing knowledge to unseen tasks, making it a more efficient but challenging approach that tests the
model’s ability to generalize beyond its training distribution.
b)   Explain why Siamese Networks are designed using two identical subnetworks instead of using a single
network. Discuss how this architectural choice benefits the network's performance and learning.
Answer:
********** 1 **********
--- page 2 ---
● Siamese Networks use two identical subnetworks (same architecture and shared weights) so that both inputs
are transformed by the exact same feature extractor. This enforces a fair, symmetric comparison: any
difference at the output comes from the inputs, not from different encoders.
● Weight sharing cuts parameters roughly in half versus two separate encoders, which reduces overfitting
and improves generalization, especially when training data is limited or classes are imbalanced.
● A shared encoder places all inputs in a common embedding space where distances are meaningful. Losses
like contrastive loss or triplet loss can then pull similar pairs together and push dissimilar pairs apart in
that single space, making similarity learning stable and consistent.
● Symmetry also makes the model order-invariant: swapping the pair (A,B) to (B,A) gives the same
distance/similarity, which matches the task’s semantics and simplifies training.
Benefits in terms of performance and learning
● Better metric learning: shared features produce comparable embeddings, so Euclidean/cosine distances
correlate with semantic similarity, improving verification and retrieval tasks.
● Data efficiency: with fewer parameters and pairwise/triplet supervision, the model learns useful
representations from fewer labeled examples (supports one-/few-shot learning).
● Robust generalization: consistent feature extraction across inputs reduces spurious asymmetries, helping
the network transfer to new classes by just comparing embeddings rather than retraining a classifier head
from scratch.
c)   Explain the role of Generator and Discriminator in Generative Adversarial Networks.
Answer:
● Generator (G): Produces synthetic samples from a random latent vector. Its goal is to create outputs that
look so realistic that the discriminator labels them as real. It learns by receiving gradients that indicate how
well it fooled the discriminator, gradually improving the realism of generated data.
● Discriminator (D): Acts as a binary classifier that distinguishes real samples (from the dataset) from fake
samples (from G). It outputs a probability of “real.” During training, it updates its weights to correctly
classify both real and fake inputs, providing the feedback signal that guides the generator’s learning.
● Adversarial training: G tries to maximize the discriminator’s error on fake samples, while D tries to
minimize its classification error. This min–max game pushes G to produce increasingly realistic data and
D to become a sharper critic, ideally reaching a balance where D cannot easily tell real from fake.
● Outcome: As training progresses, the generator captures the data distribution well enough to produce
high‑quality, realistic samples, while the discriminator remains a useful critic that stabilizes learning by
evaluating authenticity.
d)   Explain the differences between dot-product and additive scoring functions. Which of these is commonly
used in attention mechanisms, and why?
Answer:
● Dot-product (multiplicative): The score is the inner product between the query and key vectors; in practice,
it’s often divided by the square root of the vector dimension to keep values in a reasonable range before
softmax.
● Additive (Bahdanau): The query and key are first linearly projected, then combined with a nonlinearity
(like tanh), and finally reduced to a scalar score by another linear layer. Query and key can have different
dimensions.
Key differences:
● Dimensionality: Dot-product requires query and key to have the same dimension; additive works even
when they differ.
********** 2 **********
--- page 3 ---
● Computation: Dot-product is just matrix multiplications, so it’s very fast and highly parallel on
GPUs/TPUs; additive introduces a small MLP with extra parameters and operations, making it slower.
●   Stability: Scaled dot-product uses a 1/sqrt(d) factor to prevent very large scores that can saturate softmax;
additive’s tanh keeps scores bounded and is less sensitive to vector length.
● Parameters: Dot-product adds no extra learnable parameters in the scorer; additive adds projection weights
and a vector for the final scoring.
Which is commonly used and why:
● Scaled dot-product attention is the standard in modern Transformers because it’s computationally efficient,
easy to parallelize, and trains stably with the scaling factor. Additive attention remains useful when
query/key dimensions differ or in smaller RNN-based models, but it is generally slower at large scale.
e) What is the over-smoothing problem in Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)? Why is it considered a
limitation?
Answer:
● Over-smoothing is the phenomenon where, as we stack more GNN layers, node embeddings become
increasingly similar and eventually indistinguishable, often converging exponentially toward a common
representation across the graph.
● Message passing repeatedly aggregates neighbor features. With depth, information mixes across many
hops, washing out each node’s unique signal and making representations uniform
Why it’s a limitation:
● Class separability drops: nodes from different classes collapse to similar embeddings, hurting downstream
tasks like node classification.
● Expressive power decreases: deeper models lose their ability to distinguish structural/feature differences,
even with attention mechanisms, leading to performance degradation.
● Training/architecture constraints: It prevents building very deep GNNs without special designs or
regularizers, limiting depth-based gains.

Autoencoder Architecture:
● An autoencoder is a neural network made up of two parts:
● An encoder network that compresses high-dimensional input data such as an image into a lower-

● A decoder network that decompresses a given embedding vector back to the original domain (e.g. back to

● The autoencoder is trained to reconstruct an image, after it has passed through the encoder and back out

b) An AI art generator uses Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) to create new artwork styles. Explain the working
of VAEs and how they differ from standard autoencoders in terms of latent space representation and generation
capabilities. Why are VAEs particularly useful in generative tasks?
Answer: A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) learns a probabilistic latent space that supports smooth sampling and
generation, while a standard autoencoder learns fixed latent codes mainly for reconstruction, not generative
sampling. In a VAE, the encoder outputs parameters of a Gaussian distribution—mean mu and log-variance log
sigma^2—and a latent vector z is sampled via the reparameterization trick: z = mu + sigma * epsilon, with epsilon
~ N(0, I), which allows gradients to pass through sampling during training. The decoder reconstructs from z, and
training minimizes a loss with two parts: a reconstruction term plus a KL divergence that pushes the approximate
posterior toward a simple prior (usually N(0, I)), yielding a continuous, well-structured latent space.
Working of VAEs:
● Encoder: maps input x to (mu(x), log_sigma2(x)), defining q_phi(z | x) as a Gaussian per input .
● Reparameterization: sample z = mu + sigma * epsilon, where epsilon ~ N(0, I), making the stochastic step
differentiable.
● Decoder: generates x_hat from p_theta(x | z), aiming to match the original x .
● Objective: maximize E_{q(z|x)}[log p(x | z)] − KL(q(z | x) || p(z)), balancing accurate reconstructions with
a smooth latent distribution
How VAEs differ from standard autoencoders:
● Latent nature: standard autoencoders produce deterministic codes; VAEs learn a distribution over codes,
enabling sampling around each input rather than a single point.
● Regularization: standard autoencoders optimize only reconstruction, often creating irregular latent spaces;
VAEs add a KL term to align q(z | x) with a Gaussian prior, ensuring continuity and coverage for
interpolation and sampling anywhere in latent space .
● Generation: standard autoencoders lack a principled way to generate new samples; VAEs can draw z ~ N(0,
I) and decode to produce novel, coherent outputs
Why VAEs are useful for generative tasks:
● Smooth interpolation and style blending: moving in latent space yields gradual, meaningful visual changes,
enabling style mixing for AI art.
● Diversity with coherence: sampling from a well-regularized Gaussian prior produces varied yet plausible
generations without memorizing the training set.
● Trainable stochasticity: the reparameterization trick makes random sampling differentiable, allowing
efficient training of generative models that create high-quality, novel samples.

Q3.
a) Explain self-attention mechanism (using architecture diagram) in Transformer models. Explain how self-
attention helps in understanding the context in long documents, such as legal contracts or research papers.
Answer:
Self-attention lets each token in a sequence look at every other token and decide “how much to attend” to them. It
builds a new representation for each token as a weighted mix of all tokens, so meaning becomes context-aware.
Why multi-head?
● Different heads capture different relations simultaneously (syntax, long-range coreference, topic cues). One
head may track subject–verb links; another tracks entity coreference; another tracks positional patterns.
●   Parallel heads reduce the burden on a single similarity space and improve expressiveness on long text.
How self-attention builds context in long documents (legal/research)
● Long-range dependencies: Any token can directly attend to any other, regardless of distance, so terms
defined on page 1 can influence mentions on page 20.
● Disambiguation by evidence aggregation: Weights pool evidence across the whole document (e.g., a
clause’s references, citations, or definitions), sharpening meaning of ambiguous phrases.
● Structured linkage: Heads can learn to follow cross-references (e.g., “as per Section 3.2”), definitions, and
coreferent entities across sections, improving clause interpretation and entity tracking.
● Robust summaries: Context-aware token embeddings feed later layers (and the decoder), enabling accurate
extraction, question answering, and summarization of dense material.
● Efficiency note: Attention runs in parallel over tokens (vs. step-by-step RNNs), aiding training on long
sequences; variants add masking/structural constraints when needed

a) Explain how Wasserstein GANs (WGANs) address limitations of traditional GANs using the Wasserstein
distance and WGAN architecture.
Answer:
1) What goes wrong in traditional GANs
Unstable training: The discriminator can become too strong, causing vanishing gradients for the generator.
Mode collapse: Generator produces few modes (repetitive samples) rather than covering full data diversity.
Loss not informative: JS/KL-based losses can saturate (especially when supports don’t overlap), so the loss curve
doesn’t correlate with sample quality.
2) Core idea: Wasserstein (Earth-Mover) distance
Replace JS/KL divergence with the Wasserstein-1 distance, which measures the minimum “mass transport” cost to
morph one distribution into another.
Key benefit: Provides meaningful, smooth gradients even when the model and data distributions don’t overlap, so
the generator gets useful learning signals throughout training.
3) WGAN architecture changes
Discriminator → Critic: It no longer classifies real/fake. Instead, it assigns higher scores to real samples and lower
to generated ones. The objective estimates the Wasserstein distance between the two distributions.
Lipschitz constraint: To make the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual valid, the critic must be 1-Lipschitz. Original
WGAN enforces this by weight clipping (clamp critic weights to a small range). This keeps the critic smooth and
stabilizes training, though clipping can be crude.
********** 11 **********
--- page 12 ---
Improved enforcement (WGAN-GP): Gradient penalty encourages the critic’s gradient norm to be ≈1 along lines
between real and fake samples, avoiding the pitfalls of clipping and improving stability.
4) Training dynamics and loss behavior
Meaningful loss curve: The critic’s loss correlates with the estimated Wasserstein distance, so decreasing critic
loss generally indicates better sample quality—making debugging/training decisions easier.
Train critic more: WGAN recommends training the critic to near-optimality per generator step (e.g., multiple critic
updates per generator update). This yields a sharper estimate of the distance and steadier generator progress.
5) How WGAN addresses GAN limitations
Prevents vanishing gradients: Wasserstein distance supplies gradients even with disjoint supports, avoiding the flat
regions that stall the generator in JS/KL setups.
Reduces mode collapse: By measuring overall transport cost, the generator is encouraged to cover the full data
support rather than collapsing to a few modes.
Stabilizes training: The Lipschitz-constrained critic (via clipping or gradient penalty) and a distance-based
objective produce smoother, more reliable optimization and learning curves.
Architectural flexibility: Because the objective is better behaved, WGANs often tolerate broader architecture
choices and longer training without catastrophic failures.

b) What is the over-smoothing problem in Graph Neural Networks? Illustrate with an example how this problem
can affect community detection in social networks. [CO4, SO-1, BL4]
Answer:
Over-smoothing is the phenomenon where, as the number of message-passing layers increases, node embeddings
in a GNN become increasingly similar, eventually making nodes from different classes or roles nearly
indistinguishable. In other words, repeated neighborhood aggregation mixes features so much that the network
loses discriminative power across the graph.
Why it happens
● Repeated aggregation: Each layer diffuses information over larger k-hop neighborhoods. After many layers,
most nodes receive similar averaged signals.
● Low-pass effect: Typical GNN layers (e.g., graph convolution) act like low-pass filters on graph signals,
suppressing high-frequency (node-specific) components while amplifying smooth, low-frequency
components.
********** 13 **********
--- page 14 ---
● Topology influence: Dense connections, short path lengths, or strong inter-community links accelerate
homogenization. Noisy edges further dilute class- or community-specific signals.
Symptoms and consequences
● Collapsed embeddings: Distances between node embeddings shrink; clusters for different classes overlap.
● Training vs depth: Validation accuracy often peaks at shallow depth; deeper stacks degrade performance
despite more parameters.
● Metrics: Smoothness rises while measures of inter-class separability (e.g., embedding variance across
labels) fall.
Example
Imagine a tiny graph with two well-formed communities A and B, each with distinct initial features. After 5–10
GNN layers, repeated averaging causes nodes in A and B to converge toward similar vectors. A classifier on top
of these embeddings struggles to separate A from B because the features are now nearly identical.
Impact on community detection in social networks
● Suppose we apply a GNN to detect communities on a social network where:
● Within-community edges (friends with similar interests) are dense.
● There are also some cross-community edges (acquaintances, influencers).
● With shallow layers (1–2 hops), node embeddings preserve community-specific traits (e.g., shared interests,
group interactions). A downstream clustering step (k-means or a softmax classifier) cleanly separates
communities. But as we increase depth:
● Signals from cross-community edges propagate widely, blending features of distinct groups.
● Embeddings of different communities collapse toward a common center.
● Community boundaries blur; clustering merges communities or splits them inconsistently.
Concrete scenario: Consider two college clubs on a campus social network—Photography (P) and Robotics (R).
Initially, posts/interactions produce distinct features (P: image-sharing behavior; R: code/project interactions).
After many GNN layers, because both clubs follow campus-wide influencers and share some mutual friends,
embeddings across P and R homogenize. The community detection head now mislabels members or merges P and
R into one mixed group, reducing modularity and purity of detected clusters.

b) Assume: A fraud detection system in a bank represents customers and transactions as a graph. How can a Graph

Answer:
● In banking fraud detection, the data is naturally structured as a graph where nodes represent customers or
bank accounts, and edges represent transactions between them. This approach captures the interconnected
nature of financial activity. Graph-based models exploit these relationships to spot abnormal patterns.
However, the ability to detect fraud heavily depends on how effectively the model aggregates information
from a node’s neighbors.
● Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) aggregate neighbor information with uniform weighting —
treating all connections as equally important. While this works for clean, balanced datasets, it becomes a
limitation in real-world fraudulent networks where there is high heterogeneity in transaction behavior.
Fraud-related accounts are typically a small minority; their signals often get diluted among numerous
normal transactions when averaged equally.
● Graph Attention Networks (GATs) overcome this by introducing attention coefficients — learnable weights
that determine the relative importance of each neighbor during aggregation. Rather than blindly averaging,
GATs learn to focus more on informative or suspicious connections and less on routine, irrelevant ones.
This selective focus is key in fraud detection because it allows the model to prioritize critical signals.
Example: Consider an account with mostly normal transactions to trusted vendors. Suddenly, it sends multiple
high-value transfers to accounts previously linked to flagged fraudulent activity.
● In a GCN: These suspicious neighbors’ influence would be averaged with many normal ones, potentially
masking the risk.
● In a GAT: The attention mechanism assigns higher weights to these risky connections, boosting their
influence on the model’s representation and increasing the probability of detection.
● Fraudsters often operate within dense subgraphs or rings — accounts passing funds quickly among
themselves, often with cyclical paths, foreign intermediaries, or sudden bursts of activity at odd hours. A
GAT’s attention scores naturally highlight these abnormal connection clusters during training, enabling
better temporal transaction pattern recognition and improved anomaly localization.
Advantages in practice:
● Better Signal-to-Noise Ratio: Suspicious edges have stronger representation influence.

● Reduced False Negatives: Subtle links that are rare but critical in fraud chains are more likely to be detected.
GATs provide context-aware, neighbor-selective aggregation, unlike GCNs’ flat averaging. This allows them to
