CO:- 2;   You receive multiple technical proposals. Apply proposal evaluation techniques to
SO:- 2;   a. rank suppliers objectively.   [5]
BL:- 3;   Solution:
1. Establish Evaluation Criteria
Define the factors that matter most for your project. Typical categories include:
Technical Factors
• Compliance with specifications
•   Quality standards and certifications
•   Reliability and track record
•   Innovation and value-added features
Commercial Factors (optional if you want to include cost considerations)
•   Total cost of ownership
• Payment terms
•   Delivery schedule
Other Factors
•   After-sales support and warranty
•   Supplier capacity and scalability
•   Sustainability and ethical practices
Assign weightages to each criterion based on project priorities. For example,
technical compliance 50%, delivery schedule 20%, quality standards 30%.
2. Develop a Scoring System
Use a numerical scale (e.g., 1–5 or 1–10) to score each supplier against each
criterion.
Example:
Criterion   Weight Supplier A Supplier B Supplier C
Technical compliance 50%   8   9   7
Delivery schedule   20%   7   6   8
Quality standards   30%   9   8   9
3. Calculate Weighted Scores
Multiply each score by its weight and sum them up to get an overall weighted score
for each supplier.
Example calculation for Supplier A:
•   Technical compliance: 8 × 0.5 = 4
•   Delivery schedule: 7 × 0.2 = 1.4
•   Quality standards: 9 × 0.3 = 2.7
Total weighted score = 8.1
4. Rank Suppliers

Rank suppliers based on their total weighted scores. Higher score = better fit for
your project requirements.
5. Conduct Qualitative Review
Even after scoring, review proposals qualitatively for:
•   Alignment with long-term project goals
•   Risk factors (e.g., single-source dependencies)
•   Supplier flexibility or responsiveness
6. Optional Techniques for Objectivity
• Independent Evaluation Committee: Have multiple evaluators score
proposals to reduce bias.
•   Scoring Matrix / Decision Matrix: Visual comparison of scores across all
criteria.
•   Predefined Thresholds: Eliminate suppliers scoring below minimum criteria
before ranking.
•   Normalization: Adjust scores if evaluators use different scales, ensuring
fairness.

SO:- 2;   b. Analyze a reverse auction outcome and identify risks such as supplier collusion or   [5]
BL:- 4;   quality compromise.
Solution:
Reverse Auction Outcome Analysis & Risks – Key Points
Outcome Analysis:
1. Identify the lowest bid and assess realism.
2. Examine bid spread between suppliers.
3. Evaluate supplier history and reliability.
4. Check compliance with technical and contractual requirements.
5. Compare auction price with market rates or previous contracts.
Risks:
1. Supplier collusion to keep prices artificially high.
2. Quality compromise due to aggressive low bids.
3. Unrealistic pricing exceeding supplier capacity.
4. Supplier withdrawal after winning unprofitable contracts.
5. Damage to long-term supplier relationships.
Mitigation:
1. Prequalify suppliers for capacity and reliability.
2. Set minimum price or benchmark.
3. Include non-price criteria (quality, delivery, service).
4. Monitor bidding patterns for collusion.
5. Include contract safeguards for penalties and quality standards.
6. Conduct post-auction verification before awarding contracts.

CO:- 1 ;   a.   Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of different contract types in terms of
SO:- 2;   risk allocation between owner and contractor.   [5]
BL:- 5;   Solution:
1. Lump-Sum / Fixed-Price Contract
Description: Contractor agrees to complete the work for a fixed total price.
Advantages:
• Owner knows total cost upfront.
•   Incentivizes contractor to control costs and complete on schedule.
•   Risk of cost overruns largely borne by contractor.
Disadvantages:
• Owner bears risk of design changes or scope increases.
•   Contractor may compromise on quality to protect profit margin.
•   Less flexibility for changes once contract is signed.
********** 5 **********
--- page 6 ---
2. Cost-Reimbursable / Cost-Plus Contract
Description: Owner reimburses actual costs plus a fee/profit for the contractor.
Advantages:
•   Flexible; accommodates design changes and uncertain scope.
• Reduces contractor’s financial risk.
•   Encourages collaboration on complex or high-risk projects.
Disadvantages:
• Owner bears risk of cost overruns.
•   Requires detailed monitoring and accounting.
•   Less incentive for contractor to control costs.
3. Unit-Price Contract
Description: Owner pays contractor per unit of work completed.
Advantages:
•   Useful for projects with uncertain quantities.
• Owner bears less risk of scope variation.
•   Contractor motivated to be efficient.
Disadvantages:
• Owner bears risk if quantities are underestimated.
•   Contractor may reduce quality to increase profit.
•   Requires precise measurement and documentation.
4. Time & Materials (T&M) Contract
Description: Owner pays for labor and materials plus agreed markup.
Advantages:
•   Flexible; suitable for projects with uncertain scope or duration.
•   Contractor shares minimal financial risk.
•   Quick start possible without detailed design.
Disadvantages:
• Owner bears risk of cost escalation.
•   Contractor has less incentive to control costs or work efficiently.
•   Requires close supervision to avoid disputes.
5. Design-Build Contract
Description: Contractor handles both design and construction for a fixed or agreed
price.
Advantages:
•   Single point of responsibility; reduces owner’s coordination risk.
•   Potential for faster completion.
•   Contractor bears combined design and construction risk.
Disadvantages:
• Owner has less control over design.
•   Quality risk if contractor prioritizes cost over design standards.
•   Requires careful contractor selection.
