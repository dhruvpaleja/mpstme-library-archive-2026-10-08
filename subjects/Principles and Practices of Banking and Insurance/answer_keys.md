Real Time Gross Settlement (RTGS)
It is a system for the real-time transfer of funds between banks. It is used for large value
transactions and is considered to be the most secure and efficient method of inter-bank funds
transfer. The RTGS system is operated by the central bank of a country, and it ensures that the
transfer of funds takes place in real- time, meaning that the funds are transferred and settled
immediately.
National Electronic Funds Transfer (NEFT)
It is an electronic funds transfer system that enables the transfer of funds between banks in
India. It is used for small-value transactions, and the transfer of funds takes place in batches,
meaning that the funds are transferred and settled in batches at regular intervals throughout the
day.
Structured Financial Messaging System (SFMS)
It is a messaging system that facilitates the exchange of financial messages between banks and
other financial institutions. It is used to transmit various types of financial information, such as
payment instructions, account information, and status updates. SFMS provides a standardized
format for the messages and ensures that the messages are transmitted in a secure and reliable
manner.

1. Specific Policy
Under this policy, a fixed amount is insured for a specific property.
Application:
Suitable for building and machinery where the value is stable.
Compensation is limited to the sum insured or actual loss, whichever is lower.
Example:
If the factory building is insured for ₹50 lakh and the loss due to fire is ₹40 lakh, the insurer

In this policy, the value of the property is agreed upon in advance between the insurer and
insured.
Application:
Useful for machinery or special equipment where market value is difficult to determine.
The agreed value is paid in case of total loss.
Example:
If a specialized imported machine is insured for ₹20 lakh under a valued policy, the full amount
is paid if it is completely destroyed.

This policy covers stock located at different places under a single sum insured.
Application:
Suitable for factory owners who store raw materials or finished goods in multiple godowns
or warehouses.

If stock is stored in three different locations, one floating policy can cover all of them instead
of taking separate policies.

b. Principle of Insurable Interest (03)   6
The principle of insurable interest states that the insured must have a financial or pecuniary
interest in the subject matter of insurance. This means the person should suffer a financial
loss if the insured property is damaged or destroyed.
Key features
·   The insured must have a legal relationship with the subject matter.
·   The interest must exist at the time of taking the policy (in life insurance, it must also
exist at the time of claim).
·   It prevents insurance from becoming a gambling contract.
Examples
Page 10 of 18
--- page 11 ---
·   A person can insure his own house, but not his neighbour’s house.
·   A creditor has insurable interest in the life of the debtor to the extent of the loan.
·   An employer has insurable interest in key employees.
Importance
·   Ensures validity of the insurance contract
·   Prevents moral hazard and speculative insurance
·   Establishes a legitimate claim in case of loss
Principle of Indemnity (03)
The principle of indemnity means that insurance aims to restore the insured to the same
financial position as before the loss, not to allow profit from the insurance claim.
Key features
·   Compensation is limited to the actual amount of loss.
·   The insured cannot recover more than the sum insured or actual loss, whichever is
lower.
·   Applicable mainly to fire, marine, and general insurance (not life insurance).
Methods of indemnity
·   Cash payment
·   Repair of damaged property
·   Replacement of goods
·   Reinstatement of property
Example
If a factory is insured for ₹10 lakh but the actual loss due to fire is ₹4 lakh, the insurer will pay
only ₹4 lakh.
Importance
·   Prevents unjust enrichment
·   Reduces moral hazard
·   Ensures fair claim settlement
Conclusion
The principle of insurable interest ensures that only persons with a financial stake can insure a
subject matter, while the principle of indemnity ensures that the insured is compensated only
for the actual loss. Together, they maintain the legal validity, fairness, and ethical
functioning of insurance contracts.

Any 5 features - 5 marks.
Insurance is a financial mechanism that provides protection against uncertain future losses. The
main characteristics of insurance are as follows:
1. Contractual Agreement
Insurance is a legal contract between the insurer and the insured. The terms and conditions,
rights, and duties of both parties are clearly stated in the policy document.
2. Risk Transfer
Insurance involves the transfer of risk from the insured to the insurer. The insurer agrees to
bear the financial loss in exchange for a premium.
3. Risk Sharing
Insurance operates on the principle of pooling of risks. A large number of individuals exposed
to similar risks contribute premiums, and losses of a few are compensated out of the pooled
funds.
4. Payment of Premium
The insured must pay a fixed amount called a premium to receive coverage. The premium may
be paid monthly, quarterly, annually, or as a lump sum.
5. Uncertainty of Loss
Insurance covers events that are uncertain in occurrence, timing, or extent. The loss must be
accidental and not intentional.
6. Insurable Interest
The insured must have a financial or legal interest in the subject matter of insurance. Without
insurable interest, the contract becomes a wagering agreement.
7. Utmost Good Faith (Uberrimae Fidei)
Both parties must disclose all material facts. Non-disclosure or misrepresentation can make the
contract void.
8. Indemnity
Most insurance contracts (except life insurance) follow the principle of indemnity, meaning the
insured is compensated only to the extent of actual loss, preventing profit from insurance.
9. Large Number of Exposures
Insurance requires a large number of similar risk units to predict losses accurately and apply
the law of large numbers.
10. Regulated Industry
Insurance business is regulated by government authorities to protect policyholders and ensure
financial stability of insurers.

When a life insurance company launches a new term insurance product for young
professionals, the actuary plays a crucial role in ensuring that the product is financially viable,
competitive, and compliant with regulations. The role of the actuary can be illustrated as
follows:
1. Risk Assessment and Mortality Analysis
The actuary studies mortality data, life tables, and demographic trends of young professionals.
By analyzing age, lifestyle, occupation, and health patterns, the actuary estimates the
probability of death and determines the level of risk involved.
2. Premium Calculation
Based on risk assessment, the actuary calculates appropriate premium rates. The premium must
be sufficient to cover expected claims, administrative expenses, commissions, and provide a
reasonable profit margin, while remaining affordable and competitive.
3. Product Design
The actuary helps in designing policy features such as sum assured, policy term, riders (e.g.,
accidental benefit rider), and premium payment options. The aim is to align the product with
the financial needs of young professionals.
4. Reserving and Solvency Management
The actuary determines the amount of reserves the company must maintain to meet future
claims. This ensures the financial stability of the insurer and compliance with regulatory
requirements set by the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI).
5. Profit Testing and Financial Modeling
Before launching the product, the actuary performs financial projections and profit testing
under different scenarios (e.g., high claims, low investment returns) to ensure long-term
sustainability.
6. Ongoing Monitoring
After the product launch, the actuary continuously monitors claim experience and compares
actual results with expected outcomes. If necessary, pricing adjustments or product
modifications are recommended.
Conclusion

Emerging trends in Banking:-
Emerging banking trends are dominated by hyper-personalization, AI-driven automation, and
embedded finance, transforming traditional institutions into customer-centric digital
ecosystems. Key shifts include the rise of banking super apps, blockchain for secure, faster
transactions,   cardless UPI-based cash   withdrawals, and enhanced   cybersecurity
utilizing machine learning to combat fraud.
Generative AI & Hyper-Personalization: Banks are moving beyond basic analytics to
use AI for real-time, customized financial advice and product offerings, improving
customer engagement.
Super Apps & Embedded Finance: Financial services are integrating into non-banking
apps (embedded banking) or bundling services like insurance, investments, and budgeting
into "super apps" for a single-platform experience.
Digital Payments & Cashless Ecosystems: The rapid rise of UPI (Unified Payments
Interface) and digital wallets is driving a shift away from physical cash, with cardless
ATM withdrawals becoming common.
Cloud-Based Banking & Cybersecurity: Increased adoption of cloud technology helps
reduce operating costs and increase speed, while AI-powered tools are crucial for
mitigating cybersecurity threats.
Blockchain & Digital Assets: Banks are integrating blockchain technology for improved
transparency, faster international payments, and exploring regulatory-compliant digital
assets.
ESG & Sustainable Banking: There is a growing focus on integrating Environmental,
Social, and Governance (ESG) criteria into banking products, lending, and investment
strategies.
Workforce Transformation: Banks are reducing administrative staff in favor of
automation, shifting toward hiring specialized, technology-focused personnel to manage
digital tools.
