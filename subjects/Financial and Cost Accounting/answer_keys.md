Realisation Concept
The Realisation Concept states that revenue is recognised only when it is actually earned, not when
cash is received. Income is considered realised at the point of sale or when the right to receive
payment is established, regardless of when the cash actually flows in.
Key Points:
• Revenue is recorded when goods are delivered or services are rendered.
•   It prevents premature recognition of income.
• Ensures that profits are not overstated.
Example: A company sells goods worth ₹50,000 on credit in March. Although cash is received in April,
the revenue is recorded in March — the period of sale.
Money Measurement Concept
The Money Measurement Concept states that only those transactions and events which can be
expressed in monetary terms are recorded in the books of accounts. Non-monetary events, however
significant, are excluded.
Key Points:

Types of Budgets
A Budget is a quantitative and financial plan prepared in advance for a specific period. Budgets can be
classified on various bases:
1. On the Basis of Time
Short-term Budget: Prepared for a period of one year or less. Example: monthly or quarterly budgets.
Long-term Budget: Covers a period of 3 to 10 years. Used for capital expenditure and strategic
planning.
2. On the Basis of Function
Sales Budget: Estimates the expected sales in terms of quantity and value. It is the primary budget
from which all other budgets are derived.
Production Budget: Prepared based on the sales budget; it determines how many units need to be
manufactured.
Purchase Budget: Indicates the quantity and cost of raw materials to be purchased.
Labour Budget: Forecasts the number of labour hours and the cost of labour required.
Cash Budget: Estimates cash inflows and outflows for a period; helps in maintaining liquidity.
Capital Expenditure Budget: Plans for purchase of fixed assets like machinery, equipment, or buildings.
3. On the Basis of Flexibility
Fixed Budget: Prepared for a single level of activity. It does not change with actual output. Suitable for
stable, predictable operations.
Flexible Budget: Prepared for multiple levels of activity. It adjusts costs according to the actual level of
output achieved. More realistic and useful for control purposes.
4. On the Basis of Coverage
Master Budget: A consolidated summary of all functional budgets. It represents the overall plan of the
organisation.
Functional Budget: Individual departmental budgets (e.g., sales, production, finance) that feed into the
master budget.

Q7b. Ans
A. Bad Debts
Bad Debts refer to the amount of money owed to a business by its debtors which is considered
irrecoverable. When a debtor fails to pay after all reasonable efforts, the outstanding amount is
written off as a bad debt.
Treatment in Accounts: Bad debts are debited to the Profit & Loss Account as a loss and credited to the
Debtor's Account to reduce the amount receivable.
Example: A customer owes ₹10,000 and has become insolvent. This ₹10,000 is written off as a bad
debt — debited to P&L Account.
•   It reduces the net profit of the business.
• A provision for bad debts may also be created in anticipation of future bad debts.
B. Preliminary Expenses
Preliminary Expenses (also called Pre-incorporation Expenses) are expenses incurred before a company
is formed or in connection with the formation of a company. These include costs of drafting the
Memorandum and Articles of Association, registration fees, printing costs, and legal charges.
Nature: They are intangible fictitious assets appearing on the assets side of the Balance Sheet and are
written off gradually over a period of time.
Treatment: Written off against profits each year; shown under 'Miscellaneous Expenditure' on the
Balance Sheet until fully written off.
Example: A company spends ₹50,000 on registration and legal fees before incorporation. This is
recorded as a preliminary expense.
C. Assets
Assets are economic resources owned or controlled by a business that are expected to provide future
economic benefits. They are recorded on the left side (or top) of the Balance Sheet.
********** 10 **********
--- page 11 ---
Classification of Assets:
• Fixed Assets (Non-Current Assets): Long-term assets used in business operations. Example:
Land, Buildings, Machinery, Furniture.
• Current Assets: Short-term assets that can be converted into cash within one year. Example:
Cash, Debtors, Stock, Bank Balance.
• Intangible Assets: Non-physical assets with economic value. Example: Goodwill, Patents,
Trademarks, Copyrights.
•   Fictitious Assets: Losses or expenses not yet written off. Example: Preliminary Expenses,
Discount on issue of shares.
Example: A business owns a building (fixed asset), holds ₹20,000 cash (current asset), and has
registered goodwill (intangible asset).
D. Current Liabilities
Current Liabilities are short-term financial obligations of a business that are due and payable within
one accounting year (12 months). They represent amounts the business owes to outsiders in the near
term.
Common Examples:
• Creditors / Accounts Payable — amounts owed to suppliers for goods purchased on credit.
•   Bills Payable — promissory notes or bills of exchange due for payment.
• Bank Overdraft — amount overdrawn from a bank account, repayable on demand.
• Outstanding Expenses — expenses incurred but not yet paid (e.g., outstanding rent, salaries).
• Short-term Loans — loans repayable within one year.
• Income received in Advance — amounts received but not yet earned.
Treatment: Current liabilities are shown on the liabilities side of the Balance Sheet under the heading
'Current Liabilities and Provisions'.
Example: A firm owes ₹30,000 to creditors and has an outstanding salary of ₹5,000. Both are current

Budgetary Control – Definition & Limitations
Definition
Budgetary Control is a system where budgets are prepared in advance for each function/department, actual
results are recorded and compared with budgets, and corrective actions are taken on deviations to achieve
organisational objectives.
Limitations of Budgetary Control
• Based on estimates – may be inaccurate and unreliable
• Requires constant revision due to frequently changing business conditions
• Time-consuming and expensive to prepare and operate
• Creates inter-departmental conflicts over budget allocation
• May lead to rigidity and discourage managerial flexibility
• Success depends entirely on top management support and cooperation
• Not a substitute for management – it is only a tool, not a decision-maker
•   Difficult to apply in new or rapidly changing businesses
• Budget padding: managers may inflate estimates to appear more efficient
