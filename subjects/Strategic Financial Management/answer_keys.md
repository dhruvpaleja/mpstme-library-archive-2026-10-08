A. Equity finance, also known as equity capital or stock financing, involves raising funds by   8
issuing shares of ownership in a company. Here are the characteristics of equity finance:
1. Ownership Stake: Equity finance involves selling ownership stakes (shares or stocks) in the
company to investors. Each shareholder becomes a partial owner of the company and is
entitled to a portion of its profits (dividends) and voting rights in corporate decisions.
2. No Repayment Obligation: Unlike debt financing, equity finance does not require the
company to repay the funds raised. Investors provide capital in exchange for ownership, and
their returns come from the company's profitability or potential appreciation in the value of
their shares.
3. Variable Returns: Equity investors' returns are variable and tied to the company's
performance. They may receive dividends if the company distributes profits, but the amount
can fluctuate based on earnings. Additionally, investors may benefit from capital gains if the
value of their shares increases over time.
4. Risk Sharing: Equity investors share in the risks and rewards of the company's business
activities. If the company performs well, investors stand to gain, but if it struggles or fails, they
may lose part or all of their investment.

--- page 2 ---
5. Permanent Capital : Equity finance represents permanent capital for the company, as
shareholders do not have a fixed claim on the company's assets and do not expect repayment.
However, they retain their ownership stake as long as they hold onto their shares.
6. No Collateral Requirement: Equity financing does not require the company to provide
collateral, as with debt financing. Instead, investors assess the company's potential for growth
and profitability when deciding to invest.
7. Dilution of Ownership: Issuing new shares of stock dilutes the ownership stake of existing
shareholders, as their percentage ownership in the company decreases. This dilution can occur
when the company raises additional equity capital through secondary offerings or private
placements.
8. Flexibility in Use of Funds: Companies have flexibility in using equity finance proceeds, as
there are typically no restrictions on how the funds are utilized. This can be advantageous for
financing growth initiatives, research and development, or other strategic investments.

Introduction
Factoring is a financial service whereby a business sells its accounts receivable (book debts)
to a specialized financial institution called a factor at a discount. It is not merely a source of
finance but also a tool for managing receivables, credit control, and risk.
Thus, factoring improves liquidity and reduces administrative burden on firms.
Meaning and Mechanism of Factoring
Factoring is an arrangement in which:
The firm sells goods on credit.
The receivables are assigned to a factor.
The factor advances a percentage (usually 70–90%) of invoice value immediately.
The factor collects payment from customers.
The balance amount (after deducting commission and interest) is paid to the firm.
Hence, factoring converts credit sales into immediate cash.
Types of Factoring
Recourse Factoring
The client bears the risk of bad debts. If customers fail to pay, the firm must reimburse the
factor.
Page 4 of 5
--- page 5 ---
Non-Recourse Factoring
The factor bears the risk of bad debts. It provides credit risk protection.
Disclosed Factoring
Customers are informed that receivables are assigned to a factor.
Undisclosed (Confidential) Factoring
Customers are not informed about the factoring arrangement.
Domestic and International Factoring
Used for domestic trade and export trade respectively.
Advantages of Factoring
Improves Liquidity – Immediate cash flow.
Reduces Collection Burden – Factor manages receivables.
Credit Risk Protection – In non-recourse factoring.
No Additional Collateral Required.
Supports Growth – Enables higher credit sales.
Limitations of Factoring
Higher cost compared to traditional bank loans.
May affect customer relationship (in disclosed factoring).
Not suitable for firms with small receivables.
Dependence on factor’s credit assessment.
Evaluation: When is Factoring Preferable?
Factoring is more suitable when:
A firm has rapid growth in credit sales.
The company lacks strong internal credit management.
Immediate liquidity is required.
The firm wants to transfer bad debt risk.
Compared to bank credit:
Bank loans provide finance only.
Factoring provides finance + collection + credit risk management.
Thus, factoring is particularly beneficial for small and growing manufacturing firms with
limited working capital.
