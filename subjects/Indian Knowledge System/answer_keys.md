b. Town planning in ancient India (as seen in Harappan civilization, Vedic towns, and temple-
centered cities) was deeply rooted in cosmology, sustainability, and community life, while
modern urban planning is driven by industrialization, technology, and economic growth. The
key differences are:
1. Basis of Design
Traditional Town Planning: Guided by Vastu Shastra, cosmology, and rituals, emphasizing
harmony with nature and cosmic order.
Modern Urban Planning: Based on scientific analysis, engineering, and zoning laws,
focusing on infrastructure and efficiency.
2. Layout and Structure
Traditional: Grid-like layouts (e.g., Mohenjo-Daro, Harappa), central temples or public
squares, wide streets for airflow, wells, and step-wells for water.
Modern: Planned with roads, highways, skyscrapers, industrial zones, housing colonies,
often prioritizing transport and commerce.
3. Purpose and Philosophy
Traditional: Aimed at holistic living—spiritual, social, and ecological balance.
Modern: Oriented toward economic growth, industrial development, and population
management.
4. Sustainability and Environment
Traditional: Used local materials, rainwater harvesting, natural ventilation, green belts for
balance with nature.
Modern: Relies on concrete, steel, air conditioning, artificial systems, often leading to
environmental strain.
5. Social Organization
Traditional: Towns reflected community-centric living with common spaces, temples,
water bodies, and shared rituals.
Modern: Emphasizes individual living, with gated communities, private facilities, and
reduced communal interaction.

c. Introduction (2 marks)   10
Health in the Indian Knowledge System (IKS) is not merely the absence of disease but
a state of balance and harmony in all dimensions of human existence.
The Body–Mind–Intellect–Consciousness (BMIC) complex provides a comprehensive
model to understand this multi-layered nature of human life.
Unlike Western biomedical models that focus primarily on the body, the BMIC framework
integrates physical, psychological, cognitive, and spiritual well-being.
Components of the BMIC Complex (4 marks)
1. Body (Sharira)
The physical aspect, consisting of the Panchamahabhutas (five elements: earth, water,
fire, air, ether).
Responsible for physiological functions, sensory experience, and survival.
Maintained through proper diet (Ahara), exercise, and lifestyle (Dinacharya).
2. Mind (Manas)
The emotional and psychological faculty.
Stores desires, feelings, attachments, and disturbances.
Often compared to a restless monkey; requires discipline through meditation
and Pranayama.
3. Intellect (Buddhi)
The rational, discriminating faculty that guides decision-making and morality.
Determines right vs. wrong, long-term perspective vs. immediate desire.
Needs sharpening through Svadhyaya (self-study), reflection, and higher learning.
4. Consciousness (Atman/Pure Self)
The deepest layer, beyond body and mind.
Page 3 of 5
--- page 4 ---
Represents awareness, spiritual identity, and eternal truth.
Connection to consciousness provides peace, meaning, and liberation (Moksha).
Holistic Health Implications (4 marks)
Physical health: Alignment of body with correct nutrition, rest, and exercise prevents
disease.
Mental health: A disciplined mind reduces anxiety, stress, and emotional instability.
Cognitive clarity: A refined intellect supports wise decision-making and purpose-driven
life.
Spiritual fulfillment: Connection to consciousness provides inner peace, resilience, and
meaning in life.
Example:
A patient with hypertension may be treated not only with medicine (body) but also with
meditation (mind), rational stress-management strategies (intellect), and inner reflection
(consciousness). This ensures long-term healing instead of temporary relief.
Comparative Insight
Western medicine: Focuses on body (physiology) and partly on mind (psychology).
Indian BMIC model: Offers a layered approach that goes beyond symptom treatment to
address the root causes at mental, intellectual, and spiritual levels.
Conclusion
The BMIC complex highlights that health is multi-dimensional—a dynamic equilibrium of
body, mind, intellect, and consciousness.
By integrating these four aspects, the framework provides a truly holistic model of
health, useful not only for disease management but also for preventive care and overall
human flourishing.
