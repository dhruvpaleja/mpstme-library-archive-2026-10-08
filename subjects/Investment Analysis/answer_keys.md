1. Weak Form EMH
¢ Premise: All past trading information (such as historical prices and volume) is already
reflected in current stock prices.
¢ Implication: Technical analysis (using charts and past price patterns) cannot consistently
yield excess returns.
* Investor Strategy: Only new information can affect prices, so trying to "beat the market"
using past data is futile.
2. Semi-Strong Form EMH
* Premise: All publicly available information (including financial statements, news, economic
indicators, etc.) is already incorporated into stock prices.
¢ Implication: Neither technical analysis nor fundamental analysis can consistently outperform
the market.
* Investor Strategy: Only insider or non-public information could potentially lead to abnormal
returns,
3. Strong Form EMH
¢ Premise: All information, both public and private (including insider information), is fully
reflected in stock prices.
¢ Implication: Even insiders cannot consistently achieve abnormal returns.

¢ Investor Strategy: Markets are perfectly efficient, making it impossible for any investor to
outperform the market over time.

6. Fundamental Analysis: Process and Top-Down Approach
Fundamental analysis is a method used to evaluate the intrinsic value of a security, primarily aiming
at valuation—determining whether an asset is overvalued, undervalued, or fairly priced. The
ultimate goal is to guide investment decisions based on the asset’s true worth relative to its market
price.
This process typically follows a Top-Down approach, which involves three sequential levels of
investigation:
a) Economic Analysis
This stage examines the broader economic environment. Analysts assess factors such as GDP
growth, interest rates, inflation, fiscal and monetary policies, and geopolitical trends. These
indicators help determine the overall health and direction of the economy, which influences

market performance.
--- page 5 ---
b) Industry-Level Analysis

Once the macroeconomic context is understood, analysts focus on specific industries. They
evaluate industry trends, competitive dynamics, regulatory environment, and lifecycle stage
(e.g., emerging, mature, declining). This helps identify sectors with strong growth potential or
those facing headwinds.

c) Company-Level Analysis
The final step involves a deep dive into individual companies. Analysts study financial
statements, management quality, business models, competitive advantages, and valuation
metrics like P/E ratio, price-to-book, and discounted cash flow (DCF). This level provides insights
into a company’s financial health and future prospects.
