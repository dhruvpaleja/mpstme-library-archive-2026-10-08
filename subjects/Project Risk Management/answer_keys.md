exposure or risk severity

SO:- 2;   b. Analyze the use of Expected Monetary Value (EMV) in evaluating and choosing   [5]
BL:- 4;   between alternative project investment options.
Solution:
1. Concept:
• EMV (Expected Monetary Value) helps compare different project options by
evaluating their probable financial outcomes.
•   It considers both probability and impact of potential gains or losses.
2. Steps to Use EMV in Investment Decisions:
1. List all possible outcomes (success, failure, partial success).
2. Assign probabilities to each outcome.
3. Assign monetary values to outcomes.
4. Multiply each probability by its corresponding monetary value.
5. Sum all EMVs to get the overall expected value of each option.
3. Example:
Project Option Outcome Probability Monetary Impact (₹) EMV (₹)
Project A   Success 0.7   +10,00,000   +7,00,000
Failure   0.3   -5,00,000   -1,50,000
Total EMV (A)   +5,50,000
Project B   Success 0.6   +15,00,000   +9,00,000
Failure   0.4   -8,00,000   -3,20,000
Total EMV (B)   +5,80,000
Decision: Choose Project B because it has a higher EMV (+₹5.8 lakh), indicating
better long-term expected returns.
4. Advantages:
•   Provides objective, data-driven comparison.
•   Supports portfolio prioritization in uncertain conditions.
5. Limitation:
•   Doesn’t account for risk tolerance or qualitative factors (like strategic fit).

SO:- 2;   b. Analyze how interdependence among quality, schedule, and cost risks can escalate   [5]
BL:- 4;   project failures.
Solution:
1. Concept:
•   Quality, schedule, and cost are the three key constraints in project
management (the “Triple Constraint”).
•   Risks in one area often trigger or amplify risks in the others.
2. Interdependence Effects:
1. Schedule delays → Cost overruns:
o Late delivery increases labour and equipment costs.
2. Cost reduction → Quality compromise:
o Cutting budget can lead to inferior materials or reduced testing.
3. Quality issues → Rework → Delays + Higher Cost:
o Poor output causes rework cycles, increasing schedule and cost
pressures.
4. Resource constraint linkages:
o Overuse of limited skilled staff affects both productivity and quality.
5. Change management loop:
o Frequent scope changes disturb planned timelines and budgets,
reducing overall project control.
3. Example:
In a software project, testing cuts (to save cost) lead to product bugs → causes
rework → project delay → client penalty = compounding risk effect.
4. Result:
•   Small risk in one domain escalates across others, leading to project failure.
•   Integrated risk analysis across cost–time–quality is essential.
5. Mitigation Strategy:
•   Apply systems thinking: analyze all three constraints together.
• Use risk interdependency maps and integrated monitoring dashboards.
•   Maintain contingency reserves for rework and delays.

CO:- 1 ;   a.   Evaluate the effectiveness of risk avoidance as a strategy in a high-cost
SO:- 2;   infrastructure project. Justify whether it is always the best option.   [5]
BL:- 5;   Solution:
1. Meaning of Risk Avoidance:
•   Risk avoidance means eliminating the source of risk or changing project
plans to prevent the risk from occurring.
•   Example: Not constructing a bridge in a flood-prone area to avoid flood risk.
2. Effectiveness in High-Cost Infrastructure Projects:
1. Ensures safety and compliance:
o Avoiding high-risk methods or unsafe locations ensures regulatory
and environmental compliance.
2. Prevents catastrophic losses:
o   Infrastructure projects involve large investments; avoiding risky
options can save from multi-crore failures.
3. Improves stakeholder confidence:
o Demonstrates proactive and responsible management to investors,
government, and the public.
4. Reduces need for heavy contingency reserves:
o When major risks are avoided, less funding is needed for backups or
insurance.
5. Protects long-term sustainability:
o Reduces exposure to risks that can harm future operations or
maintenance costs.
3. Limitations / Why It’s Not Always Best:
1. Restricts innovation:
o Avoiding new technology or design methods may hinder
improvement.
2. Reduces opportunity gains:
o Some risks carry potential rewards (e.g., new construction technique
→ faster completion).
3. May increase other risks:
o Changing scope or design to avoid one risk may create others (e.g.,
design simplification reducing safety).
4. Cost implications:
o Avoidance may require redesigning or relocating — increasing cost.
4. Evaluation:
•   Risk avoidance is highly effective for high-impact and non-tolerable risks
(safety, legal, environmental).
•   But for manageable risks, mitigation or transfer may be more practical.
5. Conclusion:
Risk avoidance is effective but not always the best option.
The decision should depend on the risk’s probability, impact, and strategic
importance — balancing safety, cost, and opportunity.

• They can lead to major changes in deliverables or objectives
Thus, Black Swan risks show that even with structured tools like PERIL, not all risks can be
predicted, making flexibility in scope management essential.
