Q3   a. Explain how the Value Net Framework extends a traditional Porter’s
CO-1;   Five Forces analysis of the fast-food industry.   [10]
BL- 2, 3,4   (5 Marks)
********** 5 **********
--- page 6 ---
Model Answer:
1. Shifting from "Conflict" to "Cooperation"
A traditional Porter’s Five Forces analysis of the fast-food industry
(McDonald’s, Burger King, Subway) typically focuses on negative pressures:
high rivalry (price wars), low barriers to entry (new local cafes), and high
buyer power (customers switching easily). It treats the industry as a "Zero-
Sum" game where one firm's gain is another's loss.
The Value Net Framework extends this by introducing "Co-opetition"—the
idea that firms can simultaneously compete and cooperate to increase the total
"Value Pie."
2. The Introduction of Complementors
The most significant extension is the identification of Complementors—
players whose products make fast food more valuable or accessible. Porter’s
model often ignores these or mislabels them as "Suppliers."
•   Digital Infrastructure (Google/Apple): In the Value Net, Google
Maps and Apple Pay are critical complementors. If a customer can
find a "McDonald's near me" and pay instantly with a phone, the value
of the meal increases due to convenience.
•   Retail Agglomeration: Fast-food outlets often cluster together in food
courts or near big-box retailers (like Walmart). While Porter would
see this as "Intense Rivalry," the Value Net sees it as Cooperation—
the cluster attracts more total foot traffic than a single isolated store
would.
3. Bilateral Interactions and Strategic Advantage
The Value Net moves beyond a static power struggle to analyze Bilateral
Interactions:
• The Delivery Ecosystem: Porter might see delivery apps
(UberEats/Zomato) as "Suppliers" who take a cut of the profit. The
Value Net recognizes them as Complementors that expand the
"Customer" node to include people who would never have visited the
physical store.
•   Sustainable Advantage: By cooperating with these complementors
(e.g., exclusive delivery deals or integrated tech kiosks), a fast-food
giant creates a Technical Barrier to Entry. A small local burger shop
cannot match the seamless app integration of a global leader,
protecting the leader's market share in a way that "Price" alone cannot.
Conclusion: The "Win-Win" Perspective
While Five Forces helps a manager understand how to defend their profits,
the Value Net helps them understand how to grow the market. By managing
the network of complementors (tech, delivery, and location partners), a fast-
********** 6 **********
--- page 7 ---
food firm achieves a sustainable advantage by being the most "frictionless"
choice for the consumer.
b. "Precision-Pro" (PP) is a mid-sized manufacturing company that
produces high-end robotic arms for automotive assembly lines.
Recently, the company has seen a surge in orders despite charging a
15% premium over competitors. You are an internal consultant tasked
with mapping PP’s internal value chain to identify the sources of this
competitive advantage.
Construct a complete Value Chain Analysis for Precision-Pro?
(5 Marks)
Answer:
To identify the source of the 15% price premium, we categorize the firm's
activities into Primary (direct value creation) and Support (enabling
efficiency) activities.
1. Primary Activities
• Inbound Logistics: Global sourcing and exclusive 5-year contracts
for high-purity rare-earth magnets ensure superior component quality
that rivals cannot access.
•   Operations: The "Smart-Factory Hub" uses 24/7 AI-driven
automation to achieve a near-zero defect rate (0.001%), ensuring high
precision.
• Outbound Logistics: Use of a private, climate-controlled delivery
fleet protects the calibration of the robots during transit.
• Marketing & Sales: Specialized technical workshops for automotive
engineers build brand authority and trust within the niche market.
•   Service: IoT-based remote monitoring allows for "predictive
maintenance," fixing parts before they break and ensuring zero
downtime for the customer.
2. Support Activities
• Technology Development: R&D investment (12% of revenue) led to
the patented "Self-Healing Surface," a unique product feature.
• Human Resource Management: An "Expert-to-Expert" hiring policy
(10+ years experience) ensures the high-level skill required to manage
AI systems.
• Firm Infrastructure: A cloud-based ERP system integrates sales data
with production, allowing for real-time operational agility.
********** 7 **********
--- page 8 ---
Source of Competitive Advantage
The 15% premium is justified by the linkage between Technology
Development (Support) and Service (Primary). By using proprietary R&D to
enable predictive IoT monitoring, Precision-Pro moves from selling a
"product" to selling "reliability." For automotive assembly lines, the cost of a
stoppage is so high that they are willing to pay a premium for a robot that
guarantees zero downtime.

Q3   a. Explain how the Value Net Framework extends a traditional Porter’s
CO-1;   Five Forces analysis of the fast-food industry.   [10]
BL- 2, 3,4   (5 Marks)
********** 5 **********
--- page 6 ---
Model Answer:
1. Shifting from "Conflict" to "Cooperation"
A traditional Porter’s Five Forces analysis of the fast-food industry
(McDonald’s, Burger King, Subway) typically focuses on negative pressures:
high rivalry (price wars), low barriers to entry (new local cafes), and high
buyer power (customers switching easily). It treats the industry as a "Zero-
Sum" game where one firm's gain is another's loss.
The Value Net Framework extends this by introducing "Co-opetition"—the
idea that firms can simultaneously compete and cooperate to increase the total
"Value Pie."
2. The Introduction of Complementors
The most significant extension is the identification of Complementors—
players whose products make fast food more valuable or accessible. Porter’s
model often ignores these or mislabels them as "Suppliers."
•   Digital Infrastructure (Google/Apple): In the Value Net, Google
Maps and Apple Pay are critical complementors. If a customer can
find a "McDonald's near me" and pay instantly with a phone, the value
of the meal increases due to convenience.
•   Retail Agglomeration: Fast-food outlets often cluster together in food
courts or near big-box retailers (like Walmart). While Porter would
see this as "Intense Rivalry," the Value Net sees it as Cooperation—
the cluster attracts more total foot traffic than a single isolated store
would.
3. Bilateral Interactions and Strategic Advantage
The Value Net moves beyond a static power struggle to analyze Bilateral
Interactions:
• The Delivery Ecosystem: Porter might see delivery apps
(UberEats/Zomato) as "Suppliers" who take a cut of the profit. The
Value Net recognizes them as Complementors that expand the
"Customer" node to include people who would never have visited the
physical store.
•   Sustainable Advantage: By cooperating with these complementors
(e.g., exclusive delivery deals or integrated tech kiosks), a fast-food
giant creates a Technical Barrier to Entry. A small local burger shop
cannot match the seamless app integration of a global leader,
protecting the leader's market share in a way that "Price" alone cannot.
Conclusion: The "Win-Win" Perspective
While Five Forces helps a manager understand how to defend their profits,
the Value Net helps them understand how to grow the market. By managing
the network of complementors (tech, delivery, and location partners), a fast-

food firm achieves a sustainable advantage by being the most "frictionless"
choice for the consumer.

1. Shifting from "Conflict" to "Cooperation"
A traditional Porter’s Five Forces analysis of the fast-food industry
(McDonald’s, Burger King, Subway) typically focuses on negative pressures:
high rivalry (price wars), low barriers to entry (new local cafes), and high
buyer power (customers switching easily). It treats the industry as a "Zero-

The Value Net Framework extends this by introducing "Co-opetition"—the
idea that firms can simultaneously compete and cooperate to increase the total
"Value Pie."
2. The Introduction of Complementors
The most significant extension is the identification of Complementors—
players whose products make fast food more valuable or accessible. Porter’s
model often ignores these or mislabels them as "Suppliers."
•   Digital Infrastructure (Google/Apple): In the Value Net, Google
Maps and Apple Pay are critical complementors. If a customer can

of the meal increases due to convenience.
•   Retail Agglomeration: Fast-food outlets often cluster together in food
courts or near big-box retailers (like Walmart). While Porter would
see this as "Intense Rivalry," the Value Net sees it as Cooperation—
the cluster attracts more total foot traffic than a single isolated store
would.

Interactions:
• The Delivery Ecosystem: Porter might see delivery apps
(UberEats/Zomato) as "Suppliers" who take a cut of the profit. The

"Customer" node to include people who would never have visited the
physical store.

(e.g., exclusive delivery deals or integrated tech kiosks), a fast-food

cannot match the seamless app integration of a global leader,
protecting the leader's market share in a way that "Price" alone cannot.

o Can competitors easily duplicate or substitute the resource?
o Resources that are protected by patents, unique processes,
strong brand identity, or proprietary knowledge are difficult
to imitate.
4. N - Non-Substitutable
o Can another resource replace its function?
o   If substitutes exist, the competitive advantage may not be
sustainable.
