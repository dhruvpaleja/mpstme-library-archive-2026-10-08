TechNova Pvt. Ltd., a mid-sized technology firm, has recently developed a
breakthrough innovation called the SmartBio Sensor, a wearable biosensor
capable of continuously monitoring glucose and hydration levels. The
technology holds strong potential for application across both the medical and
fitness sectors. However, the company now faces a strategic dilemma:
How to best exploit this technology for commercial success?
The management is considering four alternative approaches.
One option is to employ the technology in its own production and products by
launching a wearable health device under the TechNova brand. This would
allow the company to retain complete control over the product and technology
but would require substantial investment in manufacturing, marketing, and
distribution, areas in which TechNova currently lacks strong capabilities or
consumer recognition.
A second alternative is to contract out the manufacture or marketing to an
established consumer health brand. This arrangement would enable faster
market entry and take advantage of the partner’s marketing strength, but it
would also mean sharing profits and relinquishing some control over the final
product.
A third option under review is to form a joint venture with a global med-tech
company to jointly develop and market specialized clinical or sports versions of
CO 2;   the SmartBio Sensor. Such an arrangement would allow TechNova to share
BL-4   both risk and research capabilities, while also gaining access to global markets.
However, it would also involve complex negotiations, possible conflicts of
interest, and shared decision-making authority.
The final option being discussed is to license out the SmartBio technology to
multiple companies in different application areas such as healthcare
monitoring, fitness wearables, and sports analytics. Licensing would provide
TechNova with royalty-based revenue and require minimal investment, yet it
could also lead to rapid diffusion of the technology and a potential loss of its
distinct competitive advantage.
At present, TechNova’s relative standing in the market can be described as
moderate in terms of technological capability but weak in marketing and
distribution. The urgency of exploitation is high because competitors are
rapidly entering the biosensor market, and the need for complementary
technologies, such as data analytics and software integration, is also
significant. The SmartBio technology is positioned in the early growth stage of
its life cycle and is considered distinctive and central to the firm’s research and
development efforts. Its potential applications are broad, ranging from
healthcare to sports and wellness.
Question : Using David Ford’s framework on factors affecting technology
exploitation decisions, evaluate first and second alternative available to
TechNova. [10]

• Extend banking facilities to the poor;
• Eliminate the exploitation of the poor by moneylenders;
• Ereate opportunities for self-employment for unemployed
people in rural Bangladesh;
• Offer the disadvantaged an organizational format that they
can understand and manage by themselves;
• Reverse the age-old vicious circle of ‘low income, low saving
and low investment’, into virtuous circle of ‘low income,
injection of credit, investment, more income, more savings,
more investment, more income’.

The original project was setup in Jobra (a village adjacent to Chittagong
University) and some neighbouring villages and ran during 1976–1979. The core
concept was of ‘microfinance’ – enabling people (and a major success was with
women) to take tiny loans to start and grow tiny businesses.
With the sponsorship of the central bank of the country and support of the
nationalized commercial banks, the project was extended to Tangail district (a
district north of Dhaka, the capital city of Bangladesh) in 1979. Its further success
there led to the model being extended to several other districts in the country,
and in 1983, it became an independent bank as a result of government
legislation. Today, Grameen Bank is owned by the rural poor whom it serves.
Borrowers of the Bank own 90% of its shares, while the remaining 10% is owned
by the government. It now serves over 5 million clients and has enabled 10,000
families to escape the poverty trap every month.

the frontiers of surgery, drug therapy, transplant technology and a host of other research

innovation. The demonstrable and continuing success of applying the Toyota Production
System in everything it does has helped it build strong foundations on which more radical
developments — like the Prius hybrid car - can be explored.
