iii. Why differential weathering in rocks
Differential weathering occurs because different rocks or minerals have varying
resistance to weathering processes. The concept here is variation in mineral
composition and hardness. Hard, resistant rocks like quartzite weather more
slowly, while softer or chemically unstable rocks like shale or limestone weather

Deep-sea faulting can generate tsunamis due to the sudden vertical displacement of

movement causes the seabed to uplift or subside, it displaces a large volume of
overlying water. This displacement generates long-wavelength waves that travel
across the ocean as tsunamis, gaining height as they approach shallow coastal
areas.

Porphyritic texture in igneous rocks forms due to a two-stage cooling process of

rocks and is commonly seen in rocks like porphyritic basalt or andesite.

b. Describe one method of geophysical investigation that is popular to   10
understand mineral prospecting in rocks
One of the most widely used geophysical methods for mineral prospecting is the
electrical resistivity method. This method is based on the concept that different
rocks and ore bodies conduct electric current differently. In the field, current is
passed into the ground through electrodes, and the resistance offered by subsurface
materials is measured. Minerals such as metallic sulphides often show low
resistivity, while dry rocks and some non-metallic formations show high
resistivity. By studying these variations, geologists can infer the presence of
mineralized zones below the surface.
The resistivity method is useful because it helps in locating ore bodies,
determining the depth of weathering, identifying fractures, and understanding the
subsurface arrangement of rock layers. It is especially important where mineral
deposits are not directly visible at the surface. The data obtained are plotted and
interpreted to distinguish between host rock and possible mineral-bearing zones.
Because it is comparatively economical and non-destructive, it is popular in
mineral exploration work.

ii. How geology is used as a basis in different branches of Civil Engineering
Geology forms the foundation of civil engineering through the concept of
engineering geology, which applies geological principles to the design and
construction of infrastructure. In geotechnical engineering, knowledge of soil and
rock types, their strength, permeability, and compressibility is essential for
designing safe foundations, embankments, and retaining structures. The behavior
of materials under load directly depends on their geological origin and structure.
In structural engineering, geology helps assess site conditions such as fault
zones, seismic risks, and ground stability. Understanding concepts like faulting,
folding, and seismicity is crucial for earthquake-resistant design. In hydraulic
and water resources engineering, geology is used to study groundwater flow,
aquifers, and seepage through dams and canals, based on the principles of
hydrogeology.
For transportation engineering, geological investigations guide route alignment
by identifying stable terrains and avoiding landslide-prone or weak zones. In
environmental engineering, geology helps in waste disposal, contamination
studies, and land-use planning by understanding subsurface conditions and
pollutant migration. Thus, geology provides the scientific basis for safe,
economical, and sustainable engineering practices across all civil engineering
disciplines.
b. Explain the various types of mitigation measures that can be used for   10
reducing probability of landslides in areas that have highly fractured rocks
In highly fractured rock masses, stability is reduced due to the presence of joints,
cracks, and discontinuities. One of the primary mitigation measures is rock
reinforcement, based on the concept of rock mass strengthening. Techniques
such as rock bolting, cable anchoring, and dowels are used to bind fractured blocks
together and improve overall shear strength. Shotcrete (sprayed concrete) is often
applied to seal the surface and prevent small fragments from loosening.
Another crucial measure is drainage control, grounded in the concept of pore
water pressure reduction. Water entering fractures reduces friction and increases
the likelihood of sliding. Installing surface drains, sub-surface drains, and weep
holes helps remove excess water and maintain slope stability. Proper drainage is
especially important in fractured rocks where water can easily infiltrate.
Slope modification is also widely used to reduce instability. This includes
flattening steep slopes, benching (creating step-like terraces), and removing
unstable rock masses. These measures reduce the driving forces acting on the
slope, improving the factor of safety. In addition, retaining structures such as
retaining walls, gabion walls, and crib walls are constructed at the base of slopes to
provide support and resist downward movement.

What is the difference between circular type and wedge type of landslides?
Circular landslides, also known as rotational slides, occur along a curved slip
surface. They are common in homogeneous materials such as soil, clay, or highly
weathered rock. The movement involves rotation of the sliding mass, and the
concept involved is rotational failure governed by cohesion and internal
friction. These slides typically produce a concave-upward failure surface and are
analyzed using soil mechanics principles.
Wedge-type landslides occur in rock masses where two or more planes of
weakness (such as joints or bedding planes) intersect to form a wedge-shaped
block. The block slides along the line of intersection of these planes. This type of
failure is controlled by structural geology, particularly the orientation of
discontinuities relative to the slope. Wedge failures are common in hard, fractured
rocks like quartzite and are typically planar rather than curved.
What are the main reasons for landslides?
Landslides are caused by a combination of natural and human-induced factors that
reduce slope stability. One of the main causes is geological structure, including
faults, joints, and fractures, which create planes of weakness. The concept of shear
strength vs. driving force is central—landslides occur when driving forces exceed
resisting forces.
Water plays a major role through increased pore water pressure, which reduces
friction and cohesion within the slope material. Heavy rainfall, rapid snowmelt, or
poor drainage can trigger landslides. Additionally, earthquakes and vibrations
can destabilize slopes by suddenly reducing the strength of materials.
Other causes include weathering, which weakens rocks over time; steep slopes,
which increase gravitational forces; and human activities such as deforestation,
mining, excavation, and construction, which disturb natural slope conditions.
Together, these factors interact to trigger slope failure when stability is
compromised.

solidifies, which directly controls their texture and crystal size. The three main

Explain any three igneous structures
Igneous structures are large-scale features formed during the emplacement and
solidification of magma or lava. One important structure is the dyke, which is a
tabular, discordant intrusion that cuts across existing rock layers. It forms when
magma forces its way through fractures and solidifies, reflecting the concept of
intrusion along zones of weakness.
Another structure is the sill, which is also tabular but concordant, meaning it forms
parallel to the bedding planes of surrounding rocks. Sills are created when magma
intrudes between layers, indicating lateral magma movement along
stratification planes.
A third structure is the batholith, a massive, irregularly shaped intrusive body
formed deep within the crust. Batholiths represent large magma chambers that
have cooled slowly over time and are often associated with mountain-building
processes. This relates to the concept of deep-seated plutonism and large-scale
magmatic activity.
Other structures may include laccoliths (dome-shaped intrusions) and volcanic
features like lava flows, but the above three are among the most commonly
discussed.
Explain the concept of sedimentary environments
A sedimentary environment refers to the specific setting in which sediments are
deposited, controlled by physical, chemical, and biological conditions. The concept
is based on depositional processes and energy conditions, which determine the
type, size, and arrangement of sediments.
Sedimentary environments are broadly classified into continental, transitional,
and marine environments. Continental environments include rivers (fluvial),
deserts (aeolian), and lakes (lacustrine), where sediments are deposited by wind or
water under varying energy conditions. Transitional environments, such as deltas
and beaches, occur at the interface between land and sea and are influenced by
both terrestrial and marine processes.
Marine environments include shallow continental shelves and deep ocean basins,
where fine sediments settle under low-energy conditions. Each environment
produces characteristic sedimentary structures and rock types. By studying these
environments, geologists can interpret past conditions and reconstruct Earth’s
geological history.
b. Explain the concept of the Geological Time Scale   10
The Geological Time Scale (GTS) is a systematic framework used to divide
Earth’s 4.6-billion-year history into hierarchical units based on major geological
events and biological evolution. It is built on the concept of stratigraphy (study of
********** 18 **********
--- page 19 ---
rock layers) and paleontology (study of fossils), which together help establish the
relative and absolute ages of rocks. The scale organizes time into eons, eras,
periods, epochs, and ages, allowing geologists to correlate rock sequences across
different regions.
The largest divisions are eons (Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic, and Phanerozoic),
representing broad stages in Earth’s development. Each eon is subdivided into
eras, which reflect major changes in life forms—for example, the Paleozoic,
Mesozoic, and Cenozoic eras within the Phanerozoic eon. These are further
divided into periods (e.g., Jurassic, Cretaceous), and then into epochs and ages
for finer resolution. The concept of faunal succession is central, where fossil
assemblages follow a predictable order through time.
The Geological Time Scale is constructed using both relative dating (principles
like superposition and cross-cutting relationships) and absolute dating
(radiometric techniques). It provides a chronological record of Earth’s evolution,
including tectonic events, climate changes, and biological milestones.
Elaborate upon the Vindhyan suite of rocks and explain the time scale
connections of it
The Vindhyan Supergroup is one of the largest and most significant sedimentary
rock sequences in India, extensively developed in central India (Madhya Pradesh,
Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, and surrounding regions). It consists predominantly of
sandstones, shales, and limestones, deposited in a stable basin environment
over a long geological time span. The sequence is generally horizontal or gently
dipping, indicating relatively undisturbed deposition.
The Vindhyan Supergroup is divided into two major subdivisions: the Lower
Vindhyans (Semri Group) and the Upper Vindhyans (Kaimur, Rewa, and
Bhander Groups). These rocks represent a long period of sedimentation under
varying environmental conditions such as shallow marine, fluvial, and lacustrine
settings. The concept involved here is basinal sedimentation over geological
time, reflecting changes in depositional environments.
In terms of the Geological Time Scale, the Vindhyan rocks belong to the
Proterozoic Eon, approximately ranging from about 1.7 billion to 600 million
years ago (though exact dating is still debated). This places them in the
Precambrian time, before the abundant appearance of complex life forms. The
absence of well-developed fossils in most Vindhyan rocks makes precise
correlation difficult, but stromatolites (fossilized microbial structures) are found in
some limestone units, indicating early life.
Thus, the Vindhyan Supergroup provides an important record of Precambrian
sedimentation and early life, linking Indian geology to the global Geological
Time Scale. It helps in understanding Earth’s early environmental conditions,
basin evolution, and the transition toward more complex life forms in later
geological periods.

Differentiate between the three different types of unconformities and a special
mention of columnar joints
Unconformities represent gaps in the geological record, caused by periods of
erosion or non-deposition. The concept involved is break in stratigraphic
continuity. The three main types are angular unconformity, disconformity, and
nonconformity.
An angular unconformity occurs when older rock layers are tilted or folded and
then eroded before younger horizontal layers are deposited on top. This indicates
significant tectonic activity (folding/tilting) followed by erosion and renewed
deposition.
A disconformity occurs between parallel layers of sedimentary rocks, where there
is a gap in time due to erosion or non-deposition. The layers above and below appear
parallel, but fossils or dating reveal a missing time interval.
A nonconformity occurs when sedimentary rocks are deposited over older igneous
or metamorphic rocks. This represents a major time gap, as the underlying rocks
must first form deep within the Earth, be uplifted, and eroded before sedimentation
begins.
Columnar joints, in contrast, are not unconformities but are structural features
formed in igneous rocks due to cooling and contraction. As lava cools, it shrinks
and fractures into polygonal columns, typically hexagonal. This is based on the
concept of thermal contraction and stress release. A classic example is the Giant’s
Causeway, where spectacular columnar basalt formations are seen. These joints are
important in engineering geology because they influence rock stability and
permeability.

Weathering and deposition by river water (fluvial processes) and wind (aeolian
processes) differ mainly in energy, transport capacity, and the nature of sediments
involved. In rivers, weathering is largely mechanical through abrasion, hydraulic
action, and solution. Fast-flowing water carries sediments that grind against the
channel bed and banks, breaking rocks into smaller particles. Chemical weathering
(solution) is also significant, especially in limestone terrains. In contrast, wind
causes weathering mainly through deflation and abrasion, where fine particles

Deposition by rivers occurs when the velocity of water decreases, leading to
settling of sediments in a graded manner (concept of sorting by velocity). Coarse

capacity decreases, typically forming features like dunes and loess. Aeolian
deposits are usually well-sorted and fine-grained, as wind can only transport
lighter particles over long distances.
Thus, rivers are capable of transporting a wide range of particle sizes and produce
layered deposits, while wind primarily transports fine particles and produces
highly sorted, often cross-bedded deposits. The controlling concepts are energy of
transporting medium and particle size selection.
Elaborate upon the Deccan Plateau stratigraphy and why the lava layers are
so horizontal
The Deccan Plateau is characterized by extensive volcanic rock sequences known
as the Deccan Traps, formed by massive outpourings of basaltic lava during the
late Cretaceous period (around 66 million years ago). The stratigraphy consists of
multiple layers of basalt flows, interbedded with red bole layers (paleosols) and
occasional sedimentary intertrappean beds containing fossils.
The Deccan stratigraphic sequence is typically divided into lower, middle, and
upper formations based on variations in lava flow characteristics, mineral
composition, and regional distribution. Each lava flow represents a separate
volcanic event, and together they form a thick pile of nearly horizontal basalt
layers extending over a vast area of western and central India.
The reason these lava layers are so horizontal lies in the nature of the eruptions.
The Deccan volcanism involved fissure eruptions, where low-viscosity basaltic
lava flowed out through long cracks in the Earth’s crust rather than erupting from a
central volcano. Due to its fluidity, the lava spread over large distances in thin
sheets, settling in nearly horizontal layers. The concept involved is low-viscosity
lava flow and flood basalt formation.
Additionally, the region experienced relatively stable tectonic conditions after the
eruptions, with minimal deformation such as folding or faulting. This preserved the
original horizontality of the layers, consistent with the principle of original
horizontality in stratigraphy. Thus, the Deccan Plateau presents a classic example
of layered flood basalt sequences with remarkable horizontal continuity.

B   Mitigation Measures for Landslides in Sedimentary Rock Areas   10

A wedge-type landslide occurs when a mass of rock bounded by two
intersecting planar surfaces—such as joints, bedding planes, or faults—
slides downward along the line of intersection of these planes. This type is

rocks where the intersection line dips toward the slope face. The movement
is typically translational and involves a single rigid block or wedge detaching

In contrast, a laminar-type landslide (also called a planar or translational

mechanics, and scale of movement.

nearly horizontal, typically seen in high-grade metamorphic terrains. When
compressional forces are even stronger and persistent, the result is an
isoclinal fold, where both limbs are parallel and dip in the same direction,
indicating very tight folding common in orogenic belts. These fold
variations reveal the degree of stress and tectonic compression that the rocks

A   Difference between Cleavage and Hardness in Minerals   10
Cleavage and hardness are both key physical properties used to identify
minerals, but they describe entirely different aspects of a mineral’s behavior.
Cleavage refers to the tendency of a mineral to break along specific planes
of atomic weakness, which are determined by its crystal structure. These
planes represent zones where atomic bonds are weaker, allowing the mineral
6   to split smoothly and repeatedly in certain directions, producing flat, shiny
CO-3,   surfaces. Cleavage is described by the number, quality, and orientation of
1   planes, such as perfect, good, or poor. For example, mica exhibits perfect
BL-2   cleavage in one direction, allowing it to split into thin flexible sheets, while
feldspar shows two good cleavages at nearly right angles. In contrast, quartz
lacks cleavage altogether and breaks irregularly, showing a conchoidal
fracture instead.
Hardness, on the other hand, measures a mineral’s resistance to scratching
or abrasion, which depends on the strength of its atomic bonds rather than
their arrangement. It is quantified using the Mohs Hardness Scale, which
ranks minerals from 1 (talc), the softest, to 10 (diamond), the hardest known
natural substance. For instance, gypsum (hardness 2) can be scratched by a

scratch glass. While cleavage deals with how a mineral breaks, hardness
concerns how resistant it is to being scratched, and both are essential for
accurate mineral identification and understanding mineral durability in
engineering and geological contexts.
Different Types of Joints and Columnar Joints
Joints are natural fractures or cracks in rocks along which there has been
no significant movement parallel to the fracture plane. They are formed due
to processes such as cooling, unloading, or tectonic stress and play a major
role in rock weathering, slope stability, and groundwater movement. Joints
are classified mainly into three types — tension joints, shear joints, and
columnar joints — based on their origin and appearance.
1. Tension Joints (Extension Joints): These joints form due to tensile
stress when rocks are stretched or undergo release of overburden
pressure. They are usually vertical or near-vertical, and the fracture
surfaces open slightly apart. Such joints are common in sandstones,
limestones, and granites, where they form due to the relaxation of
stress during uplift or erosion.
2. Shear Joints: These joints result from compressive or shear stress,
where the rock fractures under tangential forces. They are typically
inclined to bedding planes and often occur in pairs or conjugate
sets, intersecting each other at an angle. Shear joints are common in
folded or faulted rocks and are often precursors to fault formation
under higher stress conditions.
3. Columnar Joints: These are a special type of cooling joint formed in
igneous rocks, especially in basaltic lava flows or shallow
intrusions. As the molten rock cools and contracts, polygonal cracks
develop perpendicular to the cooling surface, forming hexagonal or
polygonal columns that resemble pillars. The best-known examples
are the Giant’s Causeway in Northern Ireland and the Deccan
Traps in India. These joints form due to thermal contraction during
solidification, and their regular geometry reflects uniform cooling
conditions.
B   Processes of Weathering by River, Water, and Wind   10
Weathering is the natural process by which rocks are broken down,
disintegrated, or decomposed at or near the Earth’s surface due to the
action of air, water, and biological activity. When carried out by rivers,
water, and wind, the processes vary depending on the energy, medium, and
environmental conditions involved.
Rivers act as powerful agents of both mechanical and chemical
weathering. As river water flows, it performs hydraulic action, where the
sheer force of moving water dislodges rock particles from the riverbed and
banks. In abrasion (or corrasion), sediments carried by the river act like
sandpaper, grinding and scraping against rocks to wear them down.
Attrition occurs when transported rock fragments collide and break into
smaller, smoother pieces. Additionally, chemical weathering occurs when
river water dissolves soluble minerals like limestone and dolomite through
********** 11 **********
--- page 12 ---
solution and carbonation, especially in humid environments, creating
caves, sinkholes, and other karst features.
Water, in its various forms, contributes extensively to weathering.
Rainwater, slightly acidic due to dissolved carbon dioxide, chemically
reacts with rock minerals in a process known as carbonation, particularly
affecting carbonate rocks. Surface water and groundwater further aid in
oxidation, hydration, and hydrolysis, altering the chemical composition of
minerals and weakening rock structures. In cold regions, frost weathering
(freeze–thaw action) occurs when water seeps into cracks, freezes, and
expands, eventually breaking the rock apart.
Wind, on the other hand, is a dominant weathering agent in arid and semi-
arid regions. It performs mechanical weathering by deflation, where loose
particles are lifted and removed, and abrasion, where wind-driven sand and
dust polish and sculpt rock surfaces into ventifacts and yardangs. Over long
periods, wind smoothens and reshapes desert landscapes, eroding weaker
rocks and leaving behind resistant features. In summary, while rivers and
water combine mechanical and chemical processes, wind primarily relies
on mechanical abrasion, reflecting the diversity of weathering mechanisms
shaped by environmental conditions.
Stratigraphy of the Deccan Plateau
The Deccan Plateau in India is one of the world’s largest and most
geologically significant volcanic provinces, characterized by the Deccan
Traps, a vast sequence of Cretaceous–Paleogene age (about 66 million

across western and central India. The word “Trap” comes from the Swedish
word trappa, meaning “stairs,” referring to the step-like landscape formed

The stratigraphy of the Deccan Plateau reveals a layered structure
composed mainly of horizontal basalt flows interbedded with sedimentary
and volcanic ash layers, representing multiple eruptive phases.

Folds are bends in rock layers formed due to compressional forces, and they are
commonly classified based on the orientation of the axial plane. In an upright fold, the
axial plane is vertical and the limbs dip symmetrically in opposite directions, indicating
uniform compression. In an inclined fold, the axial plane is tilted, and one limb becomes

pushed beyond vertical. In a recumbent fold, the axial plane is nearly horizontal, and the
fold appears to lie on its side, typical of highly disturbed regions such as mountain belts.

B   Causes of Landslides in Metamorphic Rocks   10
Landslides commonly occur in regions underlain by metamorphic rocks
because these rocks often exhibit structural weaknesses and anisotropy
developed during metamorphism. One major reason is the presence of
foliation, schistosity, or cleavage planes, which act as natural planes of
weakness along which sliding can easily occur, especially when these planes
dip parallel to the slope. Rocks such as schist, gneiss, and phyllite have
layered textures, and when subjected to gravity, water infiltration, or
vibration, the blocks tend to detach and move downslope along these weak
planes. Another contributing factor is intense weathering, which alters
minerals (like biotite or feldspar) into clay, reducing cohesion and increasing
the susceptibility of slopes to failure. Jointing and fracturing caused by
tectonic activity further reduce the rock’s integrity, allowing rainwater or
groundwater to seep in, increasing pore pressure and lowering the shear
strength of the slope materials. In mountainous metamorphic terrains, steep
slopes, heavy rainfall, and seismic vibrations act as triggers, causing slope
failures in zones where the structural alignment of foliation coincides with
********** 3 **********
--- page 4 ---
the direction of slope movement. Hence, the combination of inherent
structural anisotropy and external triggering factors makes metamorphic
terrains particularly prone to landslides.
Difference between Circular and Wedge Type Landslides
A circular landslide (also known as a rotational slide) occurs when the
failure surface is curved or concave upward, typically developing in
homogeneous or isotropic materials like clay or weathered rock. The
movement involves a backward rotation of the slope mass around an axis
above the base of the slide, resulting in a characteristic curved scarp and
stepped terraces at the surface. These slides are common in slopes made of
uniform soil or weakly consolidated rock where shear strength is consistent
throughout.
In contrast, a wedge-type landslide occurs when the failure surface forms
along two intersecting planar surfaces, such as joint planes, faults, or
bedding planes—conditions that are typical in jointed or foliated
metamorphic rocks. The rock mass moves downward and outward along
the line of intersection of these planes, forming a wedge-shaped block that
detaches from the slope. Wedge failures are thus structurally controlled
and depend on the orientation of discontinuities relative to the slope face.
In summary, circular landslides occur in homogeneous materials with
curved slip surfaces, while wedge landslides occur in jointed or foliated
rocks along intersecting planar surfaces, making them more common in
metamorphic terrains.

B   Segregation and Layers of the Earth – Explanation   10
The Earth is segregated into three main layers — the crust, mantle, and
core — due to a process called planetary differentiation, which occurred
early in Earth’s history about 4.5 billion years ago. When the Earth was still
molten, gravity caused the denser materials such as iron and nickel to sink
toward the center, while lighter silicate minerals floated upward to form
the outer layers. This natural separation based on density, composition, and
temperature created a layered internal structure. Cooling and
solidification over time preserved this differentiation, resulting in a planet
composed of distinct concentric zones with varying physical and chemical
properties.
1. Crust
The crust is the Earth’s outermost and thinnest layer, forming the solid
surface on which we live. It accounts for less than 1% of the Earth’s volume
and varies in thickness from about 5–10 km beneath oceans (oceanic crust)
to 30–70 km beneath continents (continental crust).
• Composition: The continental crust is mainly composed of granitic
rocks rich in silica and aluminum (sial), while the oceanic crust
consists mainly of basalt and gabbro, rich in magnesium and iron
(sima).
•   Characteristics: The crust is brittle, fractured by faults and joints, and
hosts all surface geological processes such as weathering, erosion, and
sedimentation.
2. Mantle
Beneath the crust lies the mantle, extending to a depth of about 2,900 km. It
makes up nearly 84% of Earth’s volume and is composed mainly of silicate
minerals rich in magnesium and iron, such as olivine and pyroxene.
• The upper mantle (up to ~700 km) includes the asthenosphere, a
semi-molten, plastic layer that allows tectonic plates to move.

Sedimentary Environments
A sedimentary environment refers to a specific natural setting where
sediments accumulate, are transported, and eventually lithified to form
sedimentary rocks. Each environment has distinct physical, chemical, and
biological conditions that determine the type of sediment deposited and its
resulting rock characteristics. These environments are broadly categorized
into continental, transitional, and marine settings.
• Continental Environments:   Include   rivers   (fluvial),   lakes

biological sedimentation, producing limestones, shales, and cherts.
Each sedimentary environment leaves behind distinctive textures,
structures, and fossil content, which geologists use to reconstruct past
climates, sea levels, and depositional conditions—a key aspect of
interpreting Earth’s geological history.
B   Concept of the Geological Time Scale   10
The Geological Time Scale (GTS) is a scientific framework that represents
the entire history of the Earth, from its formation about 4.6 billion years
ago to the present, divided into hierarchical units based on significant
geological and biological events. It is constructed through the study of
stratigraphy, paleontology, radiometric dating, and fossil succession. The
time scale is divided into Eons, which are subdivided into Eras, Periods,
Epochs, and Ages. Each division marks major transitions in Earth’s
********** 8 **********
--- page 9 ---
development—such as the appearance or extinction of dominant life forms,
continental drift, or climatic shifts.
The four principal Eons are:
1. Hadean (4.6–4.0 billion years ago) – Earth’s formation and early
differentiation.
2. Archean (4.0–2.5 billion years ago) – Development of the first stable
continental crust and earliest life forms.
3. Proterozoic (2.5 billion–541 million years ago) – Growth of
continents, buildup of atmospheric oxygen, and emergence of
multicellular life.
4. Phanerozoic (541 million years ago–present) – Marked by the
proliferation of complex life, further divided into Paleozoic,
Mesozoic, and Cenozoic Eras.
The Geological Time Scale helps geologists correlate rock sequences across
regions, understand evolutionary trends, and reconstruct Earth’s
geodynamic history. Radiometric dating provides absolute ages to calibrate
this relative timeline, linking rock formations to specific periods in Earth’s
past.
The Cuddapah Suite of Rocks and Its Time Scale Connection
The Cuddapah Supergroup (or Cuddapah suite of rocks) represents one
of the most significant Proterozoic sedimentary basins in the Indian
subcontinent, located primarily in the Cuddapah Basin of Andhra Pradesh.
These rocks are part of the Purana Group, which includes undeformed,
unmetamorphosed sedimentary   successions   deposited   after   the
stabilization of the Indian Shield. The Cuddapah Basin covers an area of about
44,000 square kilometers and consists of a thick sequence—up to 12 km—
of shales, limestones, quartzites, dolomites, and sandstones, with minor
igneous intrusions like dolerite and basalt.
Geologically, the Cuddapah rocks belong to the early to middle
Proterozoic Eon, approximately 1,800 to 1,200 million years ago (Ma). The
sequence rests unconformably over the Archean granitic and gneissic
basement (Dharwar craton), indicating a major tectonic and erosional
break before sedimentation began. The basin evolved through multiple
depositional cycles in shallow marine and continental environments,
recording a long history of subsidence, sedimentation, and minor volcanic
activity. The rock succession is divided into several groups—Papaghni,
Chitravati, Nallamalai, and Kurnool groups—each representing distinct
depositional phases within the basin.
In terms of the Geological Time Scale, the Cuddapah suite corresponds to the
Proterozoic Era of the Precambrian Eon, marking a period of crustal
stabilization, sedimentary basin development, and early oxygenation of
Earth’s atmosphere. These formations are significant not only for
understanding India’s early geological evolution but also for their economic
importance, as they host valuable uranium, limestone, and building stone
resources.
Thus, the Cuddapah rocks serve as a window into Earth’s Proterozoic
history, bridging the ancient Archean crustal foundations with the more
dynamic and biologically rich Phanerozoic world that followed.

BL-1,   Folds are bends or curvatures in rock layers formed due to compressional
3   stresses acting on the Earth’s crust, and they are classified based on the
orientation of the axial (fold) plane. A symmetrical fold has limbs dipping
equally on either side of the axial plane, indicating uniform pressure from both
directions. In an asymmetrical fold, one limb dips more steeply than the
other, showing unequal compressional forces. When the pressure is extreme,
one limb may tilt beyond the vertical, forming an overturned fold, where
both limbs dip in the same direction but at different angles. In even more
intense deformation, the fold becomes almost horizontal, resulting in a
recumbent fold, which is common in severely deformed metamorphic
terrains. Another variety, the isoclinal fold, occurs when both limbs are
parallel and dip in the same direction due to strong compression. These
different fold types reflect the intensity and direction of tectonic forces
acting during mountain-building processes.
Thrust and normal faults represent two opposite styles of crustal
deformation caused by different stress regimes. A thrust fault is a low-angle
reverse fault formed under compressional stress, where the hanging wall
moves upward and over the footwall. It shortens and thickens the crust,
typically found in orogenic belts such as the Himalayas, where rock layers
are pushed over one another due to continental collision. In contrast, a normal
fault develops under tensional stress, where the crust is being pulled apart.
Here, the hanging wall moves downward relative to the footwall, leading to
crustal thinning and the formation of rift valleys and block mountains such
as the East African Rift.
Diagrammatically, a thrust fault shows compressive arrows pushing the crust
together, causing overlapping of strata, while a normal fault shows arrows
pulling apart, resulting in vertical displacement with the hanging wall moving
down. These two fault types together explain how Earth’s crust alternately
shortens, uplifts, or extends, shaping mountain ranges, valleys, and basins
over geological time.

Formation of Hanging Valleys and Corrie Lakes
Both hanging valleys and corrie (or cirque) lakes are features formed due
to glacial erosion in mountainous regions. A hanging valley develops where
a smaller tributary glacier joins a larger main glacier. Since the main glacier
carries a much thicker mass of ice, it exerts greater erosive power and cuts its
valley much deeper than the tributary glacier. After the glaciers retreat, the
********** 10 **********
--- page 11 ---
smaller valley is left “hanging” above the main glacial trough, often forming
a steep cliff or waterfall where the tributary once joined the main glacier.
This difference in depth results from differential erosion, where thicker ice
erodes more intensely than thinner ice. Over time, streams flowing through
the hanging valley may carve vertical gorges or cascades as they adjust to the
lower main valley floor.
A corrie or cirque lake, on the other hand, forms at the head of a glacial
valley, where snow and ice accumulate in a natural hollow on the mountain
side. Repeated freeze–thaw cycles, combined with glacial plucking and
abrasion, enlarge this hollow into an armchair-shaped depression with steep
back walls and an overdeepened basin. The ice erodes the rock floor more
effectively in the center, leaving a rock lip or moraine ridge at the outlet.
When the glacier melts, meltwater fills this overdeepened basin, creating a
small, circular corrie lake (or tarn). The formation occurs in stages — first
through snow accumulation (nivation), then through rotational glacial
erosion, and finally through post-glacial filling with water.
In summary, both features are products of glacial sculpting: hanging valleys
form due to unequal erosional depths between main and tributary glaciers,
while corrie lakes form due to localized erosion at glacier heads, leaving
behind bowl-shaped depressions that later hold glacial meltwater.
A   Difference between Streak and Lustre in Minerals   10
Streak and lustre are both important physical properties used in identifying
minerals, but they describe very different characteristics. Streak refers to the
color of the powdered form of a mineral, obtained by rubbing the mineral
against an unglazed porcelain plate called a streak plate. The streak is often
more consistent than the color of the mineral itself, which may vary due to
impurities or surface tarnish. For example, hematite may appear metallic gray
or black in hand specimen but always leaves a reddish-brown streak, while
pyrite, which looks similar, leaves a blackish-green streak. Similarly,
limonite gives a yellow-brown streak, helping to distinguish it from other
iron oxides. Thus, streak helps in accurate mineral identification, especially
for metallic minerals.

Types of Unconformities and Columnar Joints
An unconformity represents a gap or break in the geological record—a
surface of erosion or non-deposition separating rock layers of different ages,
indicating a period of crustal disturbance or weathering before newer layers
were deposited. The three main types of unconformities are:
1. Angular Unconformity: Occurs when older rock layers are tilted
or folded due to tectonic activity and then eroded, after which new
horizontal layers are deposited above them. The discordance in angle
********** 11 **********
--- page 12 ---
between the two sets of strata clearly shows a time gap. An example
is seen in the Hutton’s Unconformity at Siccar Point, Scotland.
2. Disconformity: Here, both the older and younger strata are
parallel, but an erosional surface or gap in sedimentation exists
between them, indicating a missing time interval. Since no angular
discordance is visible, disconformities are recognized through fossil
evidence or irregular erosion surfaces.
3. Nonconformity: This type occurs when sedimentary rocks lie over
eroded igneous or metamorphic rocks, marking a significant break
in geological history and uplift before sediment deposition resumed.
A special structure related to igneous rocks is the columnar joint, which
forms during the cooling and contraction of thick lava flows or shallow
intrusions. As basaltic lava cools uniformly, it contracts and fractures
perpendicularly to the cooling surface, producing polygonal (mostly
hexagonal) columns. Famous examples include the Giant’s Causeway in
Northern Ireland and the Deccan Traps in India. These columns represent
shrinkage patterns and provide insight into the cooling history and flow
dynamics of volcanic rocks.
B   Difference between Weathering by River and by Wind   10
Weathering by rivers and wind are both agents of erosion and landscape
modification, but they differ in mechanisms, intensity, and the resulting
landforms. River (fluvial) weathering primarily involves mechanical and
chemical processes driven by moving water. Flowing rivers erode their beds
and banks through hydraulic action (the force of water dislodging particles),
abrasion or corrasion (rock fragments carried by water scraping the
riverbed), attrition (particles colliding and breaking into smaller pieces), and
solution (chemical dissolution of soluble minerals such as limestone). Over
time, these processes deepen and widen valleys, forming characteristic
landforms like V-shaped valleys, waterfalls, meanders, and floodplains.
River weathering is strongest in humid climates where water flow is
continuous and volume is high.
In contrast, wind (aeolian) weathering operates mainly in arid and semi-
arid regions, where vegetation is sparse and loose sediments are exposed.
Wind erodes surfaces through deflation (lifting and removal of fine particles),
abrasion (sand-laden wind polishing and sculpting rock surfaces), and
attrition (grains striking and wearing down each other). Unlike rivers, wind
acts as a selective agent—transporting fine sand and dust while leaving behind
coarser materials, producing desert pavements, ventifacts (wind-shaped
rocks), and yardangs (streamlined ridges). While river weathering relies on
water’s erosive power and chemical activity, wind weathering is dominated
by mechanical action through friction and impact, and is most effective in
dry environments.
Principles of Stratigraphy
Stratigraphy is the branch of geology concerned with the study of layered
sedimentary rocks (strata) and their chronological relationships. It forms the
foundation for understanding Earth’s history, past environments, and fossil
evolution. The main principles of stratigraphy were established through
careful field observation and are used to interpret the sequence and relative
ages of rock layers.
1. Law of Superposition: In an undisturbed sequence of sedimentary
rocks, the oldest layer lies at the bottom, and successively younger
********** 12 **********
--- page 13 ---
layers are deposited above it. This principle helps establish the relative
chronology of strata.
2. Principle of Original Horizontality: Sediments are generally
deposited in horizontal or nearly horizontal layers. If layers are
found tilted or folded, it indicates tectonic disturbances that occurred
after deposition.
3. Principle of Lateral Continuity: Sedimentary layers originally
extend laterally in all directions until they thin out or encounter a
physical barrier. This helps correlate rock layers across valleys or
eroded regions.
4. Principle of Cross-Cutting Relationships: Any geologic feature
such as a fault, intrusion, or unconformity that cuts across existing
layers must be younger than the rocks it disrupts.
5. Principle of Faunal Succession: Fossil assemblages in sedimentary
layers occur in a definite and predictable order, allowing geologists
to correlate rocks of the same age even across distant regions.
Together, these principles allow geologists to reconstruct the sequence of
geological events, establish relative ages of formations, and interpret the
paleoenvironmental conditions under which the rocks were deposited.
Stratigraphy thus serves as a crucial tool for geological mapping, mineral
exploration, and understanding Earth’s evolutionary history.
