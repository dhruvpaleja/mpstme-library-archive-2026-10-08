For terahertz antenna design, key performance criteria include high gain,
wide bandwidth, high efficiency, low mutual coupling, and compact size,
crucial for applications like high-speed wireless communication and
imaging.
Here's a more detailed breakdown:
Gain: Terahertz antennas need to efficiently radiate and receive signals,
requiring high gain to overcome the inherent signal attenuation at these
frequencies.
Bandwidth: A wide operating bandwidth is essential for accommodating
various terahertz applications and supporting high data rates.
Efficiency: High efficiency ensures that the antenna radiates the
maximum amount of power without losses, which is important for
maximizing signal strength.
Mutual Coupling: In antenna arrays, minimizing mutual coupling
between elements is crucial for maintaining the performance of each
antenna element and improving overall system efficiency.
Compact Size: Terahertz antennas need to be compact to facilitate
integration into various devices and systems.

Millimeter wave (mmWave) is a range of electromagnetic frequencies that
exist between microwaves and infrared light. Millimeter waves have
wavelengths between 1-10 millimeters and occupy the 30-300 GHz range of
the electromagnetic spectrum. In the telecom and wireless industry, the
definition of mmWave might also include bands starting at 24 GHz, because
some 5G deployments and Wi-Fi standards that use mmWaves operate
there.
mmWaves have very short wavelengths and occupy a range of frequencies
that have large amounts of unused spectrum. This enables the creation of
extremely wide communication channels that can transfer large volumes of
data at high speeds. The high throughput that mmWaves provide makes this
frequency range highly suitable for a wide range of bandwidth-intensive
applications, including high-quality video transmission, augmented reality,
virtual reality, radar, satellite links and next-generation Wi-Fi systems. For
example, mmWaves are used in some 5G deployments to enhance network
performance in areas where existing cellular coverage requires more
bandwidth and speed.
The mmWave frequency band is also known as the extremely high
frequency band by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU). In the
context of 5G, this frequency band is also commonly referred to as 5G high-
band or high-frequency 5G.
5G is a cellular mobile network standard developed by the 3rd Generation
Partnership Project. It’s also a global standard defined by IMT-2020, an ITU
standard for providing wireless broadband communication. To provide full
coverage, 5G operates across three spectrum ranges -- low-band, mid-band
and high-band. Millimeter wave refers specifically to the high-band range.

Q7   a.   Explain the concept of Line-of-Sight (LOS) and Propagation Techniques.   [07]
CO-4; SO-1;   Ans:
BL-2
Line-of-sight (LOS) propagation requires a direct, unobstructed path
between transmitter and receiver, while non-line-of-sight (NLOS)
propagation occurs when signals reach the receiver via reflections,
diffractions, or scattering, overcoming obstacles.
Line-of-Sight (LOS) Propagation:
Definition:
LOS propagation occurs when the transmitter and receiver have a direct,
unobstructed path with minimal obstacles between them.
Characteristics:
Signals travel in a straight line without significant deviations or reflections.
Ideal for point-to-point communication and applications like microwave and
millimeter-wave communication.
Requires clear visibility and is susceptible to obstructions like buildings,
terrain, or weather conditions.
Advantages:
Higher signal strength and throughput due to less attenuation.
Simpler to design and implement.
Disadvantages:
Limited by obstructions and environmental conditions.
Requires careful alignment of antennas.
Non-Line-of-Sight (NLOS) Propagation:
Definition:
NLOS propagation occurs when the direct path between transmitter and
receiver is blocked by obstacles, and the signal reaches the receiver through
alternative paths like reflections, diffraction, or scattering.
Characteristics:
Signals can travel around or through obstacles.
Common in urban environments or areas with challenging terrain.
Utilizes lower frequencies for clearer information transfer.
Advantages:
More robust in environments with obstacles and obstructions.
Enables communication in areas where LOS is not possible.
Disadvantages:
Signal attenuation, multipath propagation, and interference can occur.
Requires more complex signal processing techniques.

In THz communications, antenna misalignment (where antennas are not
perfectly aligned) and blockage (where objects obstruct the signal path)
are significant challenges due to high path loss and the need for
directional antennas, requiring mitigation strategies.
Antenna Misalignment:
********** 12 **********
--- page 13 ---
Problem:
THz systems rely on high-gain, directional antennas to overcome high
path loss, but perfect alignment is practically impossible. Even small
misalignments can severely degrade signal strength and connectivity.
Environmental factors like wind, earthquakes, and traffic can cause
antennas to move, leading to misalignment.
Impact:
Misalignment reduces the total directional gain, leading to a weaker
signal and increased outage probability. The higher the antenna gain, the
more sensitive the system becomes to mispointing.
Mitigation:
Automatic Beam Steering: Systems can dynamically adjust antenna
pointing to compensate for misalignment.
Closed-Loop Pointing Control: Feedback mechanisms can continuously
monitor and correct antenna pointing.
Beamwidth Design: Ensuring the beamwidth is sufficiently larger than
the expected misalignment can provide some tolerance.
Relaying: Using relays to overcome blockage and misalignment.
Blockage:
Problem: The high penetration loss of THz waves makes them vulnerable
to blockage by objects or people.
Impact: Blockage results in signal attenuation and potential link failure.
Mitigation:
Directed NLOS Transmission: Exploiting multipath signals (reflections)
to overcome blockage by directing antennas towards the strongest
reflections.
Relaying: Using relays to bypass the blockage and establish a new path.
Antenna Placement: Strategically placing antennas to minimize the
likelihood of blockage.
Adaptive Beam Steering: Adjusting the beam direction to avoid
obstacles.

Answer-
Energy Efficiency (EE) is a crucial performance metric for multi-antenna
systems, as it considers both the spectral efficiency (data rate) and the
power consumption, aiming to maximize data throughput while [06]
minimizing energy usage.
Here's a breakdown of how EE relates to the performance of multi-
antenna systems:
1. Increased Spectral Efficiency (SE) and its Impact on EE:
MIMO (Multiple Input Multiple Output) Systems:
Multi-antenna systems, particularly MIMO, are designed to enhance SE
by enabling spatial multiplexing (sending multiple data streams
simultaneously) and spatial diversity (improving reliability).
SE and EE Trade-off:
While increased SE is desirable, it often comes at the cost of increased
power consumption (e.g., more complex signal processing, more
antennas).
Optimizing EE:
********** 13 **********
--- page 14 ---
The goal is to find the optimal balance between SE and power
consumption to maximize EE.
2. Factors Affecting EE in Multi-Antenna Systems:
Number of Antennas:
More antennas can lead to higher SE, but also increase power
consumption (RF circuits, DSP).
Power Allocation:
Efficient power allocation strategies are crucial to maximize EE, ensuring
that power is used effectively to achieve high SE without excessive
consumption.
Antenna Selection:
Choosing the right antennas for transmission (e.g., based on channel
conditions) can improve EE.
Circuit Power (CP) Utilization:
The power consumption of the radio-frequency (RF) circuits and digital
signal processing (DSP) components also significantly impacts EE.
3. Techniques for Improving EE in Multi-Antenna Systems:
Adaptive Power Allocation:
Dynamically adjusting transmit power based on channel conditions and
user demands to optimize EE.
Antenna Selection:
Selecting the optimal set of antennas for transmission based on channel
conditions and user requirements.
Joint Antenna and User Selection:
Optimizing both antenna selection and user selection to maximize EE.
Reinforcement Learning (RL):
Using RL algorithms to optimize energy efficiency in massive MIMO
networks by switching on/off underutilized base stations.
Optimizing Antenna Models:
Developing feasible parameter optimization approaches to design
sophisticated antenna models to reduce energy loss.
