Q1] An Interview with Mr. Uday Kotak

MR Uday S. Kotak, no stranger to the world of finance, but the latest
to wear the tag of "banker", is keen to dispel the impression that his
new bank is just another in a long line of banks.

He takes pains to point out that his organisation has always been
doing everything that a bank does (except perhaps opening savings
bank accounts).

According to him, the "hygiene" factor of a bank label has now been
added to position itself on a financial services platform with focus on
investment advisory.

As he would have it, Kotak Bank is certainly not a babe-in-banking-
woods. In a chat with Business Line, at Chennai, where he had come
recently to open his bank's branch, Mr Kotak threw light on his
strategy and plans for the bank.

We understand you are focusing more on channel/vendor financing -as
an avenue for corporate credit. What is the market size and what is
your share in this? What are the growth prospects in this area?

A major paradigm shift is taking place in the way corporate finance is
structured. Traditionally, banks in India approached corporates and
said "here is our bag of money". I don't think that is sustainable any
longer. You have to go to the supply chain and look for gaps.

%
--- page 2 ---
If you take the automobile sector, for instance, we are end-to-end
providers. We have done car financing at one end of the retail chain.
Now we are going to the other end and financing the ancillary units
and suppliers. We do about Rs 2,000 crore of car financing a year. At
any point of time we have about Rs 300 crore of dealer finance. We do
about Rs 1,000 crore of commercial vehicle financing. We are targeting
another Rs 500 crore at the auto ancillary unit level. The total churn or
the transaction volumes in the auto sector would be in excess of Rs
5,000 crore a year. We are number three in auto financing, behind
ICICI and HDFC Bank. As a vertical, it is a large part of our balance
sheet.

Has fee income in this segment been coming down? Is the model
replicable in other sectors?

If you are in the end-to-end financing, we can compensate the
reductions if any in fee income. But the auto sector is growing at 30
per cent. And yes. We are trying to replicate the auto financing model
in other sectors - maybe in FMCG, Pharmaceuticals, tractors etc.

The profits and spreads for Kotak Primus (car financing subsidiary)
seem to be under pressure. Your comments?

Don't look at the profits of Kotak Primus in the normal way. That is
because there is a very large royalty that Kotak Primus pays the bank
- under our agreement with Ford. If you look at Kotak Primus's
operating profits (before the royalty payments), it is quite good.
Spreads have been under pressure. But even today, I can get a
customer for car finance at 15 per cent and 7 per cent. It depends on
the risk profile that you want to take on.

Is the risk and the lower spreads, a function of geography - since you
are primarily in the cities?

Yes. To some extent. As you go deeper, you get higher returns, but
you also run higher risks. We cover the top 60 to 70 cities now.
Primarily, we'll stay there. But as a bank, we do have the obligation to
go to rural areas and we'll do that.

Do you have any plans to merge Kotak Primus with the bank?
No. It will continue as a specialised passenger car financing subsidiary.

The RBI has permitted us to carry on with it as a subsidiary. The bank
will not be doing auto financing but taking up other areas.

We hear that you are not for reducing your promoters stake in favour
of foreign partners? Does that mean you will take on domestic
partners?

I'll tell you where I am on this. We are waiting for a clarification on the
rule that allows foreign promoters 74 per cent holding while restricting
domestic promoters to 49 per cent. We have been asked to reduce our
holding anytime after one year from the grant of license. But it is open
ended and no specific time frame has been set.

What's the major advantage that you have gained by converting from
an NBFC into a bank?

Well, we see the conversion as conferring a longer-term advantage.
We didn't have access to call money earlier. No doubt there is a
branch cost - but that is met not only by deposits but also by
investment products. We see this as a stronger model.

If interest rates are as flat as they are currently, is there a great
advantage in being a bank?

Good question. I'll answer it in the context of what we have seen
happen in the US. In good times, when wholesale money is available
and interest rates are low, one can continue as an NBFC and grow.
What the bank provides is a stability of liability. When interest rates
tighten at some point of time, wholesale money may vanish.

Will your banking strategy allow you to retain such stable deposits -
You said earlier that savers are now becoming investors. In that event,
wont depositors move to higher yielding products?

If you have Rs 100, you'll put Rs 80 in various products but leave
around Rs 20 in the SB account. We need to capture that and make it
grow bigger. There is a fundamental change happening, globally.

Most banks have been focusing on the stock of money. Now it. is
moving to the flow. Money is becoming more mobile. If you look at the
nationalised banks, it is all focused on the stock.

The trick to get stable money for us, is to be in the middle of the flow.
As we do have more of investment products, and keep on the churn,
some part of the flow will keep on getting left behind and the residue
will gradually grow.
